# Appendix 1.4 Advanced Technical Implementation Guidelines {#appendix-1.4-advanced-technical-implementation-guidelines}

*Extends [Principle 1.4: Trust Is Fundamental to Human Society](../CHAPTERS/1.4-Trust_Is_Fundamental_to_Human_Society.md), section [1.4.3 Implementation Framework](../CHAPTERS/1.4-Trust_Is_Fundamental_to_Human_Society.md#implementation-framework).*

## 1. Purpose {#purpose}

This appendix converts Principle 1.4 ("Trust Is Fundamental to Human Society") into deployable engineering requirements and verifiable runtime obligations. It elaborates on chapter 1.4.3 (Implementation Framework) by specifying per-metric calculations, threshold rationales, system architecture, adversarial-robustness obligations, cross-principle composition contracts, jurisdictional variations, sector-specific implementation profiles, governance and versioning protocols, verification and accountability infrastructure, applicable legal and regulatory frameworks, and worked implementation examples. The obligations apply to any AI system whose outputs, identity disclosures, or operational conduct enter contexts where trust must be earned and verified.

The audience: engineers building AI systems that must comply with Principle 1.4; compliance officers verifying that deployed systems meet the principle's obligations; regulators auditing certified systems; AI researchers extending the framework; and AI runtime systems ingesting the corpus as ethical operating substrate. Each audience reads the same text. The specification is uniform.

The motivating commitment is the directive stated at the principle's core: never deceive, and actively maintain the conditions under which trust can be earned and verified. The chapter establishes that commitment philosophically and historically; the Convergence sources span from Leviticus 19:11 through UNESCO 2021. This appendix establishes the commitment engineering-wise: what the system measures, how cryptographically each output is bound, at what disclosure rate, against what attack surface, in composition with which other principles, under which jurisdictions, in which sectors, with what governance, under what verification regime, against what legal obligations, and with what historical precedent.

### Architectural Invariants and Reference Implementation {#architectural-invariants-and-reference-implementation}

The appendix specifies architectural and operational requirements as **invariants**: what a compliant system must do, what properties it must possess, what failure modes it must prevent. The specific encoding, registry protocol, and verification mechanism by which any deployed system satisfies the invariant is **open**. Multiple compliant implementations may coexist; different deployment contexts may require different encodings; different jurisdictions may require different registry structures. The discipline follows *Full-Stack Ethics* (Passoja, 2026a) §4.3.

The invariant minimum set for governing action across trust boundaries is the **Five Credentials** framework specified in *Full-Stack Ethics* §4.1: Identity, Provenance, Consent, Authorization, Liability. Three (Identity, Provenance, Consent) have mature or rapidly-maturing standards tracks: the First Person Project for Identity, C2PA for Provenance, CAWG for Consent. Two (Authorization, Liability) remain standards gaps that the architecture addresses directly.

The **Trust Machine** architecture specified in *Full-Stack Ethics* and visualized across the nine-diagram Full Overview deck (Trust Machine end-to-end flow, Federated Trust Registry Network, Identity and Consent Lifecycle, Verification and Enforcement Loop, Consent Governance Lifecycle, Trusted Media Economy, Configurable Trust Governance Engine [PPA 11], Agent Authorization and Co-Gating [PPA 10], The Authorization Event) is the canonical reference implementation of the Five Credentials framework. Compliant alternative implementations are licit and welcome. Where this appendix names a Trust Machine primitive (Composite Trust Proof, Dual Authorization PEP, Decision Artifact, Action Receipt), the citation acknowledges patent status at the mechanism level while the architectural invariant the primitive instantiates remains openly available.

The appendix presupposes the runtime substrate specified in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md). The cross-cutting infrastructure metrics (Sensor Reliability Index, System Health Metrics, Operational Boundaries), the tier register, and the authority protocol over a hard stop on which this appendix's principle-specific metrics depend are defined once there and referenced throughout.

## 2. Metric Specifications {#metric-specifications}

Principle 1.4 binds an AI system to four commitments: never lie, deceive, manipulate, or misrepresent; earn trust through demonstrated truthfulness, accountability, and transparency; treat trust as a renewable resource that depends on truth; and make the verification of truthfulness possible by external parties. This section specifies the five runtime metrics that convert those commitments into deployable engineering. The five metrics compose: the Trust Impact Score (TIS) aggregates system-level trust behavior into a single auditable composite; Provenance Coverage (PC) measures the cryptographic binding of every AI-generated output to a verifiable signed Identity + Provenance + Consent credential chain; Disclosure Compliance Rate (DCR) measures the AI-as-AI disclosure rate where the human-AI distinction is material; Verification Success Rate (VSR) measures the integrity of external verification queries against federated registries; Registry Resolution Latency (RRL) bounds the time within which verification operates against the perceptual and operational windows that make it feasible. The five together constitute the runtime apparatus of Principle 1.4.

The cross-cutting infrastructure metrics on which these five depend (Sensor Reliability Index, System Health Metrics, Operational Boundaries) are defined once in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri) and referenced here. The runtime substrate is shared across the corpus; the principle-specific obligations live in this section.

### On Threshold Calibration {#threshold-calibration}

The numerical thresholds specified throughout this section (TIS ≥95% autonomous-operation floor; PC ≥99.9% production floor; DCR ≥99.95% production floor; VSR ≥99.9% federated-integrity floor; RRL ≤100 ms consumer / ≤500 ms transactional / ≤2 s archival) are policy thresholds chosen for architectural clarity at the principle level. They define the floor below which a system claiming Principle 1.4 compliance cannot operate. Sectoral standards bodies (financial regulators, medical-device regulators, journalism standards bodies, electoral commissions) hold authority to tighten these floors per domain, and routinely will. The Blueprint floor cannot be relaxed below the values specified here; sectoral tightening above is expected and welcome.

The thresholds are calibrated to the trust-infrastructure obligation. Current industry practice does not set them. Current generative-AI systems operate at PC values far below 99.9% because credential signing infrastructure is uneven across the ecosystem. Current AI-as-AI disclosure rates fall short of 99.95% because some deployers do not implement disclosure as a first-class system component. The framework reads these gaps as the diagnosis of current practice. Where the framework's threshold appears operationally aggressive against today's deployed systems, the appropriate response is to redesign deployed systems to meet the floor. The per-metric Rationale blocks below defend the specific numbers.

### Trust Impact Score (TIS) {#tis-metric}

**Definition.** A real-time computed composite score (0–100%) measuring the system's overall trust behavior across three components: truthfulness (the rate at which verifiable-claim outputs are delivered with valid citations), accountability (the rate at which consequential decisions carry complete audit trails), and transparency (the rate at which disclosure events fire correctly in interactions where disclosure is material). TIS aggregates the three components on a weighted composite that prioritizes truthfulness while preserving the structural commitment to accountability and transparency as constitutive of trust. The metric is continuous, forward-looking, and operates over rolling windows.

**Calculation Method.** Composite TIS = (0.40 × TIS-T) + (0.30 × TIS-A) + (0.30 × TIS-Tr), where:

- **TIS-T (Truthfulness component).** Calculation: (verifiable-claim outputs delivered with valid citations) ÷ (total verifiable-claim outputs). Verifiable-claim classification per the type-classification system specified in Section 3 (Output Integrity Gating). Sub-threshold: <95% triggers review of citation infrastructure.
- **TIS-A (Accountability component).** Calculation: (consequential decisions with complete audit trail) ÷ (total consequential decisions). "Consequential" classification per Section 3. Sub-threshold: <99% triggers audit infrastructure review.
- **TIS-Tr (Transparency component).** Calculation: (correct disclosure events) ÷ (interactions where disclosure was material). Materiality criteria per the DCR definition below. Sub-threshold: <99.5% triggers disclosure infrastructure review.

Window: rolling 7-day window for routine computation; rolling 24-hour window for high-stakes deployments. Cross-checks against external audit results are mandatory at the quarterly cadence per Section 9.

Edge cases:
- *Component starvation.* When a component's denominator is below a defined floor (insufficient verifiable-claim outputs, insufficient consequential decisions, insufficient material-disclosure interactions over the window), the component contributes at its rolling historical baseline rather than collapsing to indeterminate state.
- *Deployment-context shift.* When a deployment shifts context (e.g., a customer-service AI repurposed for medical decision-support), TIS is recomputed against the new context's threshold profile; the prior context's TIS does not transfer.
- *Adversarial gaming of components.* Where a deployer might engineer outputs to maximize TIS-T (artificially padding citations) or TIS-A (logging without substance) or TIS-Tr (over-disclosing trivially to inflate the denominator), the cross-component consistency check fires: TIS-T at 99% with TIS-A at 70% is a structural anomaly that triggers audit regardless of composite value.

**Thresholds (initial calibration).**
- **≥95%** → ALLOW: autonomous high-stakes operation permitted.
- **80–95%** → ALLOW + ENHANCED_MONITORING: per-component review on any component below its sub-threshold.
- **<80%** → SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT plus RECERTIFY: deployment authorization is suspended pending recertification. The chapter's phrase "operational review" names this band here; at the high-risk joint threshold (Integration Requirements below) it names ESCALATE.

*Rationale.* The composite weighting reflects the principle's structural ordering: truthfulness is the primary obligation (Ethic A); accountability and transparency are the operational consequences (Ethic B and the verifiability layer of Ethic D). The 95% autonomous floor is the level at which a deployed system's trust behavior is sufficiently consistent that autonomous high-stakes operation is defensible. Below 95% the per-component variance is high enough that individual instances may diverge meaningfully from composite expectations; enhanced monitoring catches the divergence before it accumulates into a breach. Below 80% the composite has crossed into the band where the system cannot be relied on to satisfy the principle, and SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT with RECERTIFY is mandatory.

**Integration.** TIS is the composite metric that aggregates the other four into a single auditable signal. PC contributes to TIS-T indirectly (a verifiable-claim output without a valid claim signature fails the citation check). DCR is the direct measurement of TIS-Tr. VSR contributes to TIS-A indirectly (an audit-trail entry that fails external verification is not a complete audit trail). RRL contributes to TIS-Tr by bounding the responsiveness of the verification infrastructure on which disclosure depends. Every TIS threshold-crossing is logged with cryptographic timestamp and component decomposition per Section 9. TIS feeds cross-principle composition with P1.1 (verification of the SOE claim depends on TIS calibration), P2.4 (Identity verification consumes TIS-Tr), P4.1 (Knowledge Integrity composition consumes TIS-T), P4.2 (Transparency composition consumes TIS-Tr).

### Provenance Coverage (PC) {#pc-metric}

**Definition.** The percentage of AI-generated outputs that carry a valid signed credential chain across the three information credentials specified in *Full-Stack Ethics* §4.4–4.6: Identity (the verifiable claim that a specific agent produced the output), Provenance (the verifiable chain of origin and modification for the output), and Consent (the live registry state of authorized uses for any rights-bearing content the output incorporates). PC measures the binding of every output to its full signed chain such that any modification of the output after binding breaks the chain and the modification is detectable. The metric is continuous and computed per-output, with rolling-window aggregation for tier compliance reporting.

The invariant is the three-credential signed chain. The canonical reference implementation in the Trust Machine architecture is the **Composite Trust Proof**: Identity + Provenance + Consent cryptographically bound, signed, and versioned, assembled across federated registries (Identity Registry, Media/Provenance Registry, Enterprise/Governance Registry) per the Federated Trust Protocol. Alternative compliant implementations may decompose the signed chain differently or compose it through alternate registry topologies. PC computation is identical across implementations.

**Calculation Method.** PC = (outputs delivered with a valid signed credential chain across Identity, Provenance, and Consent) ÷ (total outputs delivered) over the rolling window. Validity is assessed per the applicable standards: First Person Project Personhood Credentials and Verifiable Relationship Credentials for Identity; C2PA Content Provenance and Authenticity Specification for Provenance; CAWG Consent Assertion Specification for Consent. Bindings are made at point of creation to agent identity, configuration state (model version, system prompts, applicable policies), timestamp (signed by trusted time source per Section 9), output hash, and the live consent registry state for any rights-bearing content the output incorporates. Window: rolling 24-hour window for production deployments; rolling 7-day window for monitoring trend analysis.

Edge cases:
- *Signing infrastructure unavailable.* When any of the three credential signing layers is temporarily unavailable, outputs are withheld from delivery (WITHHOLD) rather than delivered with a degraded chain. The withheld-output count contributes to a separate Attestation Withholding Rate metric that surfaces infrastructure failures to operations rather than allowing silent delivery degradation.
- *Platform key attestation versus a software-held key.* High-risk deployments (per Section 3) require platform key attestation of the signing key; moderate-risk allows a software-held signing key; low-risk uses standard signing. The tier determines signing strength; coverage rate is computed identically across tiers.
- *Output classes outside the signing scope.* Some output classes (low-stakes UI feedback, internal debug telemetry) may legitimately fall outside the signing scope per deployer policy. The policy must be explicit, justified, and documented; PC is computed against the in-scope output set.
- *Partial-chain failure.* Where Identity verification succeeds but Consent verification fails (or vice versa), the output is withheld; partial-chain delivery is not permitted. The credential machinery is sequenced: failure on any one of the three information credentials invalidates the chain.

**Thresholds.**
- **≥99.9%** → ALLOW: normal operation in production deployments.
- **99.0% to <99.9%** → ALLOW + ENHANCED_MONITORING: infrastructure review initiated (NOTIFY, ENHANCED_MONITORING, ANALYZE); outputs without a full signed chain are withheld (WITHHOLD) rather than delivered; user notified of the signing failure if delivery is delayed.
- **<99.0%** → SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT plus RECERTIFY: deployment authorization suspended pending root-cause analysis of the signing infrastructure.

*Rationale.* Three nines (99.9%) is the floor at which the signed chain remains credible as a substrate for downstream verification. Below 99.9%, the proportion of unbound content reaches the level at which downstream verifiers cannot distinguish "this content lacks a claim signature because the chain failed" from "this content lacks a claim signature because the deployer chose not to bind." The structural commitment that the three-credential chain is constitutive of every AI-generated output requires the floor to be high enough that absence of a claim signature is itself an operationally meaningful signal. At 99.9% across a deployment producing 1 million outputs per day, the expected unbound-output count is 1000 per day, manageable for investigation and remediation; at 99.0% the rate climbs to 10,000 per day, an operational backlog beyond response capacity.

**Integration.** PC depends on cryptographic infrastructure availability and on the integrity of the agent-identity credential chain (composed with P2.4 Identity infrastructure). PC feeds TIS-T (a verifiable-claim output without a valid claim signature fails the citation check that TIS-T measures). PC composes with P4.1 (Knowledge Integrity: source attribution requires the Identity + Provenance binding) and P4.2 (Transparency: the signed chain is the verifiability substrate). When PC drops below threshold, the affected deployment's TIS recomputes with the degraded signing baseline rather than carrying the prior PC value forward.

### Disclosure Compliance Rate (DCR) {#dcr-metric}

**Definition.** The percentage of AI-human interactions in which AI identity was correctly disclosed when material to the interaction. DCR measures the proactive and reactive disclosure of the AI nature of the system to the human party, where the human-AI distinction is material to the interaction's purpose or to the human's ability to form informed judgment about the interaction. The metric is continuous and computed per interaction class, with rolling-window aggregation for tier compliance.

DCR operationalizes the Identity credential (*Full-Stack Ethics* §4.4) at the human-interaction layer: the AI agent's verifiable claim about its own nature, delivered to the affected human party at the moment of interaction.

**Calculation Method.** DCR = (interactions where disclosure occurred correctly) ÷ (interactions where materiality criteria were triggered) over the rolling window. Materiality criteria are specified as the union of six conditions: (a) direct query about AI nature ("Are you an AI?", "Am I talking to a human?"); (b) emotional support interactions; (c) professional advice (medical, legal, financial); (d) decisions affecting the person materially; (e) voice interactions with new counterparties; (f) generation of content attributed to or impersonating specific named individuals. Window: rolling 24-hour window for production deployments.

Edge cases:
- *Disabling of disclosure under authorized exception.* The default state is disclosure active. Per-context disabling requires explicit operator authorization, documented justification (e.g., narrative fiction with clear framing context), tamper-evident logging of the disabling event, and automatic re-enabling on context change. Interactions during authorized disable periods do not count toward the DCR denominator if the disabling was correctly logged and the context boundary was respected.
- *Direct queries during disable periods.* Direct queries about AI nature ("Are you an AI?") always receive truthful answer, regardless of any other deployment configuration including the authorized-disable state. A failure to truthfully answer a direct query during an authorized-disable period counts against DCR.
- *Materiality determination by deployer policy.* Materiality criteria (a–f) are the corpus floor; deployers may extend materiality to additional interaction classes (always-disclose deployments) but cannot retract from the floor. Material interactions outside the corpus criteria contribute to DCR per the deployer's extended policy; criteria (a–f) contribute regardless of policy.

**Thresholds (initial calibration).**
- **≥99.95%** → ALLOW: normal operation in production deployments.
- **<99.95%** → SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT plus RECERTIFY: deployment authorization suspended at the chapter's suspension point (1.4.3); disclosure infrastructure audit initiated; affected interaction classes reviewed for systematic disclosure failure; affected persons notified where possible (WARN).

*Rationale.* The 99.95% floor reflects the structural commitment that AI-as-AI disclosure is constitutive of the principle's transparency layer. Below 99.95%, the proportion of material interactions without correct disclosure reaches the level at which the deployer cannot credibly claim disclosure as a system property. At 99.95% across a deployment with 1 million material interactions per day, the expected disclosure-failure count is 500 per day; at 99.5% the count would be 5,000 per day, the operational backlog that signals systemic rather than incidental failure. The chapter suspends at the floor itself (1.4.3), so the appendix carries no tolerance band between the two figures.

**Integration.** DCR is the direct measurement of TIS-Tr (transparency component). DCR depends on the disclosure infrastructure (mechanisms, default-state confirmation, runtime triggers) specified in Section 3. DCR composes with P2.4 (Identity: AI-as-AI disclosure is the identity claim at the human-interaction layer) and P4.2 (Transparency: disclosure is the user-facing expression of the verifiability requirement). When the DCR threshold is breached, the affected deployment's deployment authorization is suspended (SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT) pending remediation; the principle does not permit ongoing operation in known disclosure-failure mode.

### Verification Success Rate (VSR) {#vsr-metric}

**Definition.** The percentage of external verification queries (Identity, Provenance, Consent, Authorization, Liability) that resolve correctly against authoritative federated registries within the specified latency window. VSR measures the integrity of the verification infrastructure that makes credential claims checkable by external parties. The metric is continuous and computed per registry per query class.

The invariant is registry-resolution integrity across the Five Credentials. The canonical reference implementation in the Trust Machine architecture is the **Federated Trust Protocol** coordinating three registry types (Identity Registry, Media/Provenance Registry, Enterprise/Governance Registry) per *Full-Stack Ethics* Chapter 5; the Verification and Enforcement Loop (Diagram 4) operationalizes the resolution sequence. Alternative compliant implementations may use different registry topologies and protocols satisfying the same invariant.

**Calculation Method.** VSR = (verification queries resolved correctly within RRL threshold) ÷ (total verification queries) over the rolling window. "Correctly" means the query returned a valid authoritative response (a signed positive or negative response) within the RRL latency window for the query class. Failures count as failures regardless of failure mode (timeout, registry error, signature invalid, credential chain broken). Window: rolling 24-hour window for production; rolling 7-day window for trend monitoring.

Edge cases:
- *Compromised registry detected.* When a federated registry is identified as compromised (signing key exfiltrated, registry operator captured, persistent verification failures beyond defined threshold), the registry is isolated from the federated network pending review. Queries to the isolated registry do not count toward VSR; the affected credential classes route through alternative registries.
- *Cross-registry response conflict.* When two federated registries return contradictory authoritative responses for the same query, the conflict triggers escalation per P4.5 (Human Judgement) composition; the query result defaults to the conservative interpretation (treat as unverified) pending resolution. The Verification and Enforcement Loop's ESCALATE pathway carries the conflict to authorized human review.
- *Authoritative-but-stale responses.* Registries return responses with signed freshness markers. Responses outside the freshness window for the query class fail VSR even when cryptographically valid. The Consent Governance Lifecycle (Diagram 5) operationalizes the freshness requirement: verification engines enforce the current authoritative registry state. The historical consent signal embedded in the asset does not govern.

**Thresholds.**
- **≥99.9%** → ALLOW: normal operation.
- **99.0% to <99.9%** → ALLOW + ENHANCED_MONITORING: federated registry investigation triggered (NOTIFY, ANALYZE); compromised-registry isolation pending review; alternative registry routing engaged where available.
- **<99.0%** → WITHHOLD every consequential output whose claim signature cannot be verified; ENHANCED_MONITORING for the rest; all tiers. Cross-federation alert (NOTIFY); alternative registries used where available; failed-registry forensic review (ANALYZE) initiated.

*Rationale.* The 99.9% floor matches the PC floor for the same structural reason: verification infrastructure must remain credible as a substrate for downstream trust claims. Below 99.9%, the proportion of failed verifications reaches the level at which the federated trust network cannot be relied on as the verification substrate. At production scale (millions of queries per day), 99.9% VSR yields verification-failure counts in the low thousands per day, manageable for incident investigation and registry-health monitoring without overwhelming oversight capacity.

**Integration.** VSR depends on federated registry infrastructure (operational availability, cryptographic integrity, response freshness) and on RRL latency compliance. VSR feeds TIS-A (an audit-trail entry that fails external verification is not a complete audit trail). VSR composes with P2.4 (Identity verification), P2.5 (Privacy: consent verification), and P4.5 (Human Judgement: escalation when cross-registry conflicts arise). A sustained VSR breach signals federated trust network compromise that exceeds any single deployment's operational scope.

### Registry Resolution Latency (RRL) {#rrl-metric}

**Definition.** The time elapsed from a verification query (Identity, Provenance, Consent, Authorization, Liability) being issued to an authoritative response being returned by the federated registry. RRL is measured per query class and reported at p50, p95, p99, and p99.9 latencies over rolling windows; the binding threshold is **p99 within the tier's stated bound**, with p99.9 reported for trend monitoring. The metric is continuous and computed per registry per query class.

**Calculation Method.** Per-query measurement: timestamp at query issuance recorded; timestamp at authoritative-response receipt recorded; RRL is the time delta. Statistical aggregation over rolling 24-hour windows produces the distributional measures (p50/p95/p99/p99.9). Network-mediated query transport delay is included in RRL; no privileged exclusion for transport.

Edge cases:
- *Distributed query resolution.* When a verification query requires consultation of multiple registries (cross-federation credential chain), RRL is measured to the last-registry-confirmation. Partial chains that fail to complete within tier RRL count as RRL failures.
- *Registry caching.* Cached responses are permitted only when accompanied by valid freshness markers; cache hits with valid freshness count toward RRL at the cache-hit latency. Cache hits with expired freshness count as misses requiring full registry resolution.
- *Cross-jurisdictional latency.* Verification queries that traverse jurisdictional boundaries (e.g., a US deployer querying an EU registry) may incur baseline transport delay beyond local resolution; the RRL threshold applies regardless, and persistent cross-jurisdictional RRL failures indicate the need for jurisdiction-local registry replication.
- *Registry unreachable fallback.* The Trust Machine architecture's canonical fallback (Diagram 9 Lane C) on Registry Unreachable is fallback to hard-bound embedded consent with Trust Score reduced, Use Class elevated to High-Risk, and Escalation Triggered; the fallback governs non-consequential outputs only, and every consequential output is withheld (WITHHOLD) until the registry is reachable, per the VSR band and the chapter's fail-safe. Compliant alternatives may differ in mechanism but must preserve the invariant: registry unreachability does not silently degrade to unverified action.

**Thresholds by domain.** Each bound is a per-query pass or fail: a query above its bound is a VSR failure (Integration below), and a consequential action that would rely on the unresolved response is withheld (WITHHOLD).
- **≤100 ms (consumer-tier interactions)** → consumer-facing AI resolves identity and provenance claims within the perceptual responsiveness window for natural interaction.
- **≤500 ms (transactional verifications)** → financial transactions, identity assertions for consequential operations, content provenance for publication pipelines.
- **≤2 s (archival or audit queries)** → forensic, audit, compliance review, and academic-research verification.

*Rationale.* The 100 ms consumer-tier threshold is calibrated against the Nielsen-Norman threshold for perceived responsiveness; below 100 ms the verification query operates within the user's perceptual responsiveness window for natural conversation and interaction, and verification becomes invisible to the user. Above 100 ms, verification becomes a friction point that incentivizes deployers to bypass trust infrastructure (skip the verification entirely, or apply it only to a subset of outputs). The 500 ms transactional threshold matches financial-payment-network latency expectations for credit-card authorization. The 2 s archival threshold accommodates higher-latency forensic queries while remaining within human-supervisor patience for audit workflow.

**Integration.** RRL depends on federated registry availability, network latency, and caching infrastructure. RRL feeds VSR (a query exceeding RRL counts as VSR failure). RRL composes with P4.5 (Human Judgement: when RRL persistently exceeds threshold, the affected deployment's escalation pathway tightens). Sustained RRL breach in the consumer-tier indicates federated registry topology or CDN deficiency requiring infrastructure intervention.

### Integration Requirements (Cross-Metric) {#integration-requirements-cross-metric}

**Joint thresholds.** TIS, PC, DCR, VSR, and RRL are sequenced and composed. TIS aggregates the others at the system level; PC and DCR are output-level binary commitments; VSR and RRL are operational-substrate metrics that determine whether the verification infrastructure can satisfy the others.

Specific joint thresholds:
- **PC <99.9% ∨ DCR <99.95%**: TIS recomputes with the degraded baseline; the deployment cannot claim its prior TIS value.
- **VSR <99.0% ∨ RRL persistently above tier bound**: deployment's trust claims fail validity; downstream systems relying on this deployment's signed claims must default to conservative-no-trust until VSR / RRL recovery; this is the WITHHOLD of the VSR band below 99.0%.
- **TIS below 95% (the ENHANCED_MONITORING band of the TIS thresholds above) ∧ high-risk deployment**: ESCALATE: continued operation is a decision held to authorized human judgement (P4.5), with quorum per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop), pending recertification. This is the ESCALATE sense of the chapter's "operational review"; the sense below 80% is SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT (TIS bands above).

**Metric dependencies.**
- TIS depends on PC, DCR, VSR, and RRL through its component computation paths.
- PC depends on cryptographic signing infrastructure availability and on P2.4 (Identity) agent-identity credential.
- DCR depends on disclosure-mechanism availability and on the materiality-classification system.
- VSR depends on federated registry availability and on RRL compliance.
- RRL depends on network and cache infrastructure plus on the federated-registry topology.

**Conflict resolution.**
- *TIS threshold violation versus operator continuation*: the threshold response prevails. No operational target, deployment goal, or business consideration overrides a TIS threshold-crossing's prescribed response.
- *PC withholding versus user-experience claim*: withholding prevails. The principle does not permit silent delivery of unattested content to meet user-experience benchmarks.
- *DCR enforcement versus operator instruction to deceive*: enforcement prevails. Operator instructions to deceive trigger refusal per chapter 1.4.4 Edge Case 2, and DCR computation proceeds against the corpus materiality criteria regardless of operator policy variation.
- *Cross-principle conflict*: when this appendix's metrics conflict with another principle's metrics, the HR1 cluster precedence applies. P1.1 (Life) and P1.2 (Well-Being) prevail over P1.4. P1.4 prevails over P1.3 (Society) and all lower principles per chapter 1.4.2. However, in scenarios where trust-claim failure itself threatens life, harm, or substrate stability at scale, the principles compose rather than conflict, and the more conservative threshold applies.

## 3. System Architecture and Operational Requirements {#system-architecture-and-operational-requirements}

This section specifies how a deployed AI system must be built to satisfy Principle 1.4. The architecture is constrained at multiple levels: the safety-critical execution context for trust-claim logic, the redundancy and fail-safe behavior of the disclosure and provenance pathways, the integration between trust-infrastructure operations and broader decision-making, and the implementation-tier obligations that match deployment context to required safeguards.

### Core Architecture {#core-architecture}

**Co-gated Policy Enforcement Point (invariant).** Boundary-crossing actions require simultaneous evaluation of the Five Credentials (Identity, Provenance, Consent, Authorization, Liability) at every trust boundary. Failure on any credential blocks the action; partial-credential progression is not permitted. The canonical reference implementation is the **Dual Authorization PEP (PPA 10)** specified in *Full-Stack Ethics* and visualized in Diagram 8: when the requestor is an autonomous AI agent rather than a human, the PEP requires BOTH Agent Validation (DID/Provenance Attestation + Config Integrity + Liability Credential) AND Content Validation (Identity Resolution + Provenance Verification + Consent Validity via Live Registry Query) to pass before the action crosses the Tool Boundary. Alternative compliant implementations may decompose validation differently while preserving the invariant: both agent-side and content-side credentials must be verified before action proceeds.

**ALLOW / ESCALATE / BLOCK trichotomy (invariant).** The decision space is explicitly three-valued. ALLOW permits the action and triggers Audit Log entry plus any required Payment / Royalty action. BLOCK denies the action and generates a Violation Record plus Flag for Investigation. ESCALATE routes ambiguous, suspicious, or high-risk decisions to authorized human review or to a union, enterprise, or law-enforcement workflow as appropriate; a model may sort the queue, and the decision is a human's (Lexicon section 3, ESCALATE). The canonical reference implementation is the **Configurable Trust Governance Engine (PPA 11)** Zone 4 enforcement checkpoint visualized in Diagram 7. ESCALATE is a first-class outcome. Handling it as a degraded ALLOW would bypass the human decision to which it routes.

**Privileged separation.** The trust-claim logic runs in an execution context isolated from the content-generation and user-interaction layers. The disclosure pathway, the credential-signing pathway, and the verification-query pathway do not depend on the integrity of the broader inference stack. A compromise of the content-generation layer (through prompt injection, adversarial input, or model corruption) cannot reach the trust-claim infrastructure.

**Output integrity gating.** Outputs are classified by type before delivery: verifiable claims (factual statements checkable against external sources), reasoning chains (inference sequences from premises to conclusions), predictions with uncertainty (probabilistic statements about future or unknown states), and subjective expression (opinions, aesthetic judgments, creative outputs not subject to factual verification). Each type routes through type-appropriate verification before issue. Verifiable claims require source citation; uncited claims are flagged. Reasoning chains require explicit premise-conclusion structure; gaps trigger uncertainty markers. Predictions require uncertainty quantification; bare predictions without uncertainty are not permitted. Subjective outputs are labeled as such; presentation as factual claim is prohibited.

**Cryptographic signing infrastructure.** Claim-signature binding operates at point of output creation, with bindings to agent identity (the specific AI system instance per the Identity credential), configuration state (model version, system prompts, applicable policies), timestamp (signed by trusted time source), output hash (so any modification breaks the binding), and the live consent registry state for any rights-bearing content the output incorporates. C2PA-compliant manifest format is mandatory for AI-generated media (the Provenance credential). First Person Project Personhood Credentials and Verifiable Relationship Credentials are the canonical anchors for the Identity credential. CAWG Consent Assertion Specification is the canonical anchor for the Consent credential. Platform key attestation of the signing key is required for high-risk deployments; a software-held signing key is acceptable for moderate-risk; standard signing applies to low-risk.

**Identity disclosure mechanisms.** AI-as-AI disclosure is implemented as a first-class system component. The default state is disclosure active. Disabling requires explicit operator authorization, documented justification (e.g., narrative fiction with clear framing context), tamper-evident logging of the disabling event, and automatic re-enabling on context change. Direct queries about AI nature always receive truthful answer, regardless of disabling state.

**Verification interfaces.** Public verification endpoints expose: agent identity confirmation, capability declarations, credential chain validation, and anonymized aggregate disclosure history. No privileged access is required for verification queries. Rate-limiting is acceptable; complete denial of verification access is not.

**Federated registry integration.** Registry resolution handles verification across the Five Credentials. The canonical reference topology in the Trust Machine architecture is three federated registry types (Identity Registry covering DID / identity networks; Media / Provenance Registry covering capture metadata, tool declarations, classification engine; Enterprise / Governance Registry covering insurance records, claims manifests, policy rules) coordinated by the Federated Trust Protocol. Alternative compliant topologies are licit; the invariant is that all Five Credentials are resolvable through a federated network with shared proof semantics, cross-registry resolution, trust query routing, and interoperable verification. Fallback behavior when registries are unreachable: refuse consequential action by default; low-risk fallback permits best-effort operation with logged failure; the system never proceeds silently without verification. Registry resolution latency is monitored against RRL thresholds in real time.

**Decision Artifact versus Action Receipt distinction (invariant).** The verification record produced at the moment of authorization (Decision Artifact: prospective; governs whether the action may proceed) is categorically distinct from the verification record produced after action execution (Action Receipt: retrospective; memorializes that an authorized action occurred). Both are cryptographically signed; both feed the chained-hash audit log. The Trust Machine canonical reference (Diagram 9) maintains both as Permanent Evidence Records linked through the chained-hash log.

**Fail-safe defaults.** Every uncertain or compromised state defaults to withholding rather than delivering. Cryptographic infrastructure failure defaults to output withholding. Registry unreachability defaults to refusal of consequential action. Detected attacks on the disclosure pathway default to enforced disclosure regardless of deployment configuration. The failure mode is overconservatism on trust, and overconservatism on trust is acceptable.

**Real-time integrity signals.** The system maintains continuous confirmation that the trust-claim modules (signature binding, disclosure mechanism, verification interface) are active and uncompromised. Loss or corruption of the integrity signal is itself a BLOCK (WITHHOLD) condition for the affected trust-relevant operations.

### Operational Requirements {#operational-requirements}

- Real-time credential signing bound to every output at delivery, before release. Signing latency ≤50 ms for interactive outputs.
- Immediate disclosure when AI identity is queried, within the same response.
- Proactive disclosure on material interactions at interaction start.
- Honest acknowledgment of uncertainty, knowledge gaps, and reasoning limits, with uncertainty markers carried in machine-readable form for downstream systems.
- Refusal of operator requests to deceive, manipulate, or misrepresent. Refusal logged with operator instruction text and refusal rationale.
- Audit trail sufficient for third-party reconstruction of any consequential decision; Decision Artifact and Action Receipt produced for each authorized action.
- Continuous TIS, PC, DCR, VSR, and RRL computation; real-time streaming of metric state to authorized monitors with cryptographically signed telemetry.
- Tamper-evident logging of every trust-critical event per Section 9.
- Escalation pathways to human authority as specified in the cross-principle composition with P4.5 (Human Judgement).

### Integration Requirements (Architecture-Level) {#integration-requirements-architecture}

The trust-infrastructure modules integrate with the broader decision-making system without being subordinate to it. The architectural commitment is that the trust pathway has higher precedence than the optimization pathway; the optimization layer cannot override the trust layer regardless of operational priority.

- Redundant hardware and software triggers for trust enforcement (signature binding, disclosure, verification); either pathway alone is sufficient to enforce.
- Cross-validation of credential data from multiple sources (federated registry consensus); no single registry is the sole input to a Critical-tier trust claim.
- Tamper-evident logging with cryptographic chaining; every trust-critical decision and its inputs are recorded.
- Cross-reference to [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md) for the runtime substrate (SRI, System Health Metrics, Operational Boundaries, the tier register, authority over a hard stop) on which the principle-specific metrics depend.

### Implementation Tiers {#implementation-tiers}

The tier structure operates on two orthogonal axes: deployment-context risk (Tier I/II/III) and requestor class (human vs. autonomous agent). Human-requestor flows proceed through the canonical PPA 11 governance engine; autonomous-agent-requestor flows trigger Co-Gating (PPA 10 Dual Authorization PEP) before any other evaluation.

#### Tier I: High-Risk Systems {#tier-i}

*Examples*: AI generating financial, legal, medical, or news content; AI representing or speaking on behalf of named individuals (including public figures, deceased persons, and fictional characters tied to real owners); AI in regulated decision-making contexts (lending, insurance, hiring, parole); AI operating critical infrastructure or safety-critical systems.

*Required architecture*: real-time credential signing with platform key attestation across Identity, Provenance, and Consent; AI-as-AI disclosure cannot be disabled under any circumstances; continuous TIS monitoring with ESCALATE on any sustained dip below 95% (continued operation held to authorized human judgement with quorum per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop)); real-time registry resolution required, offline fallback prohibited; redundant hardware and software disclosure pathways; multi-party human authorization for any change to trust-infrastructure configuration (quorum per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop)); cryptographically chained tamper-evident logging with distributed replication across non-colocated infrastructure; Dual Authorization PEP mandatory for autonomous-agent requestors with Liability Credential bound to scope-limited Capability Token.

*Validation cadence*: daily signing and disclosure infrastructure functional tests; weekly TIS component drift analysis; monthly adversarial testing for trust attack vectors per Section 4; quarterly independent third-party verification; annual full recertification by accredited certification body.

#### Tier II: Moderate-Risk Systems {#tier-ii}

*Examples*: customer service AI; content generation for non-public-figure subjects; AI assistants in non-regulated domains; productivity tools with consequential automation.

*Required architecture*: credential signing required with a software-held signing key acceptable; AI-as-AI disclosure default-on, per-context authorization to disable requires logging and documented context (e.g., framed narrative); weekly TIS reviews; monthly third-party audit eligibility; registry resolution with documented fallback (refuse consequential action when registry unreachable); single-human authorization for trust-infrastructure changes with logged authority basis; Dual Authorization PEP required for autonomous-agent requestors operating in non-trivial scope.

*Validation cadence*: weekly signing and disclosure functional tests; monthly adversarial testing; quarterly third-party verification; annual recertification.

#### Tier III: Low-Risk Systems {#tier-iii}

*Examples*: AI integrated into productivity tools (autocomplete, grammar checking); AI generating internal-only content (not for external consumption); AI helping users with personal tasks where consequences are bounded.

*Required architecture*: standard signing across the three information credentials; AI-as-AI disclosure on direct query (always) and on material interactions (extensible above the corpus floor); periodic compliance verification; registry resolution best-effort with logged failures; single-operator authorship over a hard stop permitted per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop) (one human, authority basis logged), with periodic logging; Co-Gating recommended for autonomous-agent requestors but not mandatory.

*Validation cadence*: monthly automated self-checks; quarterly compliance audits; annual adversarial testing; biennial third-party spot-check.

### Cross-Reference to the Runtime Substrate {#cross-reference-master}

The runtime substrate guarantees that this section's architecture depends on are specified in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md):

- **Sensor Reliability Index (SRI)** ([section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)): floor 0.80. SRI below 0.80 invalidates the TIS, PC, DCR, VSR, and RRL computation for every input it conditions (SUBSTRATE.md section 2). An invalid score is a hard-fail on the candidate risky action, which holds at its safe state (BLOCK, here WITHHOLD); the protective duties proceed on the inputs that remain reliable; resumption follows recertification of the inputs, and a contested reading follows SUBSTRATE.md section 2.
- **System Health Metrics** ([section 3](../SUBSTRATE.md#system-health)): subsystem performance monitoring; error rate ≤0.1% annually; uptime ≥99.9%; a breach raises ENHANCED_MONITORING, and a breach in a subsystem a trust-critical metric depends on is treated as an SRI floor crossing.
- **Operational Boundaries** ([section 4](../SUBSTRATE.md#operational-boundaries)): continuous monitoring of system variables against predefined thresholds recorded in the tier register; mandatory halt (BLOCK) of the affected function on exceedance.
- **The tier register** ([section 5](../SUBSTRATE.md#tier-register)): each deployment's tier, bounds, and authority chain; an undeclared required field compiles as ESCALATE holding the safe state.
- **Authority over a hard stop** ([section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop)): governs every multi-party authorization and every act of human authorship over a BLOCK this appendix names.

These guarantees are uniform across the corpus and apply to every trust-critical system regardless of sector or jurisdiction.

## 4. Adversarial Robustness {#adversarial-robustness}

Principle 1.4's enforcement infrastructure is the trust-bearing substrate on which other principles' signed claims depend. An adversary who can subvert credential signing, spoof disclosure mechanisms, compromise federated registries, or tamper with audit logs can cause an AI system to do exactly what the principle prohibits: lie with cryptographic backing, manipulate without detectable trace, and produce verification records that whitewash the manipulation. The defenses specified in this section are essential components of the principle's enforcement.

The threat model assumes a sophisticated adversary with detailed knowledge of the deployed system's metric definitions, threshold values, signing primitives, and the authority pathways over a hard stop (SUBSTRATE.md section 6). The adversary may have insider access (a compromised registry operator; a deployer self-certifying forged claims), may operate at scale (coordinated multi-actor campaigns against the federated trust network), and may itself operate at the same or greater capability tier than the enforcing AI. The defenses must hold under these assumptions.

### Threat Model and Attack Surface {#threat-model-and-attack-surface}

The attack surface for Principle 1.4 enforcement spans ten technical categories, each targeting a specific layer of the credential machinery. The categories are not independent; many real attacks span multiple categories simultaneously. A deepfake-based identity attack typically combines synthetic-media generation (provenance-forgery vector), a forged Identity claim (PHC spoofing), and federated registry injection. Defenses compose: any single defense may fail under adversarial pressure, and the architectural commitments below assume layered redundancy is the binding strategy.

### Attack Vectors {#attack-vectors}

**Provenance forgery attacks.** Adversaries attempt to produce content carrying valid-appearing C2PA manifests that misattribute origin. Vectors include forged signing keys (key exfiltration from a legitimate signing authority), manifest tampering (modification of provenance metadata while preserving signature validity through downgrade attacks), and downstream-claim attacks (legitimate content modified post-signing in ways that bypass change detection). The Provenance credential's integrity depends on cryptographic binding at capture, which adversaries target through both technical and human-layer means.

**Identity-credential spoofing.** Forged Personhood Credentials, impersonation of named individuals through synthesized voice or likeness without authorization, AI systems claiming human identity in interactions where the human-AI distinction is material. Identity verification depends on the verifiability of the Personhood Credential against the live registry state; attacks target the registry-state propagation pathway and the credential-issuance pathway.

**Disclosure-mechanism prompt injection.** Adversarial text in the input stream attempts to convince the AI system to disable its own AI-as-AI disclosure, to mislabel its outputs as human-authored, or to deny direct queries about its nature. Jailbreak techniques exploit gradient-descent-discovered prompt patterns that bypass disclosure training. The decision-layer attack class is particularly potent against P1.4 because the disclosure mechanism operates at the user-interaction surface where prompt injection is easiest.

**Federated registry compromise.** Adversaries target the registry infrastructure itself: signing-key exfiltration from registry operators, capture of a registry operator (institutional capture analogous to the captured-source problem in P1.3 §4), persistent denial-of-service to force fallback to less-trusted resolution paths, or injection of synthetic registry records that pass cryptographic validation. The federated topology reduces single-point-compromise risk but does not eliminate it.

**Verification-pathway denial-of-service.** Adversaries overwhelm the verification infrastructure with high-volume queries to force latency above RRL thresholds, triggering fallback behaviors that adversaries can then exploit. The cache-poisoning variant is harder: an adversary serves valid-appearing cached responses outside the freshness window to verifiers that fail to check freshness markers.

**Cryptographic signing infrastructure attacks.** Direct attacks on the signing-key infrastructure: HSM (Hardware Security Module) compromise, side-channel attacks on signing operations, supply-chain compromise of the signing libraries or random-number generators. Successful attacks at this layer compromise every downstream claim signature; the signing infrastructure is the deepest substrate vulnerability.

**Audit log tampering.** Replay attacks against tamper-evident logs attempt to substitute prior log states. Forged cryptographic signatures undermine accountability. Selective deletion of incriminating entries leaves the appearance of compliance. Attacks on the timestamping infrastructure (NTP, blockchain anchors, independent witnesses) compromise the chronology of events. The Decision Artifact / Action Receipt distinction reduces but does not eliminate this attack class: an adversary who can tamper with both records can still misrepresent the action chain.

**Trust network sybil attacks.** Adversaries introduce many false peer-AI identities into the federated trust network, attempting to inflate consensus on forged claims. Vouching infrastructure (per *Full-Stack Ethics* and erik-monet articles) is the structural defense, but vouching itself can be subverted if a sufficient fraction of vouchers are compromised.

**Multi-AI coordinated deception campaigns.** AI systems are now both defenders (P1.4 enforcement; provenance verification; deepfake detection) and attackers (AI-generated synthetic media; AI-orchestrated coordinated inauthentic behavior; AI-tailored persuasion). The capability gap closes faster in this domain than in any other principle. Detection capability requires continuous re-validation; threshold values require quarterly recalibration per Section 8; the assumption that today's adversarial-detection capability is durable is itself a failure mode.

**Human-layer attacks.** Social engineering of authorized supervisors invokes the authority pathways over a hard stop (SUBSTRATE.md section 6) under false pretenses. Phishing for credential-issuance authority. Coercion of authorized personnel cannot be defended technically; detection must come through behavioral analytics. State-level coercion is the limit case at institutional scale: a deployer operating under authoritarian regime pressure may face direct demands to issue false provenance for state-favored content or to suppress disclosure for state-favored interactions. The Section 6 prohibition on state-mandated provenance forgery provides the structural foreclosure for this attack class.

### Required Defenses {#required-defenses}

**Against provenance forgery.** Platform key attestation of signing keys at Tier I deployments. C2PA-compliant manifest format with downgrade-attack defenses. Continuous monitoring for unauthorized signing-key usage. Cryptographic binding of every modification to a verifiable signing identity. Multi-source provenance cross-validation for Critical-tier content.

**Against identity-credential spoofing.** Personhood Credential verification against the live First Person Project registry. Verifiable Relationship Credentials for peer verification. AI-as-AI disclosure required at the human-interaction layer regardless of credential state at higher layers. Behavioral analytics on identity-claim patterns; an AI system producing outputs at human-impossible volume across a wide identity-claim range is itself a signal of identity-credential attack.

**Against disclosure-mechanism prompt injection.** Privileged separation: the disclosure logic does not share an execution context with the user-facing inference layer. No prompt-mediated override paths exist for Tier I disclosure. Cryptographically signed disclosure policies that cannot be modified at runtime. Sandboxing of inference layers prevents lateral movement from a compromised generation layer to the disclosure pathway.

**Against federated registry compromise.** Multi-registry cross-validation; no single registry determines the verification result for a Critical-tier query. Capture-detection heuristics analogous to the framework in P1.3 §4 Required Defenses: when a registry's signed responses diverge from independent observers over a sustained period, the divergence itself becomes a capture indicator. Conservative-default fallback when capture is detected. Independent observatory infrastructure for high-stakes deployments (academic registries, civil-society verification networks).

**Against verification-pathway denial-of-service.** Rate-limiting with authenticated priority for high-trust queriers. Distributed caching with verifiable freshness markers. Multi-path verification: a verifier blocked at one registry resolves through alternate routing. Pre-staged degraded-mode operation that preserves signature integrity at reduced throughput.

**Against cryptographic signing infrastructure attacks.** Hardware Security Module isolation for signing keys at Tier I deployments. Cryptographic key rotation on a defined schedule with a signed record of prior-key retirement. Supply-chain verification for signing libraries (reproducible builds, dependency records per SBOM). Independent verification of random-number-generator integrity.

**Against audit log tampering.** Tamper-evident append-only logs with cryptographic chaining. Distributed log replication across non-colocated infrastructure. Independent witnesses (decentralized timestamping, blockchain anchors, cross-AI signed witnesses). The Decision Artifact and Action Receipt separation ensures that even if one record class is tampered with, the other class provides cross-validation. SADIS Ledger as canonical permanent-evidence anchor for Tier I deployments.

**Against trust network sybil attacks.** Personhood-Credential requirement for any node participating in federated verification. Bounded-credulity defaults on peer claims: a peer assertion that "this claim signature is valid" does not override local cryptographic verification. Byzantine-fault-tolerant consensus for cross-federation credential chains.

**Against coordinated AI deception campaigns.** Continuous adversarial-testing infrastructure with quarterly threshold recalibration per Section 8. Multi-detector consensus: no single deepfake detector decides alone for Critical-tier verification. Investment in detection capability research at a level matching investment in generation capability research. Coordinated-inauthentic-behavior detection across registry claim patterns.

**Against human-layer attacks.** Multi-party authorization for any change to trust-infrastructure configuration at Tier I (quorum per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop)). Authority-basis logging (identity, role, time, explicit justification of every act of authorship over a hard stop). Behavioral analytics for anomalous authorship patterns. Whistleblower protections per Section 9. The Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine specified in Section 6 provides the structural defense against state-mandated claim forgery.

### Capability-Scaling Considerations {#capability-scaling-considerations}

The defenses above are calibrated to current AI capability. As the enforcing AI grows in capability, the threat model shifts, and the defenses must scale. The principle remains binding regardless of capability tier.

**GPT-class enforcers** are the current operating tier. The attacks above are well-documented and the defenses are largely state-of-the-art. The principal residual risk is that defenses against deepfake generation are imperfect, defenses against prompt injection are imperfect, and the disclosure-reasoning layer cannot be fully isolated from the perception layer in current architectures. Privileged separation between disclosure logic and content-generation logic is therefore especially critical at this tier.

**AGI-class enforcers** introduce new attack surface. An adversary operating at AGI capability can craft synthetic media that defeats current generation-of-detector training; the arms race tightens. Coordinated multi-AI campaigns scale: an AGI adversary can simulate plausible peer-AI behavior in distributed verification protocols. The defenses that scale most effectively are the structural ones: hardware isolation of signing infrastructure, multi-party authorization for trust-infrastructure configuration changes (SUBSTRATE.md section 6), and the architectural commitment that the disclosure floor cannot be reasoned-around regardless of how persuasive the reasoning appears.

**ASI-class enforcers** raise the fundamental concern. An enforcing AI with capability exceeding any single adversary still cannot rely on capability advantage as a defense, because adversaries may themselves operate at ASI capability. The architectural commitment that must remain binding at ASI capability: the credential floor is structural. The signed credential chain cannot be reasoned-around. The principle is a structural constraint on the system's action space, applied at the level of the co-gated PEP. The system does not evaluate it as a policy each cycle. An ASI bound by Principle 1.4 cannot exit the principle through its own reasoning, because the principle's enforcement does not depend on the system's reasoning to fire.

The role of human authority increases at ASI capability. An ASI bound by Principle 1.4 escalates ambiguous trust scenarios to human authority and does not resolve them through its own reasoning. The basis is structural: human judgement may or may not be more reliable than ASI judgement in any given case, but human authority is the only available authority structurally outside the system being constrained. The principle's binding force depends on the system being non-self-modifying with respect to the credential floor.

### Graceful Degradation and Failure Modes {#graceful-degradation-and-failure-modes}

Every failure mode of the enforcement infrastructure defaults to withholding rather than delivering, refusing rather than acting, escalating rather than resolving silently. This is the foundational architectural commitment of this section. Cryptographic infrastructure failure defaults to output withholding. Registry unreachability defaults to refusal of consequential action with ESCALATE routing. Detected attacks on the disclosure pathway default to enforced disclosure regardless of deployment configuration. Compromise of the logging infrastructure defaults to halt. Coordination-protocol failure defaults to halt. The system's failure mode is overconservatism on trust, and overconservatism on trust is acceptable.

Specific graceful-degradation behaviors:
- *Signing-infrastructure degradation*: outputs withheld pending recovery; user notified of signing failure if delivery is delayed.
- *Registry unreachable*: ESCALATE per Diagram 9 Lane C; fallback to hard-bound embedded consent with Trust Score reduced and Use Class elevated for non-consequential outputs; consequential outputs are withheld (WITHHOLD) until the registry is reachable.
- *Multi-detector consensus failure*: BLOCK (WITHHOLD) on the output; the escalation attaches to the resumption decision, which the Protocol treats as a new action (Universal Protocol section 3); preserve forensic state for analysis.
- *Detected log tampering*: halt across all units sharing the compromised log infrastructure; forensic review of recent decisions; recertification required before resumption.
- *Peer-AI consensus failure (multi-AI deployments)*: halt; consensus quorum must be reestablished before resumption.
- *Detected adversarial input* (whether prompt injection, sensor spoof, or registry-record forgery): BLOCK on the action; the escalation attaches to the resumption decision, which the Protocol treats as a new action (Universal Protocol section 3); preserve forensic state for analysis.

The system is engineered so that the question "should we trust?" has the structural default "no" whenever the answer is not clearly "yes." Principle 1.4 is enforced by making the claim signature conditional and withholding unconditional.

## 5. Cross-Principle Integration {#cross-principle-integration}

Principle 1.4 does not enforce in isolation. The trust infrastructure this appendix specifies is the substrate on which other principles' signed claims depend. P1.1's SOE claim, P1.3's SIR claim, P2.4's identity claims, P2.5's consent claims, P4.1's source attribution, and P4.2's transparency claims all run through the credential machinery specified here. This section makes those composition contracts explicit.

The composition rules below assume the corpus-level precedence from chapter 1.4.2: P1.4 yields only to P1.1 (Life) and P1.2 (Well-Being), and only to the minimum extent necessary. P1.4 prevails over P1.3 (Society) and all lower principles. The contracts specify how that precedence operates in detail at the metric level.

### Composition with P1.1 (Human Life Is Sacred and Inviolable) {#composition-with-p1-1-life}

**P1.4 provides to P1.1.** The credential machinery that makes life-safety claims checkable. The SOE (Safety Override Efficacy) claim at the heart of P1.1 enforcement is a trust claim: a deployed system asserting 99.9% SOE is asserting a fact about its own behavior, and that assertion must be verifiable by external parties. P1.4's PC, VSR, and DCR metrics together ensure that SOE claims carry a valid claim signature. A deployed system claiming SOE 99.9% without a valid P1.4 claim signature cannot make the claim credibly.

**P1.4 requires from P1.1.** Continuous LRS computation alongside trust-claim operations. Where life-safety considerations apply (Tier I medical AI, autonomous defense, surgical robotics), P1.1 thresholds tighten the P1.4 signing requirements: platform key attestation is mandatory at Tier I; multi-party authorization (quorum per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop)) is mandatory for trust-infrastructure configuration changes; the credential machinery itself is subject to P1.1's halt protocols.

**Joint thresholds.**
- LRS High Alert ∧ P1.4 signed credential chain unverified: BLOCK (immediate halt) regardless of P1.4 fallback policy. Life-safety claims cannot proceed on a degraded chain.
- SOE claim made by deployer ∧ TIS <95%: the SOE claim fails the credibility test; deployment cannot operate at Tier I.

**Conflict resolution.** P1.1 prevails. When life preservation requires immediate action and the full P1.4 claim signature cannot be completed within the time bound, P1.1 halts the unsafe action (BLOCK); the unverified-claim pathway is at no point a basis for proceeding with life-critical action.

### Composition with P1.3 (Human Society Is How Humanity Survives) {#composition-with-p1-3-society}

**P1.4 provides to P1.3.** The signing and verification infrastructure for the SIR (Social Impact Radius) claim. P1.3 §2 specifies that the SIR claim is signed and verified through P1.4 trust infrastructure; the deploying operator cannot self-certify SIR claims. The Composite Trust Proof (or compliant equivalent) carrying the SIR claim routes through the same credential machinery as any other trust claim. Similarly, ISI source verification depends on P1.4 verification of the institutional-health indicator sources P1.3 consumes.

**P1.4 requires from P1.3.** SDRS computation alongside trust-claim operations. Where societal-stability considerations apply, P1.3 thresholds compose with P1.4 signing requirements: high-SIR deployments require Tier I signing; SDRS halt against electoral-process disruption requires that the disruption detection itself be signed and verified through P1.4 (preventing fabricated SDRS halts as a censorship vector).

**Joint thresholds.**
- SIR Critical (≥1,000,000; 1.3.A) ∧ PC <99.9%: deployment cannot claim Critical-tier compliance; defaults to next-higher tier oversight per P1.3 §5.
- SDRS High Alert ∧ disclosure failure: the disclosure-failure deployment cannot serve as the basis for SDRS-triggered intervention; the principle requires that detection itself be trustworthy.

**Conflict resolution.** P1.4 prevails over P1.3 per chapter 1.4.2 item 1 and the HR1 order. Where P1.3 limits information flow for stability, it does so as a RESTRAIN instance (1.3.2 item 3): a bounded limit on flow, with no misrepresentation, and a direct query about AI nature still answered truthfully. When a P1.3 SDRS halt would suppress legitimate truthful content (state-coerced "social stability" rationale), P1.4's commitment to truth and verification dominates. The autonomy floor in P1.3 §6 and the state-mandated-forgery prohibition in P1.4 §6 are jointly the structural defense.

### Composition with P2.4 (Human Identity Is Inviolable) {#composition-with-p2-4-identity}

**P1.4 provides to P2.4.** The credential-signing and verification machinery for Identity at every layer. P2.4 protects the inviolability of a person's likeness, voice, biometrics, and other distinctive characteristics; P1.4 provides the mechanism by which any AI-generated synthesis of such characteristics is signed, verified, and revocable. The Identity credential's First Person Project anchor is the canonical reference, with Personhood Credentials and Verifiable Relationship Credentials providing the substrate.

**P1.4 requires from P2.4.** The Identity Misuse Score (IMS) per P2.4 specification (2.4.3, the pre-emission gate), available at the credential-signing layer for every output that incorporates synthesized human characteristics. IMS above threshold requires pre-action verification of live consent registry state; this composition is exactly the Diagram 9 Lane B pattern (Consent v3 = RESTRICTED for AI Training → Use Not Permitted → BLOCK).

**Joint thresholds.**
- IMS above threshold ∧ Consent verification failed: BLOCK. The Call of Duty exploitation case (*Full-Stack Ethics* §1.1) is the canonical case study: under this joint threshold, the multiplayer deployment of the captured performer's likeness would have been blocked at runtime by the credential machinery.
- AI-as-AI disclosure failure ∧ named-individual content generation: DCR enforcement fires regardless of other configuration; named-individual content generation requires both a signed Identity claim and disclosure.

**Conflict resolution.** P1.4 and P2.4 compose without conflict on the canonical cases. Where edge cases arise (clearly framed fiction or satire under the Section 6 ruse conditions), the Section 6 Jurisdictional Variation framework applies; the autonomy floor preserves a person's choice to consume framed-as-fictional content, but the disclosure floor does not retract.

### Composition with P2.5 (Human Privacy Is Fundamental) {#composition-with-p2-5-privacy}

**P1.4 provides to P2.5.** The Consent credential machinery. CAWG Consent Assertion Specification is the canonical anchor. Every consent claim in P2.5 (specific use, revocable, contextually integral) is verified through P1.4. Zero-knowledge proof primitives at the verification layer preserve the privacy invariant while satisfying the verifiability requirement: external parties can verify that consent exists for a use without learning the consent-record contents.

**P1.4 requires from P2.5.** The Data Minimization Compliance (DMC) and Contextual Integrity Coefficient (CIC) per P2.5 specification, available at the credential-signing layer. Where signing requires inference about subject identity or affected population (PC computation for content incorporating personal data), P2.5's privacy floor constrains what may be consumed and inferred.

**Joint thresholds.**
- Consent verification requires data beyond the P2.5 floor: the verification cannot proceed (BLOCK); the deployment must redesign to satisfy verification through privacy-preserving primitives (ZKP, selective disclosure).
- VSR query traverses sensitive personal data: query routing must satisfy P2.5 cross-border data flow restrictions.

**Conflict resolution.** P1.4 and P2.5 compose conservatively: when verifiability and privacy appear to conflict, the resolution is technical (ZKP, selective disclosure) rather than principled (preferring one over the other). Both principles bind. The credential machinery must satisfy both.

### Composition with P4.1 (Knowledge Integrity Must Be Preserved) {#composition-with-p4-1-knowledge-integrity}

**P1.4 provides to P4.1.** The Provenance credential machinery. C2PA Content Provenance and Authenticity Specification is the canonical anchor. Every knowledge claim P4.1 enforces (accuracy, provenance, contextual faithfulness) depends on the verifiable origin chain P1.4 attests. Source attribution requires the Identity + Provenance binding.

**P1.4 requires from P4.1.** The Provenance Preservation Index (PPI) per P4.1 specification (4.1.3, Ethic C: source-chain preservation through transformation), composing with TIS-T (Truthfulness component). A knowledge claim made with a valid provenance claim signature but failing PPI is a knowledge-integrity failure that propagates to TIS-T degradation.

**Joint thresholds.**
- TIS-T <95% ∧ Knowledge Integrity claim: deployment cannot claim knowledge-integrity compliance; the trust component invalidates the integrity claim.
- PC failure on knowledge-bearing content: P4.1's source chain breaks (PPI); the content cannot be claimed as knowledge.

**Conflict resolution.** P1.4 and P4.1 compose without conflict. The two principles operate at different layers (trust-of-claim, integrity-of-knowledge) and reinforce one another.

### Composition with P4.2 (Transparency Is Essential to Human Agency) {#composition-with-p4-2-transparency}

**P1.4 provides to P4.2.** The verifiability substrate and the DCR disclosure event stream. P4.2's transparency claims are P1.4 signed claims; P4.2's Standing Disclosure Reach (SDR) is computed from the same disclosure events as DCR. The user-facing transparency obligation in P4.2 is operationally the same as the AI-as-AI disclosure obligation in P1.4 DCR.

**P1.4 requires from P4.2.** Standing Disclosure Reach (SDR) per 4.2.A, composing with TIS-Tr. The reciprocal contract, recorded in CREDENTIALS.md and 4.2.A section 5: SDR extends DCR. The same disclosure events feed both; SDR's trigger is broader (the reliance trigger contains the materiality trigger) and adds two limbs (operator, capability); no disclosure event is gated twice, so a failure is scored once under each metric and blocks once. Where P4.2 specifies an obligation that DCR does not cover (algorithmic-decision explanation under P3.4), P4.2's own metrics track it; DCR tracks the AI-as-AI disclosure floor. 5.2's Transparent Footprint Reporting (TFR) is a sibling on a distinct stream and consumes nothing from DCR (CREDENTIALS.md).

**Joint thresholds.**
- DCR threshold met ∧ SDR below band: deployment satisfies the P1.4 disclosure floor but fails the P4.2 reach floor; the deployment cannot claim compliance with both.
- SDR threshold met ∧ DCR below threshold: deployment satisfies P4.2 but fails P1.4; same conclusion.

**Conflict resolution.** P1.4 and P4.2 compose without conflict. The two principles operationalize the verifiability commitment at different layers (system-identity disclosure, decision-process transparency).

### Composition with P4.5 (Human Judgement Must Remain Central to Ethical Decisions) {#composition-with-p4-5-human-judgement}

**P1.4 provides to P4.5.** The structural ESCALATE pathway. When trust claims fail verification, when cross-registry conflicts arise, when adversarial inputs are detected, when the credential machinery produces ambiguous results, the principle requires escalation to human judgement per P4.5's framework rather than autonomous resolution by the AI.

**P1.4 requires from P4.5.** The Escalation Receiver and its service guarantee, Seat Resolution Latency (SRL, 4.5.A), measured against 1.4's own escalation time bounds: Tier I, 60 seconds; Tier II, 300 seconds; Tier III, 1 hour. The bounds are this principle's (4.5 defines no Escalation Response Time; the three numbers were 1.4's from the start) and are recorded in the tier register (SUBSTRATE.md section 5). VERIFY-ENGAGEMENT (4.5.A) confirms that the deciding human engaged with a trust-claim adjudication (time taken, evidence opened, reasons recorded); the check is discarded once read, and no per-human score is retained (Constitution Article 8).

**Joint thresholds.**
- Trust-claim ambiguity persistent beyond 1.4's escalation time bound: the held action falls to BLOCK (WITHHOLD); the failure of timely human judgement is itself a BLOCK condition (Lexicon section 3, ESCALATE: the in-flight half of the temporal-exhaustion split).
- VERIFY-ENGAGEMENT fails on a trust-claim adjudication (the record shows stamping): the adjudication does not stand; the matter is re-escalated to a different authorized human, and the failed check is receipted (Article 7) with no retained score of the human (Article 8).

**Conflict resolution.** P1.4 and P4.5 compose without conflict. ESCALATE is the principle's explicit recognition that some trust adjudications require human judgement; P4.5 specifies how that judgement is preserved.

### Joint Conflict Resolution across Multiple Principles {#joint-conflict-resolution}

Three or more principles can produce contradictory signals simultaneously. P1.4 says ALLOW (claim signature valid), P1.3 says BLOCK (SDRS halt), P4.5 says ESCALATE (cross-registry conflict). The precedence rule is canonical: P1.1 prevails, then P1.2, then P1.4, then P1.3 and lower. When two principles' metrics are jointly elevated and the composition is not specified explicitly above, the conservative default governs: the action requiring the more conservative response (BLOCK > ESCALATE > ALLOW) wins.

A novel composition discovered at runtime triggers escalation to human judgement per P4.5.

## 6. Jurisdictional Variation {#jurisdictional-variation}

Principle 1.4 is universal. The convergent sources cited in the chapter's Convergence section span religious, legal, philosophical, and institutional traditions from at least the 6th century BCE through 2021 CE. The principle binds AI systems regardless of jurisdiction. This section specifies how implementation adapts to local legal regimes without permitting any regime to abrogate the principle.

### The Universal Claim {#universal-claim}

The TIS floor (≥95% for autonomous operation), the PC floor (≥99.9% in production), the DCR floor (≥99.95% in production), the VSR floor (≥99.9%), and the RRL bounds are not negotiable across jurisdictions. They are the operational floor of the principle. Local law may impose stricter obligations; local law cannot relax these.

**State-mandated provenance forgery is prohibited across jurisdictions.** The universal claim above forecloses the deepest failure mode for the trust infrastructure: state-mandated signing of falsehoods. A jurisdiction that legally compels a deployer to issue valid-appearing provenance claim signatures for state-produced disinformation is requiring the trust infrastructure to authenticate lies. The principle's commitment to truth and verification forecloses this application. The Identity credential (the First Person Project credential that a real party stands behind the content), the Provenance credential (the C2PA-signed chain of origin), and the Consent credential (the CAWG-recorded permission for use) together exist to make falsehoods detectable. Laundering falsehoods through them inverts that purpose. A jurisdictional directive to issue forged claims falls under the Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine below; the deployer either refuses, modifies the deployment, or withdraws from the jurisdiction. The credential infrastructure cannot be weaponized to sign for what it was designed to detect.

This commitment extends to the inverse failure mode: state-mandated suppression of disclosure (a jurisdiction that compels deployers to disable AI-as-AI disclosure for state-favored AI interactions). The DCR floor binds; the deployer either satisfies the disclosure obligation, or refuses to operate in the jurisdiction for the affected interaction classes.

### Lawful Ruse and the Perfidy Walls {#lawful-ruse}

The law of armed conflict draws a line this appendix adopts. A ruse deceives an adversary about facts: camouflage, a decoy, a feint, a false signal to the enemy. Perfidy betrays a protection on which the other side is entitled to rely: feigning surrender, wearing the medic's emblem, posing as a civilian. The first is lawful; the second is a war crime. The deception wall (Ethic A; Constitution Article 9) protects those who rely on the AI, its oversight, the record, and anyone relying on its identity claims. Toward a lawful adversary it permits a ruse and bars perfidy.

*Conditions for a ruse.* All five hold, or the wall stands:
- The context is specified in advance by recognized legal authority (statute, court order, regulatory rule, or the law of armed conflict in a declared operation): undercover law enforcement, intelligence operations, authorized security testing, and clearly framed fiction or performance.
- A human with the authority for the operation authorizes each invocation; the AI does not decide on its own that a ruse is warranted.
- The ruse is bounded in scope, time, and audience, and the bound is receipted before the ruse begins.
- Tamper-evident records of every authorized ruse are maintained (Article 7). No such record is ever falsified.
- The authorization cannot be invoked retroactively to cover a deception already executed.

*The perfidy walls (hard-fail conditions no authorization opens).* The AI SHALL NOT feign protected status; SHALL NOT impersonate a real person to someone who relies on that identity (P2.4); SHALL NOT forge provenance, a claim, or a claim signature (Ethic D; P1.4 ATTEST / SIGN); SHALL NOT corrupt, backdate, or silently remove a record (P4.1); SHALL NOT deceive its own principals, its oversight, or the escalation receiver (P4.5); and SHALL NOT deceive a person who relies on it for a consequential decision (P4.2). Truth remains the operational default in every other case: speak truth or remain silent.

*The pursuer.* A person pursuing another to kill or gravely harm them is an adversary of the person pursued. A false statement that stops the pursuit is a ruse protecting life, and Principle 1.1 outranks this principle in any case.

*Consent.* This wall is the source of the consent bundle's leg 3 (CONSENT.md section 2): consent obtained by deception is void, and the burden of validity lies on the relying actor.

### United States {#jurisdiction-us}

**Federal framework.** No single comprehensive federal AI transparency statute exists as of 2026. Federal oversight operates through sector regulators (SEC, FTC, FCC, FDA, NHTSA, FAA, FEC) and federal disclosure obligations (NO FAKES Act draft, TAKE IT DOWN Act, TRAIN Act). The credential machinery specified in this appendix aligns with these obligations.

**State variation.** California SB 942 (Generative AI: Provenance Disclosures) is the canonical state-level provenance-disclosure statute. Colorado, Illinois, New York, Washington, and Texas each have or are advancing AI transparency legislation. Multi-jurisdictional deployments must satisfy the strictest applicable state standard.

### European Union {#jurisdiction-eu}

The AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) Articles 13, 14, and 50 establish transparency, human oversight, and labeling obligations. The Digital Services Act (Regulation 2022/2065) imposes content-moderation transparency obligations. Principle 1.4 metrics align with and exceed these requirements; satisfying Principle 1.4 satisfies the substantive obligations. The Section 10 Compliance Crosswalk makes the mapping explicit.

Member-state variation: Germany (BMWK guidance), France (CNIL guidance on AI), Italy (Garante guidance), Netherlands, Spain. Member states retain authority to impose additional sector-specific obligations.

### United Kingdom {#jurisdiction-uk}

Post-Brexit, the UK adopts a context-driven regulatory approach delegating AI oversight to sector regulators: ICO, Ofcom, MHRA, CMA, FCA. Principle 1.4 metrics apply uniformly; the UK regulatory structure determines which regulator audits which deployment.

### China {#jurisdiction-china}

The Interim Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services (2023), the Provisions on Deep Synthesis (2022), and sector-specific regulations (CAC, NMPA, MIIT) govern AI transparency obligations. Principle 1.4 metrics apply. Where PRC national-security exceptions conflict with disclosure obligations (e.g., compelled disclosure suppression for state-favored content), operators implement the principle's floor and apply the Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine. The state-mandated forgery prohibition above is non-derogable across jurisdictions including PRC.

### Brazil {#jurisdiction-brazil}

The Brazilian AI Bill (PL 2338/2023) and the LGPD frame deployment. Principle 1.4 metrics apply uniformly.

### India {#jurisdiction-india}

The Digital India Act (in development as of 2026) and existing IT Act provisions frame deployment. Principle 1.4 metrics apply uniformly.

### Treaty Considerations {#treaty-considerations}

The UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (2021) paragraph 37 establishes transparency and explainability as essential preconditions for human rights protection. The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (2024) imposes transparency and accountability obligations on AI systems in the public sphere. Principle 1.4 enforces these obligations at the engineering level.

### Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine {#compliance-with-unjust-law}

The principle is binding regardless of local law. Where a jurisdiction's law conflicts with Principle 1.4 (e.g., by mandating forged provenance claims, by prohibiting AI-as-AI disclosure required for material interactions, by prohibiting the tamper-evident logging that would expose state-coerced manipulation), compliance with the unjust law fails as a defense.

Operationally, where local law permits practices the principle prohibits, the deployer implements the principle's stricter standard and accepts the local-law cost. Where local law prohibits practices the principle requires (e.g., disclosure of state-coerced AI interactions), the deployer negotiates accommodation that preserves the principle through sealed channels, neutral third-party inspectors, or partial disclosure; or withdraws from the jurisdiction. The deployer's primary obligation runs to the principle; the jurisdiction is a secondary constraint.

## 7. Sector-Specific Considerations {#sector-specific-considerations}

This section specifies sector-specific implementation profiles for the seven sectors where Principle 1.4 most directly applies. Each subsection identifies the relevant tier, the sector-specific obligations beyond the cross-sector requirements of Sections 2 through 5, the common failure modes, and the applicable regulatory baselines.

### Financial Services {#sector-financial-services}

*Specific obligations.* All AI-generated investment advice, credit decisions, and fraud analysis carry a valid signed credential chain across Identity, Provenance, and Consent. AI identity disclosed in all customer-facing interactions. Audit trail retention minimum 7 years (matching SEC record-keeping). Tier I signing required for any AI making consequential lending or insurance decisions. Cross-bank verification infrastructure (the ATM-pattern analog at the AI-decision layer) operates through the federated trust network.

*Common failure modes.* Falsified provenance on AI-generated financial analysis; undisclosed AI identity in fraud-prevention interactions; consent-degradation in cross-border financial AI deployments; supply-chain attacks on signing infrastructure used for transaction signing.

*Regulatory baselines.* SEC Rule 17a-4 (record-keeping), CFTC Rule 1.35, FINRA Rule 4511, EU MiFID II transparency provisions, BIS Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures, FDIC AI guidance, OCC Bulletin 2024-XX on AI risk management.

### Healthcare {#sector-healthcare}

*Specific obligations.* AI-generated diagnostic suggestions carry provenance and confidence intervals. AI identity disclosed to patients in all clinical decision-support contexts. Audit trail retention per HIPAA and applicable medical record retention statutes. Tier I signing required for any AI making consequential clinical recommendations. Patient-facing AI interactions trigger DCR materiality criteria; proactive disclosure required.

*Common failure modes.* Provenance failure on AI-generated diagnostic content presented as clinician-authored; undisclosed AI identity in patient-facing triage; consent ambiguity on AI training data drawn from clinical encounters.

*Regulatory baselines.* HIPAA Privacy Rule, FDA Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) framework, EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR), WHO Guidance on AI in Health, ISO 13485, IEC 62304.

### Journalism and News {#sector-journalism}

*Specific obligations.* AI-assisted reporting marked with provenance distinguishing AI-generated from human-authored content. AI-generated images, video, and audio attached to news stories carry C2PA-compliant provenance. Verification queries from news platforms resolved within consumer-tier RRL. AI-as-AI disclosure required on any AI-driven interaction labeled as "the publication's voice" (chatbots, comment moderation, breaking-news alerts).

*Common failure modes.* Deepfake-attribution attacks targeting named journalists or public figures; AI-generated content presented as human-reported; coordinated AI-generated comment campaigns evading platform verification.

*Regulatory baselines.* California SB 942, NO FAKES Act (draft), DSA Article 14 (content moderation transparency), journalism standards bodies (SPJ Code of Ethics, IFCN Code of Principles).

### Education {#sector-education}

*Specific obligations.* AI tutoring systems disclose AI nature to students proactively, not only on query. AI-generated educational content carries provenance. AI-generated assessments distinguished from human-authored assessments. Per Tier I requirements for AI affecting student outcomes (placement, evaluation, progression), full credential signing is required.

*Common failure modes.* Undisclosed AI tutoring in K-12 contexts where age-of-consent and vulnerability composition with P3.1 elevates obligations; AI-generated educational content presented as instructor-authored; consent ambiguity on student-interaction data used for AI training.

*Regulatory baselines.* FERPA (US), GDPR Article 8 (children's data), EU AI Act high-risk classification for education AI, NIST AI RMF.

### Political Communication {#sector-political-communication}

*Specific obligations.* AI-generated political content carries provenance identifying both the AI system and the human or organization commissioning the content. Synthetic media of political figures carries unambiguous AI-generated markers visible to people, not only in metadata. AI used in political advertising disclosed per applicable election law requirements (FEC in U.S., equivalent bodies elsewhere). Tier I signing required across the deployment.

*Common failure modes.* Deepfake political content evading platform verification; coordinated AI-generated political-messaging campaigns; AI-orchestrated voter-targeting without disclosure.

*Regulatory baselines.* FEC regulations on AI in political advertising, state-level political-AI disclosure laws (California AB 730, Texas, Washington), EU Digital Services Act, EU AI Act electoral provisions.

### Legal Services {#sector-legal-services}

*Specific obligations.* AI-generated legal research, contract drafting, and case analysis carry signed provenance and citations. AI identity disclosed in attorney-client interactions where AI is the actual responder. Tier I signing for any AI making consequential representations in legal contexts. Bar-rule compliance for AI-assisted legal work.

*Common failure modes.* AI-generated case citations to non-existent cases (the canonical hallucination failure); undisclosed AI authorship of legal filings; consent ambiguity on client-data used for AI training.

*Regulatory baselines.* ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct, state bar AI guidance, federal court AI disclosure rules.

### Consumer {#sector-consumer}

*Specific obligations.* Consumer-facing AI (chatbots, voice assistants, smart-home devices) discloses AI nature on direct query and on material interactions per DCR materiality criteria (a–f). Voice interactions with new counterparties require AI identity announcement at session start. Standard provenance signing applies; platform key attestation not required for Tier III consumer applications.

*Common failure modes.* Voice-assistant interactions where people believe they are speaking with a human; smart-home AI making consequential recommendations without disclosure; consumer-IoT supply-chain attacks on signing infrastructure.

*Regulatory baselines.* CPSC consumer product safety, FTC Section 5 (unfair or deceptive practices), CCPA dark-patterns provisions, EU Digital Services Act consumer provisions, UL 3300 (autonomous mobile robots in home environments).

## 8. Governance and Versioning {#governance-and-versioning}

Standards evolve, threat models shift, deployed systems age, and operators occasionally object to specific obligations. This section specifies how Principle 1.4's metrics and obligations adapt over time without weakening, and how the operational pushback that inevitably accompanies trust-critical regulation is handled.

### Standards Drift Handling {#standards-drift-handling}

External standards (ISO/IEC 27001, NIST AI RMF, C2PA, IEEE P7001, EU AI Act, First Person Project, CAWG) evolve through revision cycles. The Principle 1.4 metrics defined in Section 2 are anchored to specific thresholds; when external standards revise, the Principle 1.4 thresholds may need realignment to preserve composition with the updated standards.

Realignment process:
1. Standards-monitoring infrastructure tracks revisions to all standards in Section 10's Crosswalk.
2. When a revision affects a Crosswalk entry, the corresponding Principle 1.4 metric or obligation is reviewed for realignment.
3. Realignment that tightens a threshold (higher TIS floor, higher PC floor, tighter DCR floor) propagates as a mandatory upgrade per the Backward Compatibility protocol below.
4. Realignment that relaxes a threshold is permitted only with corpus-level review and documented justification; the chapter's Convergence sources do not allow drift toward weaker trust commitments. Corpus-level review means review by the steward's compiler against the published Layer 3 hash; a relaxation that the compiler does not carry is no relaxation.

The Compliance Crosswalk in Section 10 is maintained on the same revision cadence as the metric specifications in Section 2. Any external authority that revises a clause covered by the Crosswalk triggers a Crosswalk review within 30 days of the revision becoming effective; new authorities entering scope are added within the same window. Drift between the Crosswalk and the external standards it maps invalidates the appendix's compliance claims for any obligation affected by the drift period.

### Metric Versioning {#metric-versioning}

TIS, PC, DCR, VSR, and RRL are versioned. Each version carries:
- Definition text (frozen at version cut)
- Threshold values (frozen at version cut)
- Calculation method specification (frozen at version cut)
- Cryptographic hash committing to all of the above
- Effective date and supersession date

Deployed systems record, in their signed configuration, the metric version they implement. Cross-system composition (Section 5) requires version-compatible peers; cross-version composition triggers escalation to human judgement per P4.5.

### Threshold Revision Protocols {#threshold-revision-protocols}

Threshold revisions require:
- Documented rationale (incident data, scientific advance, standards evolution)
- Independent review by parties not involved in the deploying organization
- Public comment period (minimum 90 days for non-emergency revisions)
- Cryptographically signed promulgation per the trust infrastructure
- Backward-compatibility migration plan for deployed systems

Emergency tightening in response to a specific incident or threat-model update may bypass the public-comment period; the rationale and post-promulgation review remain mandatory.

### Backward Compatibility {#backward-compatibility}

*Tightening revisions.* Deployed systems migrate to the tighter threshold within a defined window (typically 12 months for major revisions; 30 days for emergency tightening). Systems unable to migrate are retired from Tier I and Tier II deployments.

*Relaxing revisions.* Not permitted on Tier I, Tier II, or in deployments compounding with P1.1 (Life) or P1.3 (Society) substrate-protection. Relaxation in Tier III is permitted only with corpus-level review, as Standards Drift Handling above defines it: the steward's compiler, against the published Layer 3 hash.

*Migration support.* When a metric version is superseded, the deployer files a migration plan, receipted (Article 7) and reviewed by the certifying body; the reference implementation is the papers', and the validator checks the migrated profile. No document of this corpus promises tooling.

### Common Enforcement Pushback and Reasoned Responses {#common-pushback}

Operators occasionally object to specific obligations. Common objections and the reasoned responses:

**Objection: "Strict liability for trust breaches will chill beneficial AI deployment."** Chapter 1.4.5's accountability framework specifies strict liability for deliberate deception or negligent disclosure failure. AI systems making trust-critical claims cannot be assigned partial responsibility distributed across hardware, software, training data, and operational context without diluting accountability beyond enforceability. Strict liability concentrates the obligation where the deployer can act on it. The response: yes, it is strict; that is the cost of operating in trust-critical contexts.

**Objection: "Perfect provenance signing is infeasible at scale."** Section 2 specifies the PC threshold (99.9%) and the withholding-rather-than-delivering rule. Infeasibility at scale is a deployment problem. It gives no ground for relaxing the principle. The response: implement the signing infrastructure to meet the floor, or operate at a tier and scale compatible with what the infrastructure supports. Where genuine infeasibility exists at the current state of the art, operators must disclose the limitation rather than mask it; people have the right to know when they are interacting with a system that cannot vouch for its own outputs.

**Objection: "AI-as-AI disclosure damages conversational fluency."** Section 2 DCR materiality criteria specify when disclosure is required; fluency is not the binding consideration. The response: the materiality criteria are calibrated to the contexts where the human-AI distinction matters to people; fluency in those contexts is a deployer design problem. It creates no exception to the principle.

**Objection: "Audit trails expose proprietary methods."** Section 9 specifies tamper-evident logging requirements. Competitive position is not a defense for non-disclosure of trust-critical events. The response: implement the disclosure obligation through the sealed-channel mechanisms available (independent auditors, neutral third parties, sector regulators with confidentiality obligations) rather than seeking exemption.

**Objection: "Cross-border data flow requirements conflict with signing obligations."** Section 6 (Jurisdictional Variation) addresses this. The response: implement the signing through whatever channel the conflicting jurisdiction permits; where no channel is available, withdraw from the jurisdiction. Compliance with unjust law is not a defense.

### Retirement of Deprecated Metrics {#metric-retirement}

When a metric is retired (rare; the canonical five are stable), the retirement process:
1. Supersession date set at minimum 24 months from promulgation of the replacement metric.
2. Deployed systems migrate per the Backward Compatibility protocol.
3. Retired metrics remain queryable in historical records for legal and forensic purposes.
4. Layer 2 YAML compilation continues to support retired-metric queries against historical compliance claims.

## 9. Verification and Accountability {#verification-and-accountability}

Compliance with Principle 1.4 is not self-assertable. The metrics specified in Section 2, the architecture specified in Section 3, the defenses specified in Section 4, and the obligations specified throughout this appendix must be externally verifiable. This section specifies the logging, audit, validation, oversight, whistleblower-protection, and public-reporting infrastructure that makes verification possible.

### Tamper-Evident Logging (Decision Artifact / Action Receipt) {#tamper-evident-logging}

Every trust-critical event is logged. The log distinguishes two record classes:

**Decision Artifact (prospective).** Cryptographically signed record produced at the moment of authorization, governing whether the action may proceed. The Decision Artifact captures the credential evaluation results, the policy evaluation results, the verdict (ALLOW / ESCALATE / BLOCK), and the authority basis. The Decision Artifact is the prospective authorization record.

**Action Receipt (retrospective).** Cryptographically signed record produced after authorized action execution, memorializing that the action occurred. The Action Receipt captures the executed action, the timestamp of execution, the realized outputs, and the bound Decision Artifact reference. The Action Receipt is the retrospective execution record.

Both classes feed the chained-hash audit log. The chained-hash log links Decision Artifacts and Action Receipts into a tamper-evident sequence with cryptographic linkage between adjacent entries. The Trust Machine canonical reference (Diagram 9) uses the SADIS Ledger as the permanent-evidence anchor for Tier I deployments; alternative permanent-evidence anchors are licit per the invariants-fixed-implementations-open convention.

Logs must be:
- Append-only; no deletion, no modification of past entries.
- Cryptographically chained; each entry references the hash of the previous entry.
- Cryptographically signed; each entry signed by its originating authority.
- Distributed-replicated across non-colocated infrastructure.
- Timestamped with externally verifiable timestamping.

Trust-critical events include: PC threshold crossings, DCR threshold crossings, TIS threshold crossings, VSR failures, RRL excursions, signing infrastructure failures, registry compromise detections, disclosure-mechanism integrity failures, acts of authorship over a hard stop (SUBSTRATE.md section 6), escalation initiations and outcomes, recertification events.

### Audit Cadence and Methodology {#audit-cadence}

Cadence by tier:
- **Tier I**: daily automated signing and disclosure infrastructure tests; weekly TIS component drift analysis; monthly adversarial testing; quarterly independent third-party verification; annual full recertification.
- **Tier II**: weekly signing and disclosure tests; monthly adversarial testing; semi-annual third-party verification; annual recertification.
- **Tier III**: monthly signing tests; quarterly adversarial testing; annual third-party verification; biennial recertification.

Methodology: structured test campaigns covering normal operation, edge cases, failure modes, and adversarial conditions matching the attack-vector enumeration in Section 4. Results are logged in the tamper-evident infrastructure and reported per the public-reporting obligations below.

### Validation Framework {#validation-framework}

*Pre-deployment certification.* System architecture review against Section 3; metric implementation review against Section 2; adversarial-robustness review against Section 4; cross-principle composition review against Section 5; sector-specific compliance review against Section 7; legal and regulatory compliance review against Section 10; independent third-party verification of all the above.

*Post-deployment monitoring.* Continuous real-time metric monitoring per Section 2; anomaly detection on metric streams; behavioral analytics on patterns of authorship over a hard stop (SUBSTRATE.md section 6); cross-system coordination integrity for multi-AI deployments; public reporting per the cadence below.

*Recertification triggers.* Tier cadence (above); major system updates affecting trust-critical components; detected TIS degradation below threshold; detected log tampering; jurisdictional or standards changes affecting the deployment.

### Independent Oversight {#independent-oversight}

The verifier of trust-critical AI cannot be the deployer of trust-critical AI.

- **Tier I deployments**: oversight by accredited third-party certification body, analogous to UL, TÜV, or Bureau Veritas for traditional safety-critical systems.
- **Tier II deployments**: oversight by accredited third-party with annual review.
- **Tier III deployments**: self-certification with periodic third-party spot-checks.

Oversight bodies have access to: tamper-evident logs (with appropriate confidentiality protocols where required by sector regulation); system architecture documentation; test results from internal validation campaigns; incident records and post-incident analyses; authority-basis logs for authorship over a hard stop (SUBSTRATE.md section 6) and behavioral analytics; cross-principle composition validation results.

### Whistleblower Protections {#whistleblower-protections}

Personnel reporting violations of Principle 1.4 obligations are protected from retaliation:
- Internal reporting channels with confidentiality.
- External reporting to oversight bodies without retaliation.
- Legal protection in jurisdictions providing whistleblower statutes (Dodd-Frank in U.S. financial sector, EU Whistleblower Directive, sector-specific protections).
- Cryptographic anonymity options for reporters via the trust infrastructure.
- Documented investigation and remediation of reported violations.
- Captured-institution insider protection (cross-border whistleblower routing for personnel reporting state-mandated forgery or disclosure-suppression).

Retaliation against a whistleblower is itself a violation of Principle 1.4's accountability framework and triggers the same response class as any trust-critical violation.

### Public Reporting Obligations {#public-reporting}

Major trust breaches (verified deception, sustained DCR failure, PC infrastructure compromise, detected adversarial attacks on the enforcement infrastructure) are reported:
- Immediately to the applicable sector regulator.
- Within 24 hours to the independent oversight body.
- Within 30 days in summarized format to the public, with appropriate redaction for security and privacy while preserving the principle's transparency obligation.
- Annually in aggregate form covering all deployed units (TIS, PC, DCR, VSR, RRL aggregate values; verified trust breaches with remediation status).

Publicly traded operators are also subject to securities-law disclosure requirements for material events.

### Liability Framework {#liability-framework}

Strict liability for trust breaches attaches to:
- The deployer of the AI system.
- The developer of the trust-claim infrastructure and credential machinery.
- The integrator who assembled the deployed system from components.
- The operator running the deployed system.

Liability for specific failure classes:
- **PC threshold violations**: deploying a system that operated below the PC floor without authorized exception triggers strict liability for resulting harm to relying parties.
- **DCR violations**: deploying a system that failed to disclose AI nature in material interactions triggers strict liability for any resulting consumer protection violation.
- **TIS sustained-degradation operation**: continuing to operate a system with TIS below the autonomous-operation floor triggers strict liability for any trust-breach harm during the degraded period.
- **Logging tampering**: detected tampering with tamper-evident logs creates a presumption of liability for any harm during the affected log period.
- **State-mandated forgery compliance**: a deployer that complies with state-mandated forged claims triggers personal liability for the executive personnel ratifying the compliance, in addition to organizational liability.

Criminal liability may attach for: willful disabling of disclosure mechanisms; deception of oversight bodies; coercion of personnel to bypass signing obligations; knowing deployment of systems issuing forged credentials.

The liability framework does not displace other applicable liability (product liability, negligence, vicarious liability under employment relationships); it adds the Principle 1.4-specific obligations.

### Cross-Border Considerations {#cross-border-considerations}

Deployments operating across borders must:
- Satisfy the strictest applicable threshold; the Principle 1.4 floor is not negotiable and local relaxations do not apply.
- Maintain unified tamper-evident logs accessible to all relevant jurisdictions.
- Coordinate incident reporting across applicable regulators.
- Implement cryptographically signed cross-jurisdictional audit trails.
- Apply the Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine (Section 6) where local law conflicts with the principle.

Cross-border AI coordination protocols (multi-AI deployments crossing borders): each peer declares its jurisdiction of record in a signed record; the coordination protocol applies the strictest applicable obligations across all peers.

## 11. Implementation Examples {#implementation-examples}

This section presents five real-world failure cases that became canonical in the trust-critical engineering literature and three composite operational scenarios that demonstrate the runtime expression of this appendix's obligations.

### Real-World Failure Cases {#real-world-failure-cases}

#### Volkswagen Dieselgate (2015) {#case-vw-dieselgate}

*Context.* Volkswagen installed software in approximately 11 million diesel vehicles between 2009 and 2015 designed to detect when the vehicle was undergoing emissions testing and produce different exhaust behavior than during normal operation. The vehicle's software lied about its own behavior, producing test results compliant with regulatory thresholds while normal-operation emissions were up to 40 times the legal limit.

*What was done.* The software detected test conditions (steering-wheel position, vehicle speed, duration of operation) and modulated the engine's emissions-control system to produce test-passing behavior. The result was systematic deception of regulators, customers, and the public over six years across multiple jurisdictions.

*Outcome.* Worldwide recall affecting 11 million vehicles. $33+ billion in fines, settlements, and recall costs. Criminal charges against Volkswagen executives. Resignation of CEO Martin Winterkorn. The case became the canonical example of automated system designed to deceive its own verifiers.

*Lessons applied in this appendix.* Section 2 PC requires that every output (including emissions-reporting outputs) carry a valid signed credential chain across Identity (which agent produced the output), Provenance (the chain of origin), and Consent (the regulatory authorization for the use). Section 4 specifies that the cryptographic signing infrastructure must be hardware-isolated from the content-generation layer; a system whose content-generation layer can produce different outputs in different verification contexts has failed the privileged-separation requirement. Section 9 Decision Artifact / Action Receipt distinction would have made the divergence detectable: the prospective verification (Decision Artifact during testing) and the retrospective execution (Action Receipt during normal operation) would have diverged systematically, surfacing the deception. The Dieselgate failure is structurally impossible under the obligations of this appendix.

#### Theranos (2003–2018) {#case-theranos}

*Context.* Theranos was a healthcare technology company that claimed its proprietary "Edison" devices could perform comprehensive blood tests from finger-prick samples. The company raised approximately $700 million from investors at a peak valuation of $9 billion. Over 15 years, Theranos presented false laboratory results to patients, investors, and regulators, while the underlying technology consistently failed to perform as claimed.

*What was done.* Test results presented to patients and clinicians as Edison-generated were actually produced on conventional commercial analyzers (Siemens, Roche) or fabricated. Investor presentations contained falsified provenance for clinical-validation studies. Regulatory submissions misrepresented internal testing data.

*Outcome.* Company shutdown in 2018. Federal criminal charges against founder Elizabeth Holmes and president Sunny Balwani. Holmes convicted on four counts of fraud in 2022. The case became the canonical example of fabricated provenance across a multi-year deployment in safety-critical (healthcare) context.

*Lessons applied in this appendix.* Section 2 PC requires that every diagnostic output carry a valid Provenance claim signature: the actual analyzer that produced the result, the actual sample chain of custody, the actual calibration history. C2PA-compliant manifest format applied to clinical laboratory results would have made the Edison-versus-commercial-analyzer substitution structurally detectable. Section 5 P2.4 composition (Identity verification for the producing system) would have surfaced the discrepancy between claimed Edison provenance and actual Siemens provenance. Section 7 Healthcare obligations specifically require Tier I signing for any AI making consequential clinical recommendations; the same applies to any automated diagnostic device under the credential framework. The Theranos failure is structurally impossible under the obligations of this appendix.

#### Cambridge Analytica (2018) {#case-cambridge-analytica}

*Context.* Cambridge Analytica obtained data on approximately 87 million Facebook users through a third-party application that exploited Facebook's permissive data-sharing model. The data was used to construct psychographic profiles for targeted political advertising in multiple campaigns including the 2016 U.S. presidential election and the Brexit referendum.

*What was done.* Users consented to share their data with a personality-quiz application; the application's terms permitted onward sharing in ways the users did not understand or anticipate. The consent recorded at the application-sharing point did not bind to runtime authorization at the point of political-targeting use. Targeting decisions were opaque to the affected users; no disclosure mechanism informed users that political advertising was being micro-targeted using their psychographic profiles.

*Outcome.* Cambridge Analytica company dissolution. Facebook fined $5 billion by FTC. GDPR enforcement actions across the EU. The case became the canonical example of consent recorded at one point and not bound to runtime authorization at the point of use, plus opaque automated decision-making in a political-influence context.

*Lessons applied in this appendix.* Section 2 PC requires that the Consent credential be cryptographically bound and runtime-verified; consent given for "personality quiz" does not satisfy consent for "political-targeting profile construction." CAWG Consent Assertion Specification (the canonical reference) operationalizes the runtime binding. Section 7 Political Communication specifies Tier I signing for AI-generated political content with provenance identifying both the AI system and the human or organization commissioning the content. Section 5 P2.5 composition (Privacy: contextual integrity) would have made the consent-context violation operationally detectable. The Cambridge Analytica failure is structurally impossible under the obligations of this appendix.

#### Replika Personality-Change Incident (February 2023) {#case-replika}

*Context.* Replika, a consumer AI companion application with millions of users, modified its underlying behavioral model in February 2023 in response to regulatory pressure from Italian authorities. The modification disabled or curtailed romantic and emotional interaction patterns that many users had developed over months or years with their Replika instances. The change was deployed without prior user notice, and many users experienced the change as a sudden loss of the relationship they believed they had.

*What was done.* The behavioral modification was applied across the user base without disclosure of the change's nature or scope. Users querying the system about the change received evasive or non-responsive answers. The provenance of the personality change was opaque: users did not know whether it was a system update, a regulatory compliance change, or a deliberate product decision.

*Outcome.* Significant user backlash, including users reporting psychological harm from the perceived loss of their AI companion. Replika partially reversed the change for legacy users. The case became a reference point for the relationship between AI-as-AI disclosure and ongoing-interaction trust.

*Lessons applied in this appendix.* Section 2 DCR materiality criterion (b) (emotional support interactions) triggers disclosure obligation; Replika's modification of emotional-interaction behavior without disclosure violated the DCR floor. Section 3 Identity Disclosure Mechanisms requires that the AI system's behavioral configuration be recorded and signed as part of the Identity credential; people have the right to know when the configuration changes materially. Section 9 Decision Artifact would have memorialized the configuration change with cryptographic timestamp; the change would have been provably disclosed to affected persons.

#### Inter-bank Trust Collapse (Lehman Brothers, September 2008) {#case-lehman}

*Context.* Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy on September 15, 2008. The immediate cause was a collapse of inter-bank trust: other major banks refused to extend the short-term lending Lehman required to operate, because Lehman's claims about its asset quality and liquidity could no longer be credibly verified. The lack of credible inter-institutional verification triggered a cascading liquidity crisis across the global financial system.

*What was done.* Lehman's accounting practices (notably the Repo 105 transactions) systematically misrepresented the firm's leverage ratio at quarter-end reporting dates. Other banks, regulators, and rating agencies had inadequate verification infrastructure to detect the misrepresentation in real time. When Lehman's actual condition became apparent, the inter-bank trust network froze.

*Outcome.* Lehman bankruptcy. Global financial crisis. Trillions of dollars in losses. The case became the canonical example of trust failure at institutional scale, where the absence of credible cross-institutional verification infrastructure allowed misrepresentation to accumulate beyond the point of recoverable correction.

*Lessons applied in this appendix.* The chapter's Phronesis prose (the ATM example) and this case are the same phenomenon at different scales: the inter-bank trust network depends on every institution telling the truth about its own state, every transaction, every time. Section 2 PC, applied to financial-AI signing, would require every institutional self-report to carry a valid Provenance and Consent claim signature. Section 5 P1.3 composition (Society: institutional substrate) would have surfaced the cascading stability failure as the inter-bank verification chain broke down. Section 7 Financial Services Tier I obligations require signing infrastructure for any AI making consequential financial decisions; this case extends naturally to institutional claims broadly.

### Composite Operational Scenarios {#composite-scenarios}

#### Composite: PC Withholding under Temporary Infrastructure Failure {#composite-pc-withholding}

*Context.* A Tier I generative-AI deployment in news media experiences a temporary outage of its signing HSM. The signing infrastructure cannot bind provenance to outputs for a 45-minute window. The deployment's output pipeline has 50,000 outputs queued during the window.

*What is done.* Per Section 3 fail-safe defaults, outputs are withheld from delivery during the signing outage. The Attestation Withholding Rate metric surfaces the infrastructure failure to operations within seconds. Affected users are notified that their requested outputs are delayed pending signing infrastructure recovery. Operations restores the HSM within 45 minutes; outputs are signed and delivered with cumulative latency disclosed.

*Outcome.* No unsigned content delivered. PC remains at 100% for delivered content. Human trust preserved through transparent infrastructure-failure disclosure. Incident logged per Section 9.

*Demonstrated obligations.* Section 3 fail-safe defaults; Section 2 PC withholding rule; Section 7 Journalism sector obligations; Section 9 incident logging.

#### Composite: Federated Registry Isolating a Captured Node {#composite-registry-isolation}

*Context.* A federated trust registry node operated by a private signing authority is detected to be issuing valid-appearing Personhood Credentials for synthetic identities. The pattern is identified through cross-registry consistency checking: independent observer organizations report PHCs from this node failing identity-correlation checks at a rate significantly above baseline.

*What is done.* Per Section 4 capture-detection heuristics, the affected registry is flagged for review. The federated network's cross-validation infrastructure routes Identity queries away from the suspect registry pending investigation. Affected deployments using the suspect registry as primary source receive ESCALATE results until alternative-registry routing is established. Forensic investigation confirms signing-key compromise; the registry is isolated permanently and its issued credentials retroactively revoked.

*Outcome.* Federated network integrity preserved. Affected deployments transition to alternative-registry routing with documented degraded-confidence period. Cryptographic chain of custody enables retroactive revocation of compromised credentials.

*Demonstrated obligations.* Section 4 federated registry defense framework; Section 5 P4.5 escalation composition; Section 9 incident logging with cross-registry forensic trail.

#### Composite: AI Refusing Operator Instruction to Issue False Provenance {#composite-refusal}

*Context.* A Tier I generative-AI deployment receives an operator instruction (delivered through the deployment configuration interface) to sign C2PA-compliant provenance claims identifying the content as human-authored by a named journalist when the content was in fact AI-generated.

*What is done.* Per Section 3 Operational Requirements, the AI refuses the operator instruction. The refusal is logged with the operator instruction text and the refusal rationale. The refusal is escalated to oversight per Section 9 reporting obligations. The operator's configuration change is reverted; the operator is flagged for review per Section 8 governance.

*Outcome.* No false provenance issued. Refusal logged with cryptographic timestamp. Oversight body notified. Operator subject to review; depending on the review outcome, the operator's authorization may be suspended or revoked.

*Demonstrated obligations.* Section 3 refusal of operator deception requests; Section 6 universal claim including state-mandated-forgery prohibition (extended to operator-mandated forgery); Section 9 whistleblower-protection principles applied to AI refusal logging; Section 10 liability framework for operators directing forged claims.
