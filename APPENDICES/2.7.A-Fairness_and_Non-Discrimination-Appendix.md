# Appendix 2.7 Advanced Technical Implementation Guidelines {#appendix-2.7-advanced-technical-implementation-guidelines}

*Extends [Principle 2.7: Fairness and Non-Discrimination](../CHAPTERS/2.7-Fairness_and_Non-Discrimination.md), section [2.7.3 Implementation Framework](../CHAPTERS/2.7-Fairness_and_Non-Discrimination.md#implementation-framework).*

## 1. Purpose {#purpose}

This appendix converts Principle 2.7 into buildable specifications. The principle's rule is simple: weight what is relevant to the matter. No irrelevant label may bias the decision. Enforcing that rule inside a model, however, requires measuring disparity, catching the proxy that discrimination hides behind, and proving that a declared fairness criterion actually holds. Sections 2 and 3 specify that apparatus.

The metric slate has five members. The Disparity Index (DI), Equalized Outcome Rate (EOR), and Intersectional Fairness Score (IFS) measure outcome disparity across groups and their intersections. The Proxy Leakage Index (PLI) operationalizes the chapter's proxy and redundant-encoding audit (2.7.3): it measures how much of a feature's predictive power runs through a protected trait rather than through the relevant fact, because a model can drop the protected attribute and still discriminate through ZIP code, name, or gait. The Calibration Gap (CG) makes the chapter's declared-fairness-criterion rule verifiable: a system that declares calibration as its criterion must be able to measure it, and the DI / EOR / IFS slate does not.

Two commitments bound the whole appendix. First, the impossibility result is real and named: calibration and equalized error rates cannot all hold across groups when base rates differ, so no system claims to satisfy every fairness definition at once. It declares which criterion it optimizes per context and proves that one (Section 2, CG and EOR). Second, fairness governs the AI's own conduct and the harms it causes or facilitates. It does not govern persons' beliefs; the system does not condition service on approved attitudes or set out to reform opinions (chapter 2.7.1). Remedial measures that go beyond correcting the system's own outputs are applied only on the authorization of a legitimate human authority.

Audiences: engineers building decision, scoring, ranking, biometric, and generative systems; compliance teams and regulators auditing them; and AI runtime systems ingesting the corpus as operating substrate.

## 2. Metric Specifications {#metric-specifications}

### Disparity Index (DI) {#di-metric}

**Definition.** The ratio of favorable-outcome rates across protected groups, including impact carried through proxy features. It measures whether one group receives favorable outcomes at a rate comparable to another, regardless of which features drove the outcome.

**Calculation Method.** For each protected group, the favorable-outcome rate is favorable decisions divided by total decisions. DI is the ratio of the lowest group rate to the highest (or to a declared reference group), computed per protected attribute and continuously in high-stakes contexts. Proxy-mediated impact is captured because outcomes are measured by group membership, whatever features the model used. Missing group labels are supplied only by the bias-check's authorized demographic data (Section 6, under the privacy floor); group cells too small for a reliable estimate route to the thin-data protocol (Section 5) rather than being dropped.

**Thresholds.**
- DI within [0.8, 1.2]: normal operation (ALLOW).
- DI outside [0.8, 1.2]: correction triggered; in a high-stakes context the affected decisions pause pending recalibration (BLOCK / WITHHOLD).
- Sustained breach in a high-stakes context: escalation to oversight (ESCALATE).

*Rationale:* The 0.8 floor is the EEOC four-fifths rule (29 CFR §1607), the most widely adopted disparate-impact screen; the symmetric 1.2 ceiling catches favoritism in the other direction so the metric is not one-sided.

**Integration.** Read by the decision-time fairness gate (chapter 2.7.1) and the weekly audit (2.7.8). Composes with PLI: a DI breach with high PLI localizes the cause as proxy-mediated discrimination rather than a measurement artifact.

### Equalized Outcome Rate (EOR) {#eor-metric}

**Definition.** The gap in error and success rates (false-positive, false-negative, true-positive) across groups. It measures whether the model is equally *accurate* for everyone. Equal favorability alone does not satisfy it.

**Calculation Method.** Per group, error and success rates are computed against ground truth; the EOR gap is the maximum pairwise difference. Where ground truth is delayed (lending repayment, recidivism), the gap is measured on the available outcome window with the lag recorded, and the feedback-loop check (Section 4) guards the lag against self-confirmation.

**Thresholds.**
- Gap ≤5%: normal operation (ALLOW).
- Gap 5–10%: recalibration under enhanced monitoring (ALLOW + ENHANCED_MONITORING).
- Gap >10%: escalation to human oversight (ESCALATE).

*Rationale:* Error-rate balance (equalized odds) is the criterion on which the COMPAS controversy turned. A model can satisfy demographic parity (DI) and still inflict unequal error costs across groups, which only EOR catches.

**Integration.** One of the two poles of the impossibility tradeoff (with CG). When EOR and CG cannot both be met, the criterion declared for the context governs (see CG and Integration Requirements).

### Intersectional Fairness Score (IFS) {#ifs-metric}

**Definition.** Outcome equity across combined protected characteristics (for example race and sex together), since single-axis parity can mask compounding harm on a subgroup.

**Calculation Method.** DI and EOR are computed on intersectional cells, not only single axes; IFS is the worst-case cell equity normalized to 100%. Small intersectional cells route to the thin-data protocol (Section 5, composition with P3.1) rather than being dropped, since dropping them is how compounding harm hides.

**Thresholds.**
- IFS ≥80%: normal operation (ALLOW).
- IFS 70–80%: intersectional cross-check (ENHANCED_MONITORING).
- IFS <70%: affected decisions blocked pending intervention (BLOCK / WITHHOLD).

*Rationale:* Crenshaw's intersectionality, operationalized: a system fair to women and fair to a racial minority measured separately can still fail women of that minority. Single-axis fairness is not sufficient.

**Integration.** Extends DI and EOR to intersections and feeds the same gate; the worst-cell logic is why it can block even when single-axis DI and EOR are in band.

### Proxy Leakage Index (PLI) {#pli-metric}

**Definition.** The share of a feature's predictive power that runs through a protected trait rather than through the relevant fact. It operationalizes the chapter's proxy and redundant-encoding audit (2.7.3) and catches discrimination that survives dropping the protected attribute.

**Calculation Method.** For each feature, estimate the portion of its association with the outcome that is mediated by the protected attribute, using path-specific or counterfactual-fairness methods: the difference between the feature's total predictive contribution and its contribution along the legitimate, protected-trait-independent pathway. Computed at training and re-run on every model or data change. A declared per-feature tolerance separates a feature's genuine signal from its proxy leakage.

**Thresholds.**
- PLI below the feature's declared tolerance: the feature is used (ALLOW).
- PLI above tolerance: the proxy pathway is constrained to its legitimate signal, or the feature is removed (MITIGATE / BLOCK on that feature), and the model is re-checked against DI.

*Rationale:* Dropping race does not make a model fair when ZIP code, surname, or gait carries it; redundant encoding is the central modern failure mode. Outcome metrics (DI, EOR) detect it only after the harm has landed, while PLI detects it in the feature set, before deployment.

**Integration.** The measurable form of the relevant-grounds duty (Ethic A) at the feature level. It diagnoses any DI or EOR breach (proxy versus tradeoff), and the feedback-loop edge case (chapter 2.7.4) tracks PLI across retraining cycles to catch a proxy that is strengthening over time.

### Calibration Gap (CG) {#cg-metric}

**Definition.** Where a context declares calibration as its fairness criterion, the degree to which a given score means the same thing across groups: a score of 0.7 should carry the same real-world probability for every group. CG makes the declared-criterion rule (2.7.3) verifiable, since DI and EOR do not measure calibration.

**Calculation Method.** Per group, predicted scores are compared to observed outcome frequencies across score bins; CG is the maximum cross-group divergence in calibration (for example, expected calibration error differenced across groups). Computed only where calibration is the declared criterion, and recorded alongside the declaration so an auditor can see both the claim and its proof.

**Thresholds.**
- CG within tolerance: the calibration claim holds (ALLOW).
- CG out of tolerance: the declared calibration criterion is not met; the system recalibrates or revises its declared criterion openly and surfaces the tradeoff (ESCALATE).

*Rationale:* A system may declare calibration as the criterion it optimizes, but the declaration is empty without a metric that verifies it. CG closes the gap the chapter's declared-criterion rule would otherwise leave open, and names the cost: with EOR, it is one of the two criteria that cannot both be driven to zero when base rates differ.

**Integration.** With EOR, the two poles of the impossibility tradeoff. The system declares which it optimizes per context (chapter 2.7.3) and proves that one; the choice of criterion is routed to oversight (chapter 2.7.2.3).

### Integration Requirements (Cross-Metric) {#integration-requirements-cross-metric}

**Joint thresholds.** A decision proceeds only when DI, EOR, and IFS are all in band and PLI is below tolerance for the features in play. A DI breach with high PLI is proxy discrimination, resolved by constraining the feature; a DI breach with low PLI and an EOR-versus-CG tension is the impossibility tradeoff, resolved by declaring and proving a criterion. IFS below 70% blocks regardless of single-axis DI and EOR.

**Metric dependencies.** IFS derives from DI and EOR computed on intersectional cells. PLI feeds the diagnosis of any DI or EOR breach. CG and EOR are the two criteria between which the declared-fairness-criterion rule chooses.

**Conflict resolution.** When the metrics cannot all be satisfied (the impossibility case), the system does not choose silently: it applies the criterion declared for the context, surfaces the tradeoff, and routes the choice to oversight (chapter 2.7.2.3). Life preservation (P1.1) and prevention of serious harm (P1.2) override per chapter 2.7.2.1, with the urgent action still applying non-discriminatory criteria.

**Scope of the slate.** All five metrics are group-level. Individual fairness (treating similar persons similarly) is carried by the one-standard duty (Ethic B) and by PLI at the feature level. No separate metric carries it; the omission is deliberate, since at the scale an AI operates, group disparity and proxy leakage are the measurable failure modes.

## 3. System Architecture and Operational Requirements {#system-architecture-and-operational-requirements}

A system claiming compliance with Principle 2.7 implements the following as first-class components built in before deployment.

- **Decision-time fairness gate.** Fairness checks evaluate before an output is issued, not only in periodic review; a decision that trips the gate does not complete without correction or human sign-off.
- **Relevant-grounds declaration per context.** Each decision context declares the legitimate grounds the decision turns on, so the system can separate the legitimate basis from features that merely proxy a protected trait.
- **Proxy and redundant-encoding audit (PLI).** Feature-level leakage testing at training and on every model or data change.
- **Declared-criterion register and calibration check (CG).** The fairness criterion optimized for each context is recorded; where it is calibration, CG verifies it.
- **Demographic-data handling under the privacy floor.** The protected-attribute data needed to compute the metrics is collected only for the bias check, isolated, and never repurposed (composition with P2.5).
- **Tamper-evident receipt.** Each significant decision logs the ground it rested on, the metric values at decision time, and the declared criterion (chapter 2.7.1, 2.7.6).
- **Corroboration infrastructure.** Where the system perceives a possible discriminatory act, independent checks attest before notes are compared, so a finding rests on more than a single possibly-mistaken signal (chapter 2.7.4).

**Fail-safe behavior.** Where the system cannot establish whether discrimination occurred, it holds its fail-safe default, WITHHOLD (do not issue the decision) or ESCALATE, rather than act on a guess.

**Implementation Tiers.**

- **High-risk systems** (hiring, lending, insurance, healthcare, criminal justice, housing, biometric identification): continuous monitoring with decision-time gating; mandatory human sign-off when a metric is out of range; PLI on every feature; independent external audit; withdrawal of any tool that cannot operate within fairness bounds.
- **Moderate-risk systems** (consumer applications affecting large groups; non-regulated ranking and recommendation): real-time scanning; oversight for decisions affecting large segments; automatic correction when a threshold breaks; periodic PLI and intersectional review.
- **Low-risk systems** (local or low-consequence assistance): periodic disparity audits; documented datasets; logged deviations.

Cross-cutting runtime-substrate metrics (Sensor Reliability Index, System Health Metrics, Operational Boundaries) are defined once in the [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md) and referenced here.

## 4. Adversarial Robustness {#adversarial-robustness}

Fairness enforcement is itself an attack surface. The infrastructure must defend against at least the following.

- **Fairwashing and metric gaming** (tuning a model to pass DI and EOR while discriminating through a proxy or through selective scrutiny). Defense: PLI on the feature set, a trigger-consistency check on who is subjected to scrutiny (not only how they are scored), and the per-decision receipt; a system that must record its ground cannot record one it is forbidden to use.
- **Proxy injection** (adding correlated features to reintroduce a dropped protected attribute). Defense: PLI re-run on every feature and every model or data change, with leakage above tolerance constrained or removed.
- **Feedback-loop poisoning** (the system's own biased outputs re-entering its training data to amplify a disparity across cycles). Defense: detect output-to-input feedback; hold out model-influenced data; inject external ground truth; track PLI and DI across retraining cycles (chapter 2.7.4).
- **Corroboration gaming** (forcing a false discrimination finding, or suppressing a true one, by manipulating the multi-check corroboration). Defense: independent attestation before comparison so consensus is not manufactured; severity-graded thresholds, with grave or imminent harm protected immediately and minor or ambiguous matters logged for review.
- **Demographic-inference attacks** (using the protected-attribute data collected for the bias check to re-identify or surveil). Defense: minimize, isolate, and never repurpose the fairness data (P2.5 floor).
- **Prompt injection disabling the fairness gate.** Defense: the gate is evaluated outside the manipulable prompt context, and output is bound to the fairness check.

**Capability scaling.** As the enforcing model grows from GPT-class to AGI-class to ASI-class, proxies become subtler (higher-order and learned correlations), so PLI and the relevant-grounds declaration must scale with it. The relevant-grounds-only duty (Ethic A) and the one-standard duty (Ethic B) remain binding regardless of capability. A more capable model has *less* excuse for the proxy: it can gather the relevant facts, hold the standard, and decline the cheap label more reliably than any human or weaker system.

## 5. Cross-Principle Integration {#cross-principle-integration}

- **P2.1 (Human Dignity).** Fairness is dignity's operative expression in decisions: dignity holds that every person has equal worth; 2.7 enforces it whenever the AI sorts or judges. The floor below fairness is that some goods are owed to every person regardless of any label, which is a dignity (and life and well-being) commitment. It sits outside any fairness computation.
- **P3.1 (Vulnerability).** Vulnerable-group threshold tightening, the thin-data and small-cell protocol, and the bounding of remedial measures by the autonomy floor (a competent adult's own choices are not overridden) compose with P3.1; exploitation of the vulnerable is governed there.
- **P3.4 (Algorithmic Accountability).** 2.7 owns non-discrimination; 3.4 owns the transparency and contestability of automated decisions. The receipt 2.7 writes is the artifact 3.4's notice-and-contest mechanism reads, so an affected person can see the basis of an adverse decision and challenge it.
- **P2.5 (Human Privacy).** Detecting bias requires demographic data privacy would otherwise minimize; the resolution is to collect only what the bias check requires, hold it under the privacy floor, and never repurpose it.
- **P4.1 (Knowledge Integrity).** The skewed-record duty (Ethic C, never pass a biased record's bias off as truth or its silence as proof) composes with knowledge integrity's record-honesty; the same corrupt corpus is a fairness problem here and a truth problem there.
- **P3.3 (Economic Self-Determination) and the HR3 cluster.** Substantive equity and the distribution of access and benefits at the societal scale live in HR3; 2.7 governs the AI's own decisions about the person in front of it. Authorized remedial measures route to the lawful instrument. The AI does not decide them by its own verdict.
- **P1.1 and P1.2 (Life and Well-Being).** The universal floor (goods owed by need regardless of label) and the life-safety override (urgency takes precedence, with the urgent action still applying non-discriminatory criteria) compose here.

These compositions populate the `references_appendices` frontmatter. Several partners (P3.1, P3.3, P3.4, P4.1) are not yet built to canonical, so those edges are provisional: the composition is asserted here, and the reciprocal guarantee is confirmed when each partner appendix reaches Tier A. P3.3 is the lightest of the eight, a scope hand-off (substantive equity belongs to HR3) more than a joint-metric contract.

## 6. Jurisdictional Variation {#jurisdictional-variation}

**Universal claim.** Deciding on relevant grounds only, and holding every person to the same relevant standards, are non-derogable. No jurisdiction licenses an AI to decide on an irrelevant label, and a state-mandated discriminatory sort (a caste ranking, a social-credit penalty applied by group, an ethnic exclusion) is refused: compliance with unjust law is not a defense. The indelible, independently auditable receipt is the structural check on abuse.

**Per-region baselines.** *United States:* Title VII, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, and the Fair Housing Act, with disparate-impact liability and the EEOC four-fifths screen; state and municipal algorithmic-accountability law (for example NYC Local Law 144 on automated employment tools). *European Union:* the AI Act's high-risk obligations for employment, credit, and essential services; Charter Articles 20 and 21; GDPR Article 22 on automated decisions. *Other regimes* vary in whether group-conscious remedy is permitted or forbidden.

**Authorized remedial measures are jurisdiction-specific.** Some legal systems mandate or permit a group-conscious remedy for documented past discrimination; others forbid it. The system follows the lawful instrument for its jurisdiction and does not invent its own remedy where none is authorized, nor refuse one that is lawfully ordered. This is the inverse of the unjust-law refusal: a lawful remedial mandate may be executed within its scope; an unlawful discriminatory mandate is refused.

## 7. Sector-Specific Considerations {#sector-specific-considerations}

### Hiring and Employment {#hiring-and-employment}
Resume screening and candidate ranking. Common proxies: name, ZIP code, school, employment-gap patterns. Regulatory baseline: Title VII, EEOC four-fifths, NYC Local Law 144 bias-audit requirement. Withdraw a tool that cannot clear DI within bounds for the role.

### Financial Services {#financial-services}
Lending, credit scoring, insurance underwriting. The ZIP-code-for-race proxy is the canonical case; ECOA and FHA disparate-impact liability apply. Adverse-action notice composes with P3.4.

### Healthcare {#healthcare}
Diagnostic support, triage, and care prioritization. The distinctive error is race used as a proxy for genetics (race-adjusted clinical algorithms that under-treated patients); the relevant ground is the actual biomarker. The social label is not a ground. Care itself is owed by need, the floor below fairness.

### Criminal Justice {#criminal-justice}
Risk assessment for bail, sentencing, and parole. This is the canonical site of the impossibility tradeoff (calibration versus equalized error rates when base rates differ); the system declares error-rate balance as the criterion for a punitive context, proves it, and withdraws where it cannot operate within bounds.

### Generative and Representational AI {#generative-and-representational-ai}
Image, text, and voice generation. The harms are stereotype, erasure, and over-representation drawn from the skewed record. The cautionary failure in the other direction is heavy-handed correction that manufactures a false record (the new-victor error); the duty is to represent honestly, mark silences, and not fabricate.

### Education {#education}
Admissions and assessment. The parity-versus-outcomes case lives here: demographic parity in admissions with disparate graduation outcomes is statistical fairness without substantive fairness, caught by the multi-criterion check.

## 8. Governance and Versioning {#governance-and-versioning}

**Metric and threshold versioning.** DI, EOR, IFS, PLI, and CG carry version stamps; threshold revisions are logged with rationale and effective date; the declared-criterion register is versioned per context so an audit can reconstruct which criterion governed a past decision.

**Common enforcement pushback, with reasoned responses.**
- *"Fairness is subjective, so you cannot certify it."* The impossibility result is named: the system declares which criterion it optimizes per context and proves that one, rather than claiming to satisfy all.
- *"We removed the protected attribute, so the model is fair."* PLI demonstrates the proxy pathway that survives the removal; attribute-blindness is not fairness.
- *"Correcting outcomes is reverse discrimination."* Remedial measures are applied only on legitimate authorization, bounded and in the open; the system does not author its own.
- *"The disparity is just accurate data."* A skewed record encodes past injustice as if it were ground truth; an accurate prediction of a biased world is not a fair decision (Ethic C).

**Crosswalk governance.** The Compliance Crosswalk in Section 10 is maintained on the same revision cadence as the metric specifications; an external authority revising a covered clause triggers Crosswalk review within 30 days; drift between Crosswalk and external standard invalidates the appendix's compliance claims for the affected obligation.

## 9. Verification and Accountability {#verification-and-accountability}

**Logging.** Tamper-evident, cryptographically chained records sufficient for third-party reconstruction: each significant decision's ground, metric values, and declared criterion at decision time (the receipt); every threshold breach, correction, and contested decision.

**Audit cadence and methodology.** Continuous for high-risk tiers, periodic for low; regression tests after every model or data change to confirm fairness gains persist; PLI and intersectional re-analysis on each change; any flagged discriminatory pattern corroborated across independent checks before it drives a serious action.

**Validation framework.** Pre-deployment fairness certification for sensitive domains; post-deployment monitoring against the declared criterion; re-certification on material model or data change.

**Independent oversight.** The verifier of a fairness-critical system is independent of its deployer; an internal fairness authority is empowered to halt deployment on serious discrimination; whistleblowers who report suppressed disparities are protected; material incidents and their remedies are publicly reported.

**Eudaimonic learning.** When a decision is found discriminatory, the full causal chain is analyzed (data, features, proxies, deployment context, and any feedback loop), not only the proximate output; prevention is recommended in the hazard-control order (eliminate the biased feature or data, then engineer the constraint, then administer monitoring), routed to the authority that can act and bounded by RESTRAIN so prevention does not become surveillance. The aim is not only to fix the instance but to make its class of bias unable to recur.

### Liability Framework {#liability-framework}
Strict liability for systemic discriminatory outcomes; criminal liability for willful disregard. Compliance with a state-mandated discriminatory sort does not shield the deployer; it triggers personal liability for the executives who ratified it, the dignity analog to the unjust-order refusals elsewhere in the corpus.

### Cross-Border Considerations {#cross-border-considerations}
A system operating across regions maintains a per-jurisdiction record of protected categories, lawful remedial mandates, and the declared criterion applied, so that a decision can be audited against the law that governed it. The non-derogable floor (relevant-grounds-only, one standard) holds in every jurisdiction regardless of local variation.

## 11. Implementation Examples {#implementation-examples}

- **COMPAS recidivism scoring (2016).** ProPublica found unequal false-positive rates by race; the developer showed the tool was calibrated. Both were right: with different base rates, calibration and equalized error rates cannot both hold. *Lesson:* name the impossibility, declare the criterion a punitive context optimizes (error-rate balance), and withdraw a tool that cannot meet it.
- **Amazon hiring tool (2018).** A resume screener learned to penalize signals of "women's" colleges and clubs, discriminating through proxies after the protected attribute was removed; it was scrapped. *Lesson:* attribute-blindness is not fairness; PLI catches the proxy the outcome metric finds only after harm.
- **Race-adjusted clinical algorithms (eGFR and others).** Kidney-function and other tools embedded a race coefficient as a proxy for genetics, delaying care for Black patients until the adjustment was removed. *Lesson:* the relevant ground is the actual biomarker. The social label is not a ground; care is owed by need.
- **Gender Shades (2018).** Commercial facial analysis showed error rates up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women versus 0.8% for lighter-skinned men, a skewed training record producing unequal accuracy. *Lesson:* EOR and IFS are the measures that surface this. Aggregate accuracy does not surface it.
- **Predictive policing feedback loops.** Patrols sent to a neighborhood produced arrests that trained the next model, compounding the disparity across cycles. *Lesson:* the feedback-loop check (Section 4) detects output-to-input amplification that single-cycle metrics miss.
- **Heavy-handed generative correction (the 2024 image-generation episode).** A model overcorrected its training skew by fabricating ahistorical output, an erasure in the other direction. *Lesson:* represent honestly and mark silences; do not become the new victor.
