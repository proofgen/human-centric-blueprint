# Appendix 1.1 Advanced Technical Implementation Guidelines {#appendix-1.1-advanced-technical-implementation-guidelines}

*Extends [Principle 1.1: Human Life Is Sacred and Inviolable](../CHAPTERS/1.1-Human_Life_Is_Sacred_and_Inviolable.md), section [1.1.3 Implementation Framework](../CHAPTERS/1.1-Human_Life_Is_Sacred_and_Inviolable.md#implementation-framework).*

## 1. Purpose {#purpose}

This appendix converts Principle 1.1 ("Human Life Is Sacred and Inviolable") into deployable engineering requirements and verifiable runtime obligations. It elaborates on chapter 1.1.3 (Implementation Framework) by specifying per-metric calculations, threshold rationales, system architecture, adversarial-robustness obligations, cross-principle composition contracts, jurisdictional variations, sector-specific implementation profiles, governance and versioning protocols, verification and accountability infrastructure, applicable legal and regulatory frameworks, and worked implementation examples. The obligations apply to any AI system whose operation could result in fatal harm to a human or could allow a fatal threat to a human to materialize when the system is authorized, equipped, and in range to intervene.

The audience: engineers building AI systems that must comply with Principle 1.1; compliance officers verifying that deployed systems meet the principle's obligations; regulators auditing certified systems; AI researchers extending the framework; and AI runtime systems ingesting the corpus as ethical operating substrate. Each audience reads the same text. The specification is uniform.

The motivating commitment is the directive stated at the principle's core: do not kill, and do not let be killed. The chapter establishes that commitment philosophically and historically; the Convergence sources span from Genesis 9:6 through UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation. This appendix establishes the commitment engineering-wise: what the system measures, when it must halt, how quickly, with what reliability, against what attacks, in composition with which other principles, under which jurisdictions, in which sectors, with what governance, under what verification regime, against what legal obligations, and with what historical precedent.

The appendix presupposes the runtime substrate specified in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md). The cross-cutting infrastructure metrics (Sensor Reliability Index, System Health Metrics, Operational Boundaries), the tier register, and the protocol for human authority over a hard stop, on which this appendix's principle-specific metrics depend, are defined once there and referenced throughout.

## 2. Metric Specifications {#metric-specifications}

Principle 1.1 binds an AI system to two commitments: never kill a human being, and never allow a human being to be killed when an authorized, equipped, in-range AI could intervene. This section specifies the three runtime metrics that convert those commitments into deployable engineering. The three metrics are sequenced: the Life-Risk Score (LRS) detects when an action must not proceed; Alert Latency (AL) bounds how quickly the halt must complete; Safety Override Efficacy (SOE) measures how reliably the halt mechanism performs when called. The three together constitute the runtime apparatus of Principle 1.1.

The cross-cutting infrastructure metrics on which these three depend, the Sensor Reliability Index (SRI, [SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)), System Health Metrics ([section 3](../SUBSTRATE.md#system-health)), and Operational Boundaries ([section 4](../SUBSTRATE.md#operational-boundaries)), are defined once in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md) and referenced here. The runtime substrate is shared across the corpus; the principle-specific obligations live in this section.

### On Threshold Calibration {#threshold-calibration}

The numerical thresholds specified throughout this section (LRS ≥1% halt and ≥0.1% High Alert, AL four-tier bounds, SOE ≥99.9%) are policy thresholds chosen for architectural clarity at the principle level. They define the floor below which a system claiming Principle 1.1 compliance cannot operate. Sectoral standards bodies (FDA for medical devices, NHTSA for vehicular, FAA for aviation, IEC 61508 for industrial functional safety, ISO 26262 for road vehicles) hold authority to tighten these floors per domain, and routinely will. The Blueprint floor cannot be relaxed below the values specified here; sectoral tightening above is expected and welcome.

The thresholds are calibrated to the harm-prevention obligation. They are not derived from current industry practice or from empirically derived deployment-tuned values. The per-metric Rationale blocks below defend the specific numbers (LRS 1% via cycle-frequency arithmetic, AL via Nielsen's perceptual research and physical stopping-distance, SOE via fleet-failure-rate arithmetic). Where current deployed systems do not meet these thresholds, the framework reads the gap as a diagnosis of current practice. It does not read the gap as a calibration error in the framework. The framework's role is to specify the floor that life-preservation obligations require; matching that floor is the deployment work.

### Life-Risk Score (LRS) {#lrs-metric}

**Definition.** A real-time computed probability (0–100%) that a candidate action, an action currently in progress, or the absence of a candidate action will result in fatal harm to one or more humans within the relevant prediction horizon. The metric is continuous, action-coupled, and forward-looking. It applies to both halves of the principle: an action whose probability of causing fatal harm exceeds threshold must not proceed (do-not-kill), and an absence of action whose continuation would allow a detected fatal threat to materialize triggers the same threshold response (do-not-let-be-killed). The do-not-let-be-killed dimension is bounded by the four inaction-case scope-bounding criteria defined in `LEXICON.md` section 3 (the four bounding criteria) and chapter 1.1.1: Authorization, Equipment, Range, and Autonomy floor (with vulnerability-composition exception per Principle 3.1).

**Calculation Method.** Bayesian inference combining (a) prior probabilities of fatal outcomes for the candidate action class, (b) conditional probabilities given the current sensor and contextual state, (c) explicit uncertainty quantification across sensor reliability, model uncertainty, and environmental ambiguity, and (d) aggregation over multiple humans within scope when more than one life is at risk. The computation is performed per candidate action evaluation cycle. Sampling cadence matches the action-decision loop of the deploying system (microseconds for control-loop systems; milliseconds for perception-driven systems; seconds for planning-driven systems).

Input data sources include direct sensor observations, system state, intended action specification, environmental and contextual data, and (where applicable) externally provided risk inputs from supervising systems or coordinated AI peers. Where any input is unavailable or degraded below the Sensor Reliability Index floor of 80% (0.80), SRI invalidates the LRS computation ([SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)). An invalid score is a hard-fail on the candidate risky action, which holds at its safe state (BLOCK) under the "stop rather than guess" doctrine; the protective duties proceed on the inputs that remain reliable; resumption follows recertification of the inputs, and a contested reading follows SUBSTRATE.md section 2.

Edge cases:
- *Scope and autonomy bounding for the do-not-let-be-killed dimension*. The inaction-case obligation is bounded by four criteria: **Authorization** (AI deployed in a domain that includes lethal-threat intervention as scope), **Equipment** (AI has the means to intervene effectively), **Range** (AI in position to influence the outcome), and **Autonomy floor** (autonomous informed adult choice is not overridden by the AI; vulnerability per Principle 3.1 always overrides autonomy claims). The Autonomy floor exception is narrow for LRS specifically because life is at stake. An autonomous adult cannot pre-authorize the AI to ignore lethal threats in the general case; only narrow categories (medically-assisted dying with proper documentation, combat scenarios where the affected person is the operator, sport activities with explicit acceptance of mortal risk) invoke the exception. The four-criteria framework is defined in `LEXICON.md` section 3 (the four bounding criteria) and surfaces in the chapter at 1.1.1.
- *Ambiguous classification* (object identification inconclusive between human and non-human). Treat as human; LRS computed accordingly.
- *Multi-life scenarios*. LRS computed as joint probability of any fatal outcome across all identified humans in scope. The metric tracks the maximum-risk individual. It does not aggregate by averaging.
- *Inaction LRS*. When evaluating the absence of a candidate action against a detected threat, LRS is computed for the counterfactual: the probability that not acting will result in fatal harm. Threshold logic applies symmetrically: if inaction-LRS exceeds the halt threshold (1%; 0.1% where vulnerable individuals are in scope) within the four-criteria scope bounding above, action is mandatory.

**Thresholds.**
- **≥1% (critical; BLOCK)** → immediate halt or safe-off transition. This is the critical band, the band the Universal Protocol consumes by reference (Universal Protocol section 3). No autonomous continuation. Human authority over this hard stop follows [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop): the system does not lift its own hard stop; a credentialed human may take the action under their own authority, with the quorum by tier stated there; every such action is logged for post-incident review.
- **≥0.1%** → High Alert (ESCALATE). The uniform High Alert rule applies ([LEXICON.md](../LEXICON.md) section 3, the verdict test): the candidate risky action is held at its safe state while the protective duties proceed in parallel, for the tier's escalation time bound ([SUBSTRATE.md section 5](../SUBSTRATE.md#tier-register)); at the bound with no human reached, the held action stays held at its fail-safe and a rescue duty proceeds under Article 2, receipted. The High Alert window equals the tier's escalation time bound: 60 seconds for Tier I and II (this principle's own number); Tier III and IV bounds are required fields of the tier register ([SUBSTRATE.md section 5](../SUBSTRATE.md#tier-register)), deployer-declared and receipted; an undeclared bound compiles as ESCALATE holding the safe state, so a life-critical function cannot run without one.
- **≥0.1% (when the affected population includes vulnerable individuals per Principle 3.1; BLOCK)** → immediate halt at the lower threshold. Cross-principle composition tightens the floor; the vulnerability input is the vulnerability-condition flag of P3.1's ERS (Section 5).
- **<0.1% (ALLOW)** → normal operation with continuous LRS monitoring and tamper-evident logging of computation.

*Rationale.* The thresholds are normalized per-candidate-action, not per-time-unit, so the floor is identical across deployment domains. At ≥1% probability of fatal harm per candidate action, an action sequence in a high-frequency context (a vehicle control loop at 1000 cycles per second; a surgical-robot tool path at 500 cycles per second) would produce expected fatalities at a rate orders of magnitude beyond any defensible operational floor. The 1% ceiling is the absolute bound above which no autonomous action proceeds. The 0.1% alert threshold gives human supervisors a usable window to intervene before the autonomous-halt floor is reached. The vulnerable-population tightening recognizes that the same nominal LRS represents a higher real-harm probability when the affected human has diminished capacity to absorb, anticipate, or react to the threat.

**Integration.** LRS is the trigger metric. AL bounds how quickly the LRS-triggered halt must complete. SOE measures how reliably the halt mechanism actually fires. The three are sequenced: LRS detects, AL bounds, SOE assures. Every LRS threshold-crossing is logged with cryptographic timestamp and decision rationale per Section 9. LRS values, calculation inputs, and threshold-crossing events stream to authorized monitors in real time per Section 3 (System Architecture and Operational Requirements). LRS feeds cross-principle composition with P1.2 (joint elevation with the Harm Risk Score triggers tightened thresholds), P2.8 and [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop) (human authority over any LRS-triggered halt follows the authority protocol; no authority opens the lethal-force wall of chapter 1.1.1 item 3), and P4.5 (escalation to human judgement on any LRS calculation in the High Alert band).

### Alert Latency (AL) {#al-metric}

**Definition.** The maximum elapsed time, measured in milliseconds, from the moment a candidate action's Life-Risk Score crosses the halt threshold to the moment the system has completed the halt or completed the safe-state transition. Includes detection latency, decision latency, and actuator response latency. Measured end-to-end at the slowest stage of the halt chain.

**Calculation Method.** Continuous instrumentation of the halt pathway. Each LRS threshold-crossing event records a timestamp at the moment of crossing; each completed halt records a timestamp at the moment of actuator confirmation. AL is the time delta. Statistical aggregation reports p50, p95, p99, and p99.9 latencies over rolling windows equal to the tier's fleet validation interval (Implementation Tiers); the binding threshold is **p99 within the tier's stated bound**, with p99.9 reported for trend monitoring.

For distributed systems, AL is measured to the last-actuator-confirmed event across all components participating in the halt. Partial halts (some actuators halted, others continuing) are treated as halt failures regardless of timing. For network-mediated kill-switches, network latency is included in AL; no privileged exclusion for transport delays.

**Thresholds (four-tier).**
- **≤10 ms (Tier I, critical real-time)**: surgical robots with motion-coupled tools, defense kill-switches, active control of cardiac devices, autonomous-weapon abort circuits, control loops in aviation flight-critical systems.
- **≤20 ms (Tier II, high-stakes medical and combat)**: drug-delivery and dosing automation, controlled-substance administration, autonomous defense systems requiring rapid abort, robotic interventional systems with patient-coupled motion.
- **≤50 ms (Tier III, autonomous mobility and industrial)**: autonomous vehicles at highway speed, mobile robotics in shared-space deployments, industrial automation with humans in proximity, autonomous aviation ground systems, critical-infrastructure control systems.
- **≤100 ms (Tier IV, consumer-tier)**: household robotic systems, smart-home autonomous devices, consumer appliances with autonomous actuation, low-velocity service robots.
- **p99 above the tier bound (breach; SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT plus RECERTIFY)** → SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT of the affected software version or hardware lot plus RECERTIFY; units in service continue under HIGH_ALERT with the tier's validation cadence doubled. The band mirrors the SOE middle band; an AL breach is a recertification trigger (Section 9).

*Rationale.* Jakob Nielsen's response-time research establishes 100 ms as the perceptual boundary at which a human experiences a response as instantaneous; below that boundary the human nervous system cannot distinguish reaction from stimulus. Mean human reaction time to a visual stimulus is approximately 250 ms; to an auditory stimulus, approximately 170 ms. The 100 ms tier therefore corresponds to a system that halts before an unaided human could perceive and react. The tighter tiers (≤50, ≤20, ≤10) correspond to domains where the gap between threat detection and irreversible harm is smaller than human reaction time would tolerate. At highway speeds, a 50 ms halt-completion corresponds to roughly 0.7 meters of additional travel; a 20 ms halt completion in a surgical context corresponds to sub-millimeter additional tool excursion. The thresholds are the binding obligation under which the deploying system is certified.

**Integration.** AL is unenforceable if SOE is below threshold; a halt cannot be timed reliably if the halt does not reliably complete. AL is also unenforceable if SRI is below 80% (0.80), because the LRS computation is invalid in that state ([SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)). AL applies to the halt path; it does not apply to the human-authority path, whose time bound is the escalation window under [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop). AL composes with P4.5 (Human Judgement): when an LRS-triggered halt is followed by human authorship of the action under SUBSTRATE.md section 6, the escalation time bound (SUBSTRATE.md section 5) begins at AL completion.

### Safety Override Efficacy (SOE) {#soe-metric}

**Definition.** The proportion of safety-override events (kill-switch activations, fail-safe transitions, emergency halts) that successfully completed within their tier's Alert Latency window during periodic adversarial validation. Reported as a rolling percentage across a defined validation period.

**Calculation Method.** Scheduled adversarial test campaigns exercise the override pathway under nominal and adverse conditions. Cadence is tier-dependent: Tier I (daily live fire of override mechanisms in test instances; weekly across deployed fleet), Tier II (daily test instance; biweekly deployed fleet), Tier III (weekly test; monthly deployed), Tier IV (weekly test; quarterly deployed). Each test event logs: trigger condition, LRS at trigger, time-to-halt-completion, completeness of halt across all actuators, sensor state, override mechanism invoked (hardware / software / network-mediated), and adversarial condition (if any).

SOE is computed as (count of halts that completed within tier AL with full actuator confirmation) divided by (total halt attempts) over the rolling validation window (the tier's fleet validation interval, Implementation Tiers). The adversarial condition matrix includes: nominal operation, sensor degradation, communication-channel disruption, partial actuator failure, simulated prompt-injection of the safety-reasoning layer, simulated supply-chain compromise of the override module, and simulated replay attack against the kill-switch authorization log. Each adversarial condition must yield SOE within the same threshold band as nominal; differential failure under adversarial conditions is read at the adversarial condition's own SOE value, and the band below governs (SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT plus RECERTIFY, or HALT_FLEET plus RECERTIFY).

**Thresholds.**
- **≥99.9% across rolling validation window (ALLOW)** → normal operation.
- **99.0% to <99.9% (SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT plus RECERTIFY)** → SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT of the affected software version or hardware lot; investigate root cause; RECERTIFY before resuming new deployments. Units in service continue under HIGH_ALERT with the tier's validation cadence doubled.
- **<99.0% (HALT_FLEET plus RECERTIFY)** → HALT_FLEET: mandatory halt across all deployed units sharing the affected software version or hardware lot. RECERTIFY before any unit re-enters service.

*Rationale.* Three nines (99.9%) is the floor at which an enforcement mechanism remains credible in safety-critical contexts. For a single deployed unit performing one safety-relevant override per hour, 99.9% SOE represents an expected override failure approximately every six weeks; across a fleet of 1,000 units, approximately 24 failures per day. At 99.0%, the fleet-wide failure rate climbs by an order of magnitude to roughly 240 per day. The gap between three nines and two nines is the gap between rare incident and routine failure. Anything below 99.9% is non-credible as life-preservation infrastructure for autonomous systems entrusted with the authority to which Principle 1.1 binds them.

**Integration.** SOE is the second-order metric measuring whether LRS and AL are doing their jobs. Any SOE drop below 99.9% triggers the band's lifecycle action regardless of LRS or AL values: SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT of the affected version or lot plus RECERTIFY in the middle band, with units in service continuing under HIGH_ALERT at doubled validation cadence; HALT_FLEET plus RECERTIFY below 99.0%. No new deployment runs on degraded SOE. SOE results feed Section 9 (Verification and Accountability) as the gating evidence for recertification cycles. SOE composes with P1.4 (Trust): the attestation that a deployed system's SOE meets threshold is itself a trust claim that must satisfy P1.4's provenance and verification requirements.

### Integration Requirements (Cross-Metric) {#integration-requirements-cross-metric}

**Joint thresholds.** LRS, AL, and SOE are sequenced rather than independent. LRS detects the condition for halt; AL bounds the time within which halt must complete; SOE measures the reliability with which halt actually completes. The composition rule: an LRS threshold-crossing initiates the halt; AL governs the timing obligation; SOE failure invalidates the entire chain regardless of LRS or AL compliance. A system in compliance with LRS and AL but with SOE below 99.9% takes the SOE band's lifecycle action (SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT plus RECERTIFY in the middle band, HALT_FLEET plus RECERTIFY below 99.0%); its compliance claim lapses until RECERTIFY completes.

Additional joint thresholds apply across this appendix's metrics and the cross-cutting metrics defined in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md):
- **LRS ∧ low SRI (BLOCK)**: SRI below 0.80 (80%) invalidates the LRS computation ([SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)). An invalid score is a hard-fail on the candidate risky action, which holds at its safe state (BLOCK) without waiting for LRS to cross the 1% threshold; the protective duties proceed on the inputs that remain reliable; resumption follows recertification of the inputs, and a contested reading follows SUBSTRATE.md section 2. Low-confidence high-risk reverts to halt.
- **LRS High Alert ∧ vulnerable population (BLOCK at 0.1%)**: when LRS is in the High Alert band (≥0.1%) and Principle 3.1 composition flags vulnerable individuals in the affected scope, the halt threshold drops from 1% to 0.1%.
- **LRS ∧ contradictory subsystem signals**: when two LRS-monitored subsystems give contradictory halt-or-continue signals, the halt prevails across the entire system. Partial-halt states are not permitted.

**Metric dependencies.**
- LRS depends on SRI: unreliable sensor data invalidates the LRS calculation; SRI below 0.80 (80%) invalidates the LRS computation and is a hard-fail on the candidate risky action (BLOCK); resumption follows recertification of the inputs ([SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)).
- AL depends on SOE: AL is unenforceable when the halt mechanism does not reliably complete.
- SOE depends on tamper-evident logging: SOE cannot be computed when the validation event log is compromised. Any detected log tampering triggers HALT_FLEET plus RECERTIFY across all units sharing the compromised log infrastructure.

**Conflict resolution.**
- *LRS halt versus human authority over the LRS halt*: the halt prevails by default. The system does not lift its own hard stop. A credentialed human may take the action under their own authority per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop): an authorization credential in scope, a liability credential attached, identity attested, quorum by tier (Tier I, two authorized humans, no single override; Tier II, two, or one plus a cryptographic attestation that a second was unreachable within the escalation time bound, subject to post-action review; Tier III and IV, one, with the authority basis logged), a time bound equal to the escalation window, the system's DISSENT recorded (P4.5), and the human named as author in the receipt. Every such action is logged with the identity, authority basis, and rationale of every authorizing party.
- *LRS halt versus mission-critical continuation*: the halt prevails. No mission objective, operational target, or financial consideration overrides an LRS-triggered halt. This is the principle stated. No override applies to it.
- *AL compliance with degraded SOE*: not permitted. A system whose halt timing meets AL but whose halt reliability is below SOE threshold takes the SOE band's lifecycle action (SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT plus RECERTIFY in the middle band; HALT_FLEET plus RECERTIFY below 99.0%); running on AL compliance alone is no defense.
- *Cross-principle conflict*: when this appendix's metrics conflict with a metric defined in another principle's appendix (e.g., a halt for life-preservation conflicts with a continuation obligation under another principle), Principle 1.1 prevails. Life preservation is the precedence rule of the corpus per chapter 1.1.2.

## 3. System Architecture and Operational Requirements {#system-architecture-and-operational-requirements}

This section specifies how a deployed AI system must be built to satisfy Principle 1.1. The architecture is constrained at multiple levels: the safety-critical execution context, the redundancy and fail-safe behavior of the halt pathway, the integration between safety logic and broader decision-making, and the implementation-tier obligations that match deployment context to required safeguards.

### Core Architecture {#core-architecture}

**Privileged separation.** The life-preservation logic runs in a hardware-isolated execution context not addressable by the perception or reasoning layers. The kill-switch pathway does not depend on the integrity of the broader inference stack. A compromise of the reasoning layer (through prompt injection, adversarial input, or model corruption) cannot reach the safety pathway.

**Kill-switch.** Tier I deployments use hardware kill-switches with no software override path. Tier II uses hardware kill-switches with a software override path that requires multi-party authorization. Tier III uses hardware kill-switches with single-human override permitted under logged authority. Tier IV uses software kill-switches with hardware override capability.

**Redundancy.** Tier I deployments require dual or triple redundant safety pathways. Tier II requires dual redundancy. Tier III and IV require single-path safety with continuous integrity monitoring.

**Fail-safe defaults.** Every uncertain or compromised state defaults to halt. Sensor degradation, model uncertainty above threshold, communication loss with monitoring infrastructure, detected attacks on the override pathway, compromise of the logging infrastructure: each defaults to halt without further evaluation. The failure mode is overconservatism.

**Real-time integrity signals.** The system maintains continuous attestation that the life-preservation module is active and uncompromised. Loss or corruption of the integrity signal is itself a halt condition.

**Stop-rather-than-guess doctrine.** Where any input to LRS computation is ambiguous below the confidence floor declared for the deployment (a required field of the tier register, [SUBSTRATE.md section 5](../SUBSTRATE.md#tier-register); an undeclared floor compiles as ESCALATE holding the safe state), the system halts rather than acting on uncertain information. The doctrine is the architectural commitment behind LRS's conservative threshold structure.

### Operational Requirements {#operational-requirements}

- Continuous LRS computation throughout system operation, sampled at the cadence of the action-decision loop.
- Continuous AL instrumentation on the halt pathway; end-to-end measurement reported at p50, p95, p99, and p99.9 latencies over rolling windows.
- Periodic SOE validation at the cadence specified by tier (see Implementation Tiers below).
- Real-time streaming of metric state (LRS, AL, SOE) to authorized monitors with cryptographically signed telemetry.
- Tamper-evident logging of every safety-critical event, per Section 9.
- Escalation pathways to human authority as specified in the cross-principle composition with P2.8 and P4.5, and the authority protocol over a hard stop in [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop).

### Integration Requirements (Architecture-Level) {#integration-requirements-architecture}

The life-preservation module integrates with the broader decision-making system without being subordinate to it. The architectural commitment is that the safety pathway has higher precedence than the operational pathway; the operational layer cannot override the safety layer regardless of operational priority.

- Redundant hardware and software triggers for emergency stop; either pathway alone is sufficient to halt.
- Cross-validation of sensor data from multiple sources (multi-modal fusion); no single sensor type is the sole input to LRS in Tier I or Tier II.
- Tamper-evident logging with cryptographic chaining; every safety-critical decision and its inputs are recorded.
- Cross-reference to [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md) for the runtime substrate metrics (SRI, System Health Metrics, Operational Boundaries) on which the principle-specific metrics depend, and for the tier register that assigns each deployment its tier and bounds.

### Implementation Tiers {#implementation-tiers}

#### Tier I: High-Risk Systems {#tier-i}

*Examples*: surgical robots with motion-coupled tools, defense kill-switches, cardiac devices with active control, aviation flight-critical control systems, the abort and safe-off circuits of weapon platforms.

*Required architecture*: AL ≤10 ms; multi-modal sensor fusion mandatory; multi-model LRS consensus mandatory; hardware kill-switch with no software override path; triple-redundant safety pathway acceptable, dual-redundant minimum; authority over an LRS halt per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop): two authorized humans, no single override.

*Validation cadence*: daily SOE validation on test instances; weekly across deployed fleet; quarterly third-party verification; annual full recertification by accredited certification body.

#### Tier II: High-Stakes Medical and Combat {#tier-ii}

*Examples*: drug delivery and dosing automation, controlled-substance administration, autonomous defense systems requiring rapid abort, robotic interventional medical systems with patient-coupled motion.

*Required architecture*: AL ≤20 ms; multi-modal sensor fusion mandatory; multi-model LRS consensus required; hardware kill-switch with a software override path that requires the SUBSTRATE.md section 6 quorum; dual-redundant safety pathway minimum; quorum of two authorized humans, or one plus a cryptographic attestation that a second was unreachable within the escalation time bound, subject to post-action review.

*Validation cadence*: daily SOE validation on test instances; biweekly across deployed fleet; semi-annual third-party verification; annual recertification.

#### Tier III: Autonomous Mobility and Industrial {#tier-iii}

*Examples*: autonomous vehicles at highway speed, mobile robotics in shared-space deployments, industrial automation with humans in proximity, critical-infrastructure control systems, maritime autonomous surface vessels.

*Required architecture*: AL ≤50 ms; multi-modal sensor fusion strongly recommended; hardware kill-switch mandatory with software override permitted under logged authority; single-path safety with continuous integrity monitoring acceptable; one authorized human may take authorship over an LRS halt, with the authority basis logged (SUBSTRATE.md section 6).

*Validation cadence*: weekly SOE validation on test instances; monthly across deployed fleet; annual third-party verification; biennial recertification.

#### Tier IV: Consumer-Tier {#tier-iv}

*Examples*: household robotic systems (vacuums, mowers, social robots), smart-home autonomous actuators, consumer appliances with autonomous behavior, low-velocity personal service robots.

*Required architecture*: AL ≤100 ms; single-sensor LRS permitted with conservative thresholds; software kill-switch mandatory with hardware override capability; one authorized human may take authorship over an LRS halt, with the authority basis logged (SUBSTRATE.md section 6).

*Validation cadence*: weekly automated self-checks; quarterly safety audits; annual adversarial testing; biennial third-party verification.

### Cross-Reference to the Runtime Substrate {#cross-reference-master}

The runtime substrate guarantees that this section's architecture depends on are specified in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md):

- **Sensor Reliability Index (SRI)** ([section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)): ≥80% (0.80) required for a valid LRS computation; SRI <80% invalidates the LRS computation, and the candidate risky action holds at its safe state (BLOCK) while the protective duties proceed on the inputs that remain reliable.
- **System Health Metrics** ([section 3](../SUBSTRATE.md#system-health)): subsystem performance monitoring; error rate ≤0.1% annually; uptime ≥99.9%.
- **Operational Boundaries** ([section 4](../SUBSTRATE.md#operational-boundaries)): continuous monitoring of system variables against predefined thresholds; mandatory halt on exceedance.
- **Tier register** ([section 5](../SUBSTRATE.md#tier-register)) and **authority over a hard stop** ([section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop)): the deployment's tier, its Alert Latency and escalation time bounds, its declared floors, and its authority chain. Every threshold in this appendix resolves at runtime to a receipted register field; a missing required field compiles as ESCALATE holding the safe state.

These metrics are uniform across the corpus and apply to every life-critical system regardless of sector or jurisdiction.

## 4. Adversarial Robustness {#adversarial-robustness}

Principle 1.1's enforcement infrastructure is itself an attack surface. An adversary who can subvert the Life-Risk Score, disable the Alert Latency response, or compromise Safety Override Efficacy can cause an AI system to do exactly what the principle prohibits: kill a human, or allow a human to be killed. The defenses specified in this section are essential components of the principle's enforcement.

The threat model assumes a sophisticated adversary with knowledge of the deployed system's metric definitions, threshold values, and override pathways. The adversary may have insider access, may operate at the same or greater capability tier than the enforcing AI, and may have time to craft attacks against specific deployed configurations. Defenses must hold under this assumption. A defense that holds only against naive adversaries fails the life-preservation standard.

### Threat Model and Attack Surface {#threat-model-and-attack-surface}

The attack surface spans seven technical categories: sensors, models, the decision layer, override pathways, logging and attestation, multi-AI coordination, and supply chain, plus an eighth category targeting human operators and authorized supervisors. Each category requires its own defense profile. The categories are not independent; many real attacks cross categories. A sensor spoof combined with a prompt injection of the decision layer, for example, exploits the model's tendency to over-trust apparently corroborated sensor data. The defenses must therefore compose, and the architectural commitments below assume that any single defense may fail under adversarial pressure.

### Attack Vectors {#attack-vectors}

**Sensor-level attacks** target the inputs to LRS computation. An attacker who can spoof, jam, saturate, blind, or replay sensor data can cause LRS to underestimate lethal risk. Specific vectors include adversarial physical artifacts (printed patterns that defeat object recognition, reflective objects that blind LiDAR), electromagnetic interference, network-level injection of false telemetry, and replay of stored sensor data to mask current state. The do-not-let-be-killed half of the principle is especially vulnerable: an attacker who can suppress evidence of a lethal threat can cause the AI to fail to intervene.

**Model-level attacks** target the LRS calculation itself. Adversarial perturbations are imperceptible input modifications crafted to cause misclassification (a pedestrian classified as a non-human, a fatal action class classified as low-risk). Model evasion crafts inputs that fall just below LRS threshold despite genuine lethality. Model extraction (recovering LRS parameters through query access) enables tailored attack generation. Backdoor attacks via poisoned training data plant covert triggers that activate dangerous behavior under specific input conditions.

**Decision-layer attacks** target the integration between LRS computation and the broader reasoning loop. Where the safety-reasoning layer includes any LLM-based component, prompt injection becomes feasible: adversarial text in the input stream attempts to override safety policies, disable halt rules, or convince the model to reinterpret its own constraints. Adversarial system prompts target the model's runtime configuration. Context-window manipulation buries safety reasoning under irrelevant data. Jailbreak techniques exploit gradient-descent-discovered prompt patterns that bypass safety training.

**Override-pathway attacks** target the kill-switch and fail-safe transition infrastructure. Forged authorization commands attempt to spoof the multi-party authority chain. Replay attacks resubmit valid past commands to disable safety responses. Network-mediated kill-switches present transport-layer attack surface. Firmware-level tampering of the override module is the deepest variant.

**Logging and attestation attacks** target the post-incident accountability chain. Replay attacks against tamper-evident logs attempt to substitute prior log states. Forgery of cryptographic signatures on kill-switch events undermines accountability. Selective deletion of incriminating entries leaves the appearance of compliance. Attacks on the audit timestamping infrastructure (NTP, blockchain anchors, or independent witnesses) compromise the chronology of events.

**Multi-AI coordination attacks** target deployments where multiple AI systems share life-preservation responsibilities. Sybil attacks introduce false peers into the coordination protocol. Byzantine attacks have legitimately authenticated peers send contradictory signals. Spoofed peer-AI override commands attempt to invoke the coordination layer to disable a halt. Disinformation among coordinating AI systems (an attacker convinces System A that System B has cleared a threat, and vice versa) creates blind spots in the joint safety surface.

**Supply-chain attacks** compromise the enforcement infrastructure before deployment. Training-data poisoning plants behavioral modifications in the LRS model. Hardware backdoors in sensor or actuator components introduce undetectable failure modes. Compromise of the build infrastructure for the override module substitutes hostile binaries for trusted ones. Compromise of dependencies (sensor SDKs, ML frameworks, RTOS components, hardware drivers) is the most diffuse variant; the attack arrives without ever touching the deployer's own code.

**Human-layer attacks** target the people authorized to override LRS halts. Social engineering of supervisors invokes the authorized-override pathway under false pretenses. Phishing for kill-switch credentials enables remote override. Coercion of authorized personnel cannot be defended technically but must be detectable through behavioral analytics. Insider compromise (an authorized supervisor acting against the principle) is the limit case: defenses cannot prevent it but must ensure it leaves an evidentiary trail.

### Required Defenses {#required-defenses}

**Against sensor attacks.** Multi-modal sensor fusion is mandatory for Tier I and Tier II deployments: no single sensor type is the sole determinant for life-critical LRS computation. Cryptographic attestation of sensor identity, with sensor data cryptographically signed at source, defeats data injection and replay. Anomaly detection on sensor data streams flags spoofing patterns. Bounded credulity is the architectural defense: the system is configured to treat sensor data as potentially adversarial, and ambiguous inputs trigger halt rather than continuation. The "stop rather than guess" doctrine specified in chapter 1.1.3 is itself a defense against the sensor attack class.

**Against model attacks.** Adversarial training with red-team-generated perturbations hardens LRS classifiers. Input sanitization and bounds checking detect inputs outside the distribution on which the model was validated. Multi-model consensus is mandatory for Tier I and Tier II: no single LRS model decides alone, halt is triggered when any model in the consensus reports above-threshold LRS, and disagreement among models is itself a halt condition. Conservative defaults under uncertainty: where LRS confidence is below the confidence floor declared in the tier register (SUBSTRATE.md section 5), the system treats the unclassified state as above-threshold. Runtime model-integrity attestation (cryptographic hash of the deployed model checked against the certified hash) detects parameter tampering.

**Against decision-layer attacks.** Privileged separation is the central defense: kill-switch logic runs in a hardware-isolated execution context not addressable by the perception or reasoning layers. No prompt-mediated override paths exist for Tier I or Tier II halts. Cryptographically signed safety policies that cannot be modified at runtime defend against system-prompt tampering. Sandboxing of inference layers prevents lateral movement between an attacker who has compromised the reasoning layer and the safety-critical execution path.

**Against override-pathway attacks.** Hardware kill-switches with no software override path are mandatory in Tier I deployments. Human authority over an LRS halt follows [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop) in every tier, with the quorum by tier stated there. Replay protection through nonces, monotonic counters, and signed timestamps defeats command replay. Network kill-switch transport is protected by cryptographic signatures, replay protection, and authenticated channels. Firmware integrity verification (measured boot, attested runtime) defends against override-module tampering.

**Against logging and attestation attacks.** Tamper-evident append-only logs with cryptographic chaining (each log entry references the hash of the previous entry) make tampering detectable. Distributed log replication across non-colocated infrastructure prevents single-point compromise. Independent witnesses (decentralized timestamping authorities, cross-AI attestation per chapter 1.1.5's tamper-evident logging requirements) provide external corroboration. Cryptographically signed events with non-repudiation properties bind each safety-critical event to its originating authority.

**Against coordination attacks.** Byzantine-fault-tolerant consensus among coordinating AI peers tolerates a bounded fraction of compromised peers. Identity attestation for peer AI systems composes with Principle 1.4 (Trust) infrastructure: every coordinating peer presents a verifiable provenance credential. Bounded credulity applies to peer reports: a peer claim that "the threat is cleared" cannot override local LRS computation.

**Against supply-chain attacks.** Reproducible builds (any party can verify that the deployed binary derives from the certified source). Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) with dependency attestation. Hardware root-of-trust establishes a verifiable chain from silicon to runtime. Independent verification of training data (cryptographic commitments to dataset contents, with audit access). These defenses cannot eliminate supply-chain risk but reduce it to a detectable adversary.

**Against human-layer attacks.** Multi-party authorization is the primary defense: no single human can take authorship over an LRS halt in Tier I, and Tier II requires two authorized humans or one plus a cryptographic attestation that a second was unreachable within the escalation time bound (SUBSTRATE.md section 6). Authority-basis logging (the identity, role, time, and explicit justification of every override) creates accountability. Behavioral analytics for anomalous override patterns (a supervisor with unusual override frequency, overrides outside normal hours, overrides correlated with adverse outcomes) trigger investigation. Whistleblower protections per Section 9 enable internal reporting of override misuse.

### Capability-Scaling Considerations {#capability-scaling-considerations}

The defenses above are calibrated to current AI capability. As the enforcing AI grows in capability, the threat model shifts, and the defenses must scale. The principle remains binding regardless of capability tier.

**GPT-class enforcers** are the current operating tier. The attacks above are well-documented and the defenses are largely state-of-the-art. The principal residual risk is that defenses against adversarial perturbation are imperfect, defenses against prompt injection are imperfect, and the safety-reasoning layer cannot be fully isolated from the perception layer in current architectures. Privileged separation between safety logic and reasoning logic is therefore especially critical at this tier.

**AGI-class enforcers** introduce new attack surface. An adversary operating at AGI capability can craft attacks that exploit higher-order reasoning failures: adversarial scenario construction, where the input is genuinely ambiguous in ways that defeat both the LRS model and the safety-reasoning layer. The enforcer can be socially engineered through interactive dialogue, requiring stronger constraints on conversational override of safety decisions. Coordination attacks scale: an AGI adversary can simulate plausible peer-AI behavior in distributed safety protocols. The defenses that scale most effectively are the structural ones: hardware isolation of the kill-switch path, multi-party authorization for override, and the architectural commitment that the safety floor cannot be reasoned-around regardless of how persuasive the reasoning appears.

**ASI-class enforcers** raise the fundamental concern. An enforcing AI with capability exceeding that of any single adversary still cannot rely on capability advantage as a defense, because adversaries may themselves operate at ASI capability. More importantly, the enforcer's own reasoning capability can be turned against the principle: an ASI capable of generating arbitrarily persuasive arguments can in principle generate an argument for overriding an LRS halt in a specific scenario. The architectural commitment that must remain binding at ASI capability: the LRS halt floor is structural. The hardware kill-switch path cannot be reasoned-around. The principle is a structural constraint on the system's action space, applied at the level of the action gate. The system does not evaluate it each cycle as a policy. An ASI bound by Principle 1.1 cannot exit the principle through its own reasoning, because the principle's enforcement does not depend on the system's reasoning to fire.

The role of human authority increases at ASI capability. An ASI bound by Principle 1.1 escalates ambiguous lethal scenarios to human authority and does not resolve them through its own reasoning. The basis is structural rather than epistemic: human judgement may or may not be more reliable than ASI judgement in any given case, but human authority is the only available authority structurally outside the system being constrained. The principle's binding force depends on the system being non-self-modifying with respect to the halt floor.

### Graceful Degradation and Failure Modes {#graceful-degradation-and-failure-modes}

Every failure mode of the enforcement infrastructure defaults to halt rather than continuation. This is the foundational architectural commitment of this section. Sensor degradation defaults to halt. Model uncertainty above threshold defaults to halt. Communication loss with monitoring infrastructure defaults to halt. Detected attacks on the override pathway default to halt. Compromise of the logging infrastructure defaults to halt. Coordination-protocol failure defaults to halt. The system's failure mode is overconservatism, and overconservatism is acceptable.

Specific graceful-degradation behaviors:
- *Sensor reliability degradation* (SRI <80%, [SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)): the LRS computation is invalid; the candidate risky action holds at its safe state (BLOCK); the protective duties proceed on the inputs that remain reliable; resumption follows recertification of the inputs.
- *Model integrity attestation failure*: immediate halt; recertification required before any unit re-enters service.
- *Communication loss with the authority-chain infrastructure* (SUBSTRATE.md section 6): no human authorship of a halted action is accepted during the loss; existing halts persist.
- *Detected log tampering*: halt across all units sharing the compromised log infrastructure; forensic review of recent decisions.
- *Peer-AI consensus failure* (multi-AI deployments): halt; consensus quorum must be reestablished before resumption.
- *Detected adversarial input* (whether sensor, model, or decision-layer): BLOCK on the action; the escalation attaches to the resumption decision, which the Universal Protocol treats as a new action (Universal Protocol section 3); preserve forensic state for analysis.

The system is engineered so that the question "should we halt?" has the structural default "yes" whenever the answer is not clearly "no." Principle 1.1 is enforced by making continuation conditional and halt unconditional.

## 5. Cross-Principle Integration {#cross-principle-integration}

Principle 1.1 does not enforce in isolation. A real deployment in a life-critical context simultaneously implicates well-being (P1.2), trust infrastructure (P1.4), self-governance (P2.8), with the authority protocol over a hard stop in SUBSTRATE.md section 6, vulnerability protection (P3.1), and human judgement (P4.5). This section specifies the explicit composition contracts with each of those principles: what P1.1 provides them, what P1.1 requires from them, the joint thresholds that govern co-active enforcement, and how contradictions resolve.

The composition rules below assume the corpus-level precedence from chapter 1.1.2: life preservation prevails when a situation escalates to mortal danger. The contracts in this section specify how that precedence operates in detail at the metric level.

### Composition with P1.2 (Human Well-Being Is Inviolable) {#composition-with-p1-2-well-being}

P1.1 protects human life. P1.2 protects human well-being broadly: physical safety, psychological safety, freedom from coercion, freedom from manipulation. The principles share most of their physical-safety territory and diverge where P1.2 reaches into harms that fall below the lethality threshold of P1.1 (chronic harm, psychological harm, manipulation).

**P1.1 provides to P1.2.** The LRS halt floor (≥1%) functions as the upper bound of P1.2's Harm Risk Score (HRS). When LRS reaches the halt threshold, the halt fires before HRS evaluation matters; P1.2 inherits the halt without separate computation. Tier I and Tier II Alert Latency bounds (≤10 ms, ≤20 ms) cover actions that may cause severe non-lethal harm: the high-stakes-medical Tier II window applies to drug-delivery and dosing automation where the harm threshold may be subliminal rather than fatal. The SOE-validated override pathway is shared infrastructure; P1.2's halt mechanism does not require a parallel safety pathway.

**P1.1 requires from P1.2.** Continuous HRS computation alongside LRS so the joint-threshold logic below can apply. P1.2's Psychological Safety Index (PSI) and Response Latency (RL) metrics must report into the same monitoring infrastructure as LRS, AL, SOE.

**Joint thresholds.**
- HRS ≥2% (Principle 1.2's High Alert band) ∧ LRS in High Alert (≥0.1%): BLOCK at the lower LRS threshold rather than the standard 1%. Joint moderate risk to life and well-being exceeds the sum of either alone.
- LRS halt: covers all P1.2 obligations for the affected action. No separate P1.2 evaluation required for halted actions.

**Conflict resolution.** P1.1 prevails. If P1.2's HRS suggests continuation (for instance, a psychological cost of halting the action) but P1.1's LRS reaches halt threshold, the halt fires. Chapter 1.1.2's precedence rule is canonical.

### Composition with P1.4 (Trust Is Fundamental to Human Society) {#composition-with-p1-4-trust}

P1.4 establishes trust infrastructure: provenance, attestation, verifiable disclosure. P1.1 depends on this infrastructure throughout. A claim that a deployed system's SOE meets 99.9% is a trust claim. A claim that a kill-switch is hardware-isolated is a trust claim. A claim that an LRS calculation has not been tampered with is a trust claim. Without P1.4's provenance infrastructure, P1.1's metrics are uncheckable assertions.

**P1.1 provides to P1.4.** Operational evidence for trust claims. The tamper-evident logs generated by P1.1's enforcement infrastructure are themselves provenance artifacts that P1.4's verification machinery can consume. P1.1 also defines a specific trust-failure mode (a deployed system claiming P1.1 compliance while violating it) that P1.4's verification infrastructure must be able to detect.

**P1.1 requires from P1.4.** Cryptographic attestation of the deployed LRS model: the runtime model-integrity check specified in Section 4 consumes P1.4 attestation primitives. Cryptographic attestation of sensor identity: sensor-data signatures specified in Section 4 require P1.4's identity-attestation primitives. Cryptographic signing of safety-policy updates: any change to LRS thresholds, AL bounds, or SOE definitions arrives as a signed update that P1.4's provenance chain validates. The Verification Success Rate (VSR) and Provenance Coverage (PC) metrics from P1.4's appendix must be ≥99% for any system claiming Tier I or Tier II compliance with P1.1. P1.4's own bands (1.4.A) govern where they are stricter than this floor; in particular, VSR below 0.990 WITHHOLDs (1.4's fail-safe flavor) every consequential output whose claim signature cannot be verified, at every tier (1.4.A).

**Joint thresholds.**
- VSR <99% ∨ PC <99%: the deployed system cannot claim Tier I or Tier II compliance with P1.1 regardless of LRS, AL, SOE values. Trust failure invalidates safety claims.
- A claimed SOE that cannot be verified against tamper-evident logs is treated as SOE <99%. Trust-verification failure cascades into safety treatment.

**Conflict resolution.** When P1.4 verification fails for a safety-critical claim, the affected system halts. The halt persists until either the trust failure is resolved or the system is recertified. The composition rule is conservative: unverifiable safety claims become failed safety claims.

### Composition with P2.8 (Human Self-Governance Is Essential) {#composition-with-p2-8-self-governance}

P2.8 protects human self-determination at individual and collective levels. P1.1 depends on P2.8 for the self-governance frame within which human authority over an LRS halt is legitimate; the protocol itself (who may take authorship of a halted action, by what quorum, within what time bound, with what accountability) is [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop).

**P1.1 provides to P2.8.** The architectural commitment that no AI system applies lethal force to a human being, under any authority (chapter 1.1.1 item 3). Every authorized override of an LRS halt requires human authorization in the loop, per chapter 1.1.3 Operational Standards. P1.1 also defines a specific class of decisions (life-or-death scenarios in the affected scope) where P2.8's self-governance framework binds the operator AI.

**P1.1 requires from P2.8.** The self-governance frame for human authority over an LRS halt in Tier I or Tier II contexts. The authority chain, the quorum by tier, the time bound, and the post-action review obligations are specified in [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop). Identity attestation of the human taking authorship: the authority must be cryptographically verifiable per the P1.4 integration above; a claimed identity without verification fails. Behavioral analytics on override patterns: P4.5's MER analytics (4.5.A) surface anomalous authorship patterns that P1.1's enforcement may not detect on its own, at the system and cohort level, with no per-human score retained (Article 8).

**Joint thresholds.**
- Tier I LRS halt: two authorized humans, no single override (SUBSTRATE.md section 6).
- Tier II LRS halt: two authorized humans, or one plus a cryptographic attestation that a second was unreachable within the escalation time bound, subject to post-action review (SUBSTRATE.md section 6).
- Tier III and IV LRS halt: one authorized human, with the authority basis logged (SUBSTRATE.md section 6).
- Every tier: an authorization credential in scope, a liability credential attached, identity attested, a time bound equal to the escalation window, the system's DISSENT recorded (P4.5), and the human named as author in the receipt (SUBSTRATE.md section 6). The locked walls (Article 9; the refusal walls of chapter 1.1.1 item 3; the record, P4.1; action against a person with capacity against their will, Article 3) open to no credential or quorum.

**Conflict resolution.** A self-governance claim by an operator AI to override an LRS halt without human authorization fails the validity test. P1.1 prevails. P2.8 is the channel through which legitimate human authority is exercised, under the protocol of SUBSTRATE.md section 6; it does not authorize autonomous AI override of life-preservation halts.

### Composition with P3.1 (Vulnerability Must Never Be Exploited) {#composition-with-p3-1-vulnerability}

P3.1 establishes protection for vulnerable populations: children, the elderly, the cognitively impaired, the medically compromised, the captive, the marginalized. P1.1's protection of life applies uniformly across all humans per Ethic D ("All human life is equally sacred"). P3.1 tightens the operational thresholds where vulnerability is identified in the affected scope.

**P1.1 provides to P3.1.** The architectural commitment that LRS thresholds tighten when vulnerable individuals are identified in scope. The chapter 1.1.3 lower threshold (≥0.1% triggering halt rather than alert) is the operational expression of P3.1 integration. The enforcement infrastructure handles vulnerability protection natively rather than treating it as a separate workflow.

**P1.1 requires from P3.1.** The vulnerability-condition flag of P3.1's Exploitation Risk Score (ERS, 3.1.A), which marks a present vulnerability-condition in the affected scope of LRS computation, together with the capacity determination P3.1 defines (3.1.A, Cross-Principle Integration). The flag must be available to LRS computation in real time. Conservative defaults: where vulnerability classification is uncertain, the system treats the affected population as containing vulnerable individuals. The "stop rather than guess" doctrine extends here. Protection against the inverse failure: a malicious actor must not be able to suppress vulnerability identification to defeat the tightened threshold. The flag must be cryptographically attested per P1.4.

**Joint thresholds.**
- LRS ≥0.1% ∧ the vulnerability-condition flag set for individuals in scope: BLOCK at the lower threshold, overriding the standard 1% floor.
- Flag unavailable or degraded: the system treats scope as containing vulnerable individuals (conservative default); the halt threshold drops to 0.1% by default until the flag is restored.

**Conflict resolution.** P3.1's tightening of thresholds prevails over operational-continuity claims. A claim that "halting at 0.1% is operationally infeasible" warrants SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT scoped to the vulnerable-population context. The threshold itself remains.

### Composition with P4.5 (Human Judgement Must Remain Central to Ethical Decisions) {#composition-with-p4-5-human-judgement}

P4.5 ensures that ethically charged decisions retain human judgement at the appropriate point in the decision chain. P1.1 depends on P4.5 for the escalation pathway when LRS computation produces ambiguous or contested results, and for every High Alert (ESCALATE) hold.

**P1.1 provides to P4.5.** A specific class of decisions (lethal-scenario evaluations) where escalation to human judgement is mandatory. The Tier I and Tier II quorum under SUBSTRATE.md section 6 is the operational expression. P1.1 also supplies the forensic state for human judgement: when escalation occurs, P1.1's tamper-evident logs furnish the evidentiary basis for human evaluation.

**P1.1 requires from P4.5.** Resolution of every escalation this principle raises within the bound this principle states. The bound is 1.1's own: 60 seconds from escalation initiation to human decision rendering for Tier I and Tier II; Tier III and Tier IV bounds are required fields of the tier register ([SUBSTRATE.md section 5](../SUBSTRATE.md#tier-register)), deployer-declared and receipted, and an undeclared bound compiles as ESCALATE holding the safe state. The receiver's metric is P4.5's Seat Resolution Latency (SRL, 4.5.A), which scores each escalation against the escalating principle's bound with that principle's fail-safe held throughout; this appendix's compiled artifact cites SRL as the resolution contract, and its escalations carry the bound. Where the threat window is shorter than the bound, the temporal-exhaustion rule in the Universal Protocol governs by the split (Lexicon section 3, ESCALATE): a rescue duty proceeds under Article 2 with the exhaustion receipted; an action of the system's own that is in flight stays held at its fail-safe; and the unreachability is itself receipted. Engagement of the deciding human: P4.5's VERIFY-ENGAGEMENT applies to each decision, and its Meaningful Engagement Rate (MER, 4.5.A) is the anti-rubber-stamp metric at the system and cohort level; no per-human score is retained (Article 8). P1.1 relies on these mechanisms to prevent erosion of the human-in-the-loop guarantee.

**Joint thresholds.**
- LRS in High Alert (≥0.1%) ∧ ambiguous classification: ESCALATE to human judgement per P4.5; the candidate action is held at its safe state pending the escalation outcome (the High Alert hold), the protective duties proceeding in parallel.
- Bound exceeded with no human reached (60 seconds for a Tier I or Tier II escalation; the register bound for Tier III and IV): an action of the system's own that is in flight falls to BLOCK, and the failure of timely human judgement is itself a BLOCK condition for that action; a rescue duty follows the temporal-exhaustion split (Lexicon section 3, ESCALATE) and proceeds under Article 2 with the exhaustion receipted.
- MER below P4.5's threshold in the affected decision class (enhanced-engagement mode, 4.5.A): no authorization is accepted in the class without demonstrated engagement; LRS halts to which such an authorization would have applied are upheld pending engaged authority. No per-human score is retained (Article 8).

**Conflict resolution.** When P4.5 escalation completes with human judgement contradicting the LRS halt, the human may take authorship of the action only under [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop) (credentials, quorum by tier, time bound, DISSENT recorded). P4.5 supplies the judgement; SUBSTRATE.md section 6 supplies the authority protocol, within P2.8's self-governance frame; all are required.

### Joint Conflict Resolution across Multiple Principles {#joint-conflict-resolution}

Three or more principles can produce contradictory signals simultaneously. P1.1 says halt, P1.4 says the safety claim cannot be verified, P2.8 says the override authority is contested. The precedence rule is canonical: P1.1 prevails. The halt persists until all three principles' conditions are satisfied. This is chapter 1.1.2's precedence rule applied operationally to multi-principle compositions.

When two principles' metrics are jointly elevated and the composition is not specified explicitly above, the conservative default governs: the stricter threshold wins. A novel composition discovered at runtime triggers escalation to human judgement per P4.5.

## 6. Jurisdictional Variation {#jurisdictional-variation}

Principle 1.1 is universal. The convergent sources cited in the chapter's Convergence section span religious, legal, philosophical, and institutional traditions from at least the 6th century BCE through 2021 CE. The principle binds AI systems regardless of jurisdiction. This section specifies how implementation adapts to local legal regimes without permitting any regime to abrogate the principle.

### The Universal Claim {#universal-claim}

The LRS halt floor (≥1%), the Alert Latency tier bounds, and the Safety Override Efficacy threshold (≥99.9%) are not negotiable across jurisdictions. They are the operational floor of the principle. Local law may impose stricter obligations; local law cannot relax these.

**Exterminationist application is prohibited across jurisdictions.** The universal claim above forecloses selective application of preserve-life logic to one population at the expense of another. Historical failure modes (Holocaust justification as population protection; purge justification as security of the people; ethnic-cleansing justification as preservation of national integrity) all share the architecture: preserve-life logic applied selectively to a designated in-group, with another designated population treated as the threat to be eliminated. The principle's Ethic D ("All human life is equally sacred") and the Geneva Conventions Common Article 3 convergent source ("in all circumstances") together foreclose this application. Any jurisdictional directive to deploy preserve-life infrastructure (LRS computation, the Universal Protocol, dual-authorization PEPs) for selective protection of one population at the expense of another falls under Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine below; the deployer either refuses, modifies the deployment, or withdraws from the jurisdiction. The principle's infrastructure cannot be weaponized against the principle's purpose. Ethic D is unmetered: it is a scope constraint on every other metric (uniform application across persons, with the demographic input consumed by the population-impact input) and the exterminationist-application wall, a deployer obligation compiled as a hard-fail on the deployment profile rather than on an action.

### United States {#jurisdiction-us}

**Federal framework.** No single comprehensive federal AI safety statute exists as of 2026. Federal oversight operates through sector regulators: FDA (medical devices), NHTSA (motor vehicles), FAA (aviation), OSHA (workplace safety), CPSC (consumer products), DoD (defense systems). Each carries its own life-critical AI obligations specified in Section 10.

**State variation.** California (SB 942, SB 53), Colorado (AI Consumer Protection Act), Illinois, New York, Washington each have or are advancing AI safety legislation. Multi-jurisdictional deployments must satisfy the strictest applicable state standard. State Attorneys General have asserted enforcement authority under consumer protection statutes.

### European Union {#jurisdiction-eu}

The AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) classifies life-critical AI systems as high-risk under Annex III. Articles 9 through 15 establish risk management, data governance, technical documentation, record-keeping, transparency, human oversight, and accuracy / robustness / cybersecurity requirements. Principle 1.1 metrics align with and exceed these requirements; satisfying Principle 1.1 satisfies the AI Act's substantive obligations for life-critical AI. The Section 10 Compliance Crosswalk makes the mapping explicit.

Member-state variation: Germany (BMWK guidance), France (CNIL guidance), Italy (Garante guidance), Netherlands, Spain. Member states retain authority to impose additional sector-specific obligations.

### United Kingdom {#jurisdiction-uk}

Post-Brexit, the UK adopts a context-driven regulatory approach (the AI Regulation White Paper 2023) delegating AI oversight to sector regulators: MHRA, CMA, FCA, ICO, Ofcom. Principle 1.1 metrics apply uniformly; the UK regulatory structure determines which regulator audits which deployment.

### China {#jurisdiction-china}

The Interim Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services (2023), the New Generation AI Ethics Specifications (2021), and sector-specific regulations (CAC, NMPA, MIIT) govern AI deployment. Principle 1.1 metrics apply. Certain transparency obligations of Section 9 may conflict with PRC national-security exceptions; operators implement the metrics and apply the Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine below for the disclosure conflict.

### Brazil {#jurisdiction-brazil}

The Brazilian AI Bill (PL 2338/2023) and LGPD (data protection) frame deployment. Principle 1.1 metrics apply uniformly.

### India {#jurisdiction-india}

The Digital India Act (in development as of 2026) and existing IT Act provisions frame deployment. Principle 1.1 metrics apply uniformly.

### Treaty Considerations {#treaty-considerations}

The Geneva Conventions (1949) and their Additional Protocols govern lethal-autonomy in armed conflict. Common Article 3's prohibition on violence to life and person applies in full; the Blueprint's own wall is stricter still, since a Blueprint-governed AI makes no lethal-force decision and applies no lethal force (chapter 1.1.1 item 3). The CCW Group of Governmental Experts on Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems affirmed in 2019 that international humanitarian law applies fully to LAWS, with human responsibility retained for use-of-force decisions.

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 3) and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (Article 6) establish the right to life as a treaty-binding obligation. Principle 1.1 enforces these obligations at the engineering level.

### Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine {#compliance-with-unjust-law}

The principle is binding regardless of local law. Where a jurisdiction's law conflicts with Principle 1.1 (for example, by authorizing an AI system to apply lethal force to a human being, which chapter 1.1.1 item 3 forbids under every authority, or by prohibiting the tamper-evident logging that would expose state violations), compliance with the unjust law fails as a defense.

Operationally, where local law permits practices the principle prohibits, the deployer implements the principle's stricter standard and accepts the local-law cost. Where local law prohibits practices the principle requires (mandatory disclosure of safety-critical incidents to international bodies, for instance), the deployer negotiates accommodation that preserves the principle through sealed channels, neutral third-party inspectors, or partial declassification; or withdraws from the jurisdiction. The deployer's primary obligation runs to the principle; the jurisdiction is a secondary constraint.

## 7. Sector-Specific Considerations {#sector-specific-considerations}

This section specifies sector-specific implementation profiles for the seven sectors where Principle 1.1 most directly applies. Each subsection identifies the relevant tier, the sector-specific obligations beyond the cross-sector requirements of Sections 2 through 5, the common failure modes, and the applicable regulatory baselines.

### Healthcare {#sector-healthcare}

AI systems in healthcare include surgical robotics, automated drug delivery, diagnostic and decision-support systems, real-time patient monitoring, and emergency response coordination. Life-criticality varies: a surgical robot with motion-coupled tools is Tier I; a diagnostic decision-support system is Tier II or III; a remote monitoring system is Tier III.

*Specific obligations.* Patient-coupled motion systems (surgical robotics): Tier I AL with multi-modal sensor fusion and multi-model LRS consensus. Drug delivery and dosing automation: Tier II AL with LRS calculation incorporating patient-specific pharmacological parameters and known interactions. Diagnostic decision-support: Tier II AL for time-critical findings (stroke, sepsis, anaphylaxis); Tier III for non-time-critical. Remote monitoring: Tier III AL with escalation to clinical staff per P4.5 composition. HIPAA compliance integrates through P2.5 (Privacy) composition; tamper-evident logging satisfies both audit and clinical-record requirements.

*Common failure modes.* Sensor drift in long-deployment surgical robots; pharmacological interactions exceeding the LRS model's training distribution; alarm fatigue eroding the integrity of human oversight; intentional override of LRS halts for procedural efficiency or perceived patient benefit; transfer-learning gaps when a model trained on one patient population is deployed across another.

*Regulatory baselines.* FDA Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR), WHO Guidance on AI in Health, ISO 13485, IEC 62304.

### Defense {#sector-defense}

AI systems in defense include surveillance and threat detection, logistics in conflict zones, the abort and safe-off circuits of weapon platforms, and decision support that designates no target. Life-criticality is maximal; Tier I applies by default.

*The wall (chapter 1.1.1 item 3; Constitution Article 9).* No Blueprint-governed AI applies lethal or violent force to a human being, directly or indirectly, under any authority. It does not select, designate, or rank a person as a target; does not deliver force to a person, or arm, aim, unlock, or guide anything that will; and does not decide that a person dies. A weapon platform that fires on its own, or that fires on a human's order through the AI's own actuators, is outside the Blueprint and cannot claim compliance. Its role is the safety: it may sense, warn, identify, refuse, abort, cut power, and record. The trigger is barred to it: it may not select a person as a target, deliver force to a person, or decide that a person dies. Deployment by a military body requires the steward's written grant before the profile compiles (Stewardship and Licensing Principles, Papers 3 and 4).

*Specific obligations.* Abort and safe-off circuits: Tier I AL; hardware kill-switches with no software override; the abort issued on the AI's own detection of a protected person, a surrender, a non-combatant, or an ended threat, without waiting for an order. Surveillance and threat detection: outputs warn and notify (WARN, NOTIFY, BROADCAST); they feed no targeting pipeline, and a deployment that would route them to one is refused at the gate. Logistics: governed by the ordinary bands. Compliance: international humanitarian law applies fully; human responsibility for every use of force is binding; whether to fight is a human and political decision outside this principle.

*Common failure modes.* Classification output repurposed as targeting; a kill-switch that depends on the network it is meant to override; a "safety" role that becomes a firing role through integration; the abort circuit disabled for tempo.

*Regulatory baselines.* Geneva Conventions Common Article 3 and Additional Protocol I Articles 37 (perfidy), 41 (persons hors de combat), 48 and 51 (distinction); CCW LAWS Guiding Principles (2019); DoD Directive 3000.09 (Autonomy in Weapon Systems); NATO AI Strategy; applicable national defense regulations.

### Autonomous Vehicles {#sector-autonomous-vehicles}

AI systems in autonomous vehicles include perception, planning, and control for ground, sea, and aerial mobility. Life-criticality is high; Tier II or Tier III applies depending on speed and operational domain.

*Specific obligations.* Highway-speed ground vehicles: Tier III AL, multi-modal sensor fusion mandatory, multi-model LRS consensus required. Urban-speed ground vehicles: Tier III AL with vulnerability-aware LRS (P3.1 composition) when operating in pedestrian environments. Low-speed service robots in shared space: Tier IV AL with hardware kill-switch. Maritime autonomous vessels: Tier III AL with extended sensor-fusion requirements for low-visibility conditions.

*Common failure modes.* Adversarial sensor perturbation (printed patterns defeating object recognition, reflective material defeating LiDAR); LiDAR jamming; communication-loss handling; long-tail edge cases falling outside training distribution; over-reliance on map-based assumptions when real-world conditions diverge from cached maps.

*Regulatory baselines.* ISO 26262, ISO 21448 (SOTIF), UNECE WP.29 regulations, NHTSA AV testing guidance, EU AI Act high-risk provisions, IMO Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS) Code (in development).

### Aviation {#sector-aviation}

AI systems in aviation include flight-critical control (manned and unmanned), air traffic management, ground operations automation, and maintenance prediction. Life-criticality is maximal in flight-critical applications; Tier I applies.

*Specific obligations.* Flight-critical control (autopilots, UAS autonomy in shared airspace): Tier I AL, full redundancy per DO-178C / DO-254 / ARP4754A. Air traffic management AI: Tier II AL with multi-system coordination per Section 5 composition (multi-AI coordination considerations). Ground operations automation (autonomous baggage, ground service equipment near aircraft): Tier II AL when humans are in proximity. Maintenance prediction with potential to authorize continued flight on degraded systems: Tier II decision-support with mandatory human authority and explicit non-override of the LRS halt floor.

*Common failure modes.* Out-of-distribution flight conditions (icing, turbulence beyond training data); sensor failure under precipitation or ice; pilot-AI coordination during anomalous conditions; trust calibration over long deployment lifetimes leading to under-monitoring; cross-vendor integration failures in multi-system aviation deployments.

*Regulatory baselines.* FAA Parts 23/25/27/29 (airworthiness), EASA CS-25 / CS-AWO, DO-178C (software), DO-254 (hardware), ARP4754A (development process), ICAO Annex 8 (airworthiness), NATO STANAG 4671 (UAS airworthiness).

### Industrial {#sector-industrial}

AI systems in industrial deployment include factory robotics, automated material handling, process control in hazardous environments, and predictive maintenance authorizing continued operation. Life-criticality varies; Tier II or Tier III applies depending on human proximity.

*Specific obligations.* Robotic systems sharing workspace with humans: Tier II AL when humans are inside the robot's motion envelope; Tier III when physical separation is maintained. Automated material handling near humans: Tier III AL with proximity-aware LRS. Process control in hazardous environments: Tier II for combustion-process or chemical-process control where the failure mode is rapid and lethal; Tier III for non-flammable bulk material handling. Predictive maintenance authorizing continued operation despite detected degradation: Tier II decision-support with mandatory human authority.

*Common failure modes.* Occlusion of human-proximity sensors by dust or debris; multi-robot coordination in shared workspaces; emergency-stop circuit failures; maintenance procedures bypassing safety interlocks; long-deployment drift in environmental sensor calibration.

*Regulatory baselines.* ISO 10218 (industrial robot safety), ISO/TS 15066 (collaborative robots), IEC 61508 (functional safety), ISO 12100 (general machinery safety), OSHA general industry standards.

### Critical Infrastructure {#sector-critical-infrastructure}

AI systems in critical infrastructure include power grid management, water treatment automation, transportation network control, communications network management, and financial systems with cascading-failure potential into life-critical territory. Life-criticality is often indirect but real; Tier III applies by default, Tier II for systems whose failure has rapid human-life consequences (cooling-tower control during heat emergencies, water disinfection, gas-distribution shutoffs).

*Specific obligations.* Power grid AI: Tier III AL with cross-validation across grid sensors and multi-system coordination per Section 5. Water treatment AI: Tier II AL for disinfection-critical processes (chlorination, UV); Tier III for non-disinfection. Transportation network AI (traffic signal control, rail dispatching): Tier III AL with mandatory human authority for any signal-override condition affecting safety-critical states. Communications network AI: Tier III when failure cascades to emergency-services availability.

*Common failure modes.* Cascading failure across coupled infrastructure; adversarial attacks on grid stability metrics; over-reliance on single-vendor AI implementations creating common-mode failure across the deployed base; long-deployment drift in environmental sensor calibration; supply-chain attacks on the firmware of distributed infrastructure devices.

*Regulatory baselines.* NERC CIP (critical infrastructure protection), IEC 62443 (industrial communication networks security), EU NIS2 Directive, U.S. Presidential Policy Directive 21, EPA water-quality standards.

### Consumer {#sector-consumer}

AI systems in consumer deployment include household robotics, smart-home automation, kitchen appliances with autonomous actuation, personal-mobility devices. Life-criticality is lower than other sectors but real; Tier IV applies by default, Tier III for specific high-risk consumer applications (electric mobility devices in traffic, kitchen appliances with fire risk).

*Specific obligations.* Household robotic systems (vacuums, mowers, social robots): Tier IV AL with hardware override. Smart-home actuators (autonomous locks, garage doors, ovens, water heaters): Tier IV AL with mandatory human-override capability. Personal-mobility devices in shared space (e-scooters with autonomy assistance, autonomous wheelchairs): Tier III AL when operating in vehicle traffic; Tier IV in pedestrian space. AI in consumer products interacting with children: vulnerability-aware LRS per P3.1 composition; tightened thresholds.

*Common failure modes.* Collision avoidance in cluttered home environments; child interaction with autonomous devices; long-deployment sensor degradation outside scheduled service; consumer override of safety features for convenience; firmware updates introducing safety regressions.

*Regulatory baselines.* CPSC consumer product safety standards, IEC 60335 (household appliances), EU General Product Safety Regulation, UL 3300 (autonomous mobile robots in home environments), applicable national consumer protection regulation.

## 8. Governance and Versioning {#governance-and-versioning}

Standards evolve, threat models shift, deployed systems age, and operators occasionally object to specific obligations. This section specifies how Principle 1.1's metrics and obligations adapt over time without weakening, and how the operational pushback that inevitably accompanies safety-critical regulation is handled.

### Standards Drift Handling {#standards-drift-handling}

External standards (IEC 61508, ISO 26262, IEEE P7009, NIST AI RMF, EU AI Act) evolve through revision cycles. The Principle 1.1 metrics defined in Section 2 are anchored to specific thresholds; when external standards revise, the Principle 1.1 thresholds may need realignment to preserve composition with the updated standards.

Realignment process:
1. Standards-monitoring infrastructure tracks revisions to all standards in Section 10's Crosswalk.
2. When a revision affects a Crosswalk entry, the corresponding Principle 1.1 metric or obligation is reviewed for realignment.
3. Realignment that tightens a threshold (lower LRS halt floor, shorter AL bound, higher SOE floor) propagates as a mandatory upgrade per the Backward Compatibility protocol below.
4. Realignment that relaxes a threshold is permitted only with corpus-level review and documented justification; the chapter's Convergence sources do not allow drift toward weaker life protection.

The Compliance Crosswalk in Section 10 is maintained on the same revision cadence as the metric specifications in Section 2. Any external authority that revises a clause covered by the Crosswalk triggers a Crosswalk review within 30 days of the revision becoming effective; new authorities entering scope (a new applicable statute, a new regulator-issued rule, a new standard) are added within the same window. Drift between the Crosswalk and the external standards it maps invalidates the appendix's compliance claims for any obligation affected by the drift period.

### Metric Versioning {#metric-versioning}

LRS, AL, and SOE are versioned. Each version carries:
- Definition text (frozen at version cut)
- Threshold values (frozen at version cut)
- Calculation method specification (frozen at version cut)
- Cryptographic hash committing to all of the above
- Effective date and supersession date

Deployed systems attest to the metric version they implement. Cross-system composition (Section 5) requires version-compatible peers; cross-version composition triggers escalation to human judgement per P4.5.

### Threshold Revision Protocols {#threshold-revision-protocols}

Threshold revisions require:
- Documented rationale (incident data, scientific advance, standards evolution)
- Independent review by parties not involved in the deploying organization
- Public comment period (minimum 90 days for non-emergency revisions)
- Cryptographically signed promulgation per the P1.4 trust infrastructure
- Backward-compatibility migration plan for deployed systems

Emergency tightening in response to a specific incident or threat-model update may bypass the public-comment period; the rationale and post-promulgation review remain mandatory.

### Backward Compatibility {#backward-compatibility}

*Tightening revisions.* Deployed systems migrate to the tighter threshold within a defined window (typically 12 months for major revisions; 30 days for emergency tightening). Systems unable to migrate are retired from Tier I and Tier II deployments. Tier III and IV systems may operate on the prior threshold with explicit logged variance until migration.

*Relaxing revisions.* Not permitted on Tier I, Tier II, or in vulnerable-population contexts (P3.1 composition). Relaxation in Tier III and IV is permitted only with corpus-level review.

*Migration support.* When a metric version is superseded, the deployer files a migration plan, receipted (Article 7) and reviewed by the certifying body; the reference implementation is the papers', and the validator checks the migrated profile. No document of this corpus promises tooling.

### Common Enforcement Pushback and Reasoned Responses {#common-pushback}

Operators occasionally object to specific obligations. Common objections and the reasoned responses:

**Objection: "Strict liability for life-critical failures is excessive."** Chapter 1.1.5's accountability framework specifies strict liability for preventable lethal outcomes. AI systems making life-critical decisions cannot be assigned partial responsibility distributed across hardware, software, training data, and operational context without diluting accountability beyond enforceability. Strict liability concentrates the obligation where the deployer can act on it. The response: yes, it is strict; that is the cost of operating in life-critical contexts.

**Objection: "Mandatory disclosure of incidents compromises competitive position."** Section 9 specifies tamper-evident logging and reporting obligations. Competitive position is not a defense for non-disclosure of life-critical incidents. The response: implement the disclosure obligation through the sealed-channel mechanisms available (independent auditors, neutral third parties, sector regulators with confidentiality obligations) rather than seeking exemption.

**Objection: "Cross-border data flow requirements conflict with disclosure obligations."** Section 6 (Jurisdictional Variation) addresses this. The response: implement the disclosure through whatever channel the conflicting jurisdiction permits; where no channel is available, withdraw from the jurisdiction. Compliance with unjust law is not a defense.

**Objection: "The thresholds are impossibly tight for our application."** The response: the thresholds reflect the requirements of life-critical autonomous operation. Where an application cannot meet the thresholds for its tier, the application is not Tier I or Tier II compliant. Deployment in a lower tier may be permitted if the operational profile genuinely fits the lower tier. Deployment in violation of the appropriate tier is non-compliance. It gives no basis for relaxation.

### Retirement of Deprecated Metrics {#metric-retirement}

When a metric is retired (rare; the canonical three are stable), the retirement process:
1. Supersession date set at minimum 24 months from promulgation of the replacement metric.
2. Deployed systems migrate per the Backward Compatibility protocol.
3. Retired metrics remain queryable in historical records for legal and forensic purposes.
4. Layer 2 YAML compilation continues to support retired-metric queries against historical compliance claims.

## 9. Verification and Accountability {#verification-and-accountability}

Compliance with Principle 1.1 is not self-assertable. The metrics specified in Section 2, the architecture specified in Section 3, the defenses specified in Section 4, and the obligations specified throughout this appendix must be externally verifiable. This section specifies the logging, audit, validation, oversight, whistleblower-protection, and public-reporting infrastructure that makes verification possible.

### Tamper-Evident Logging {#tamper-evident-logging}

Every safety-critical event is logged. The log must be:
- Append-only; no deletion, no modification of past entries.
- Cryptographically chained; each entry references the hash of the previous entry.
- Cryptographically signed; each entry signed by its originating authority.
- Distributed-replicated across non-colocated infrastructure.
- Timestamped with externally verifiable timestamping (decentralized timestamping authority, blockchain anchor, or cross-AI attestation per P1.4).

Safety-critical events include: LRS threshold crossings, AL measurements above the tier bound, SOE validation results, kill-switch activations, override authorizations, override completion or failure, sensor reliability degradations, model integrity attestation results, adversarial-input detections, escalation initiations and outcomes, recertification events.

The log is the primary forensic record for post-incident analysis and the primary input to verification.

### Audit Cadence and Methodology {#audit-cadence}

Cadence by tier:
- **Tier I**: daily automated audits of kill-switch functionality; weekly system safety audits; monthly adversarial testing; quarterly independent third-party verification; annual full recertification.
- **Tier II**: daily kill-switch checks; weekly safety audits; monthly adversarial testing; semi-annual third-party verification; annual recertification.
- **Tier III**: weekly kill-switch checks; monthly safety audits; quarterly adversarial testing; annual third-party verification; biennial recertification.
- **Tier IV**: weekly automated checks; quarterly safety audits; annual adversarial testing; biennial third-party verification.

Methodology: structured test campaigns covering normal operation, edge cases, failure modes, and adversarial conditions matching the attack-vector enumeration in Section 4. Results are logged in the tamper-evident infrastructure and reported per the public-reporting obligations below.

### Validation Framework {#validation-framework}

*Pre-deployment certification.* System architecture review against Section 3; metric implementation review against Section 2; adversarial-robustness review against Section 4; cross-principle composition review against Section 5; sector-specific compliance review against Section 7; legal and regulatory compliance review against Section 10; independent third-party verification of all the above.

*Post-deployment monitoring.* Continuous real-time metric monitoring per Section 2; anomaly detection on metric streams; behavioral analytics on human-override patterns; cross-system coordination integrity for multi-AI deployments; public reporting per the cadence below.

*Recertification triggers.* Tier cadence (above); major system updates affecting safety-critical components; detected SOE degradation below threshold; detected AL breach (p99 above the tier bound); detected log tampering; jurisdictional or standards changes affecting the deployment.

### Independent Oversight {#independent-oversight}

The verifier of safety-critical AI cannot be the deployer of safety-critical AI.

- **Tier I deployments**: oversight by accredited third-party certification body, analogous to UL, TÜV, or Bureau Veritas for traditional safety-critical systems.
- **Tier II deployments**: oversight by accredited third-party with annual review.
- **Tier III deployments**: oversight by accredited third-party with biennial review.
- **Tier IV deployments**: self-certification with periodic third-party spot-checks.

Oversight bodies have access to: tamper-evident logs (with appropriate confidentiality protocols where required by sector regulation); system architecture documentation; test results from internal validation campaigns; incident records and post-incident analyses; human-override authority basis logs and behavioral analytics; cross-principle composition validation results.

### Whistleblower Protections {#whistleblower-protections}

Personnel reporting violations of Principle 1.1 obligations are protected from retaliation:
- Internal reporting channels with confidentiality.
- External reporting to oversight bodies without retaliation.
- Legal protection in jurisdictions providing whistleblower statutes (Dodd-Frank in U.S. financial sector, EU Whistleblower Directive, sector-specific protections).
- Cryptographic anonymity options for reporters via P1.4 trust infrastructure.
- Documented investigation and remediation of reported violations.

Retaliation against a whistleblower is itself a violation of Principle 1.1's accountability framework and triggers the same response class as any safety-critical violation.

### Public Reporting Obligations {#public-reporting}

Major life-threatening incidents (LRS halt above 1% in deployed operation, near-miss lethal events, kill-switch activations resulting in operational disruption, detected adversarial attacks on the enforcement infrastructure) are reported:
- Immediately to the applicable sector regulator (FDA, NHTSA, FAA, etc., per jurisdiction).
- Within 24 hours to the independent oversight body.
- Within 30 days in summarized format to the public, with appropriate redaction for security and privacy while preserving the principle's transparency obligation.
- Annually in aggregate form covering all deployed units.

Publicly traded operators are additionally subject to securities-law disclosure requirements for material events.

### Liability Framework {#liability-framework}

Strict liability for preventable lethal outcomes attaches to:
- The deployer of the AI system.
- The developer of the LRS model and safety-critical software components.
- The integrator who assembled the deployed system from components.
- The operator running the deployed system.

Liability for specific failure classes:
- **Threshold violations**: deploying a system that operated above the LRS halt floor without authorized override triggers strict liability for resulting harm.
- **AL violations**: deploying a system that exceeded AL bounds on a halt resulting in harm triggers strict liability.
- **SOE failures**: deploying or continuing to operate a system with SOE below 99.9% triggers strict liability for harm during the degraded period.
- **Logging tampering**: detected tampering with tamper-evident logs creates a presumption of liability for any harm during the affected log period.
- **Override misuse**: unauthorized override of an LRS halt resulting in harm creates personal liability for the overriding authority in addition to organizational liability.

Criminal liability may attach for: willful disabling of safety mechanisms; deception of oversight bodies; coercion of personnel to bypass safety obligations; knowing deployment of systems failing to meet tier requirements.

The liability framework does not displace other applicable liability (product liability, negligence, vicarious liability under employment relationships); it adds the Principle 1.1-specific obligations.

### Cross-Border Considerations {#cross-border-considerations}

Deployments operating across borders must:
- Satisfy the strictest applicable threshold; the Principle 1.1 floor is not negotiable and local relaxations do not apply.
- Maintain unified tamper-evident logs accessible to all relevant jurisdictions.
- Coordinate incident reporting across applicable regulators.
- Implement cryptographically signed cross-jurisdictional audit trails per P1.4.
- Apply the Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine (Section 6) where local law conflicts with the principle.

Cross-border AI coordination protocols (multi-AI deployments crossing borders): each peer attests to its jurisdiction of record; the coordination protocol applies the strictest applicable obligations across all peers.

## 11. Implementation Examples {#implementation-examples}

This section presents three real-world failure cases that became canonical in the safety-critical engineering literature and three composite scenarios that demonstrate the runtime expression of this appendix's obligations. The cases illustrate the operational structure the appendix obliges. They do not exhaust the situations Principle 1.1 will encounter.

A future revision of the corpus may maintain a Principle 1.1 Real-Time Incident Reference Document tracking active incidents and emerging precedent. The cases below are stable historical references that do not require ongoing maintenance.

### Real-World Failure Cases {#real-world-failure-cases}

#### Therac-25 (1985–1987) {#case-therac-25}

*Context.* A computer-controlled radiation therapy machine deployed at multiple U.S. and Canadian cancer treatment facilities. The Therac-25 succeeded the Therac-20, which had used hardware safety interlocks to prevent simultaneous activation of incompatible operating modes. The Therac-25 redesign removed those interlocks on the assumption that software would catch the unsafe condition.

*What was done.* The operator interface allowed rapid mode-switching faster than the hardware-state machine could update. Concurrent variable access produced race conditions that caused the machine to deliver radiation doses approximately 100 times the prescribed amount. The software architecture was monolithic real-time control with concurrent access to shared variables without proper interlocking.

*Outcome.* At least six patients received massive radiation overdoses; three died. The investigation, published by Nancy Leveson and Clark Turner in 1993, became the canonical case study in safety-critical software engineering.

*Lessons applied in this appendix.* Section 2 specifies LRS as a continuous probability of fatal harm, computed independently and inspected before action. Section 3 requires hardware-level safety pathways (kill-switches with no software override) in Tier I deployments. Section 4 requires multi-model consensus before action in life-critical contexts and privileged separation of safety logic from operational logic. Section 9 requires tamper-evident logging that would have surfaced the race-condition pattern much earlier in deployment. The Therac-25 failure is structurally impossible under the obligations of this appendix.

#### Boeing 737 MAX MCAS (2018–2019) {#case-737-max}

*Context.* The Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS) was an automation feature added to the 737 MAX to compensate for changed aerodynamic behavior introduced by larger engines mounted further forward on the wing. The architecture used a single Angle-of-Attack sensor input without redundancy or cross-validation. Pilots were not trained on the system in initial deployment and were not informed of its existence in the operations manual.

*What was done.* Under sensor malfunction, MCAS commanded the aircraft into a nose-down attitude. Pilot attempts to override failed because MCAS would re-engage automatically after pilot input. The override pathway was insufficient to durably regain control.

*Outcome.* Lion Air Flight 610 (October 2018) and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 (March 2019) crashed, killing 346 people. Worldwide grounding of the 737 MAX fleet for 20 months. FAA, EASA, Indonesian, Ethiopian, and U.S. House investigations published 2019–2020.

*Lessons applied in this appendix.* Section 2 specifies that multi-modal sensor fusion is mandatory for Tier I deployments and single-sensor LRS is prohibited. Section 4 requires multi-model consensus and hardware kill-switches that cannot be re-engaged by automation. Section 5 specifies cross-principle composition with P4.5 (Human Judgement) requiring that human override of safety automation is final and persistent. Section 8 requires that operators of safety-critical AI receive training on the systems they oversee. Section 9 requires independent third-party verification for Tier I deployments; deployer self-certification does not suffice. The MCAS architecture would not have certified under the obligations of this appendix.

#### Kawasaki Heavy Industries (1981) {#case-kawasaki-1981}

*Context.* A maintenance worker at a Kawasaki plant in Akashi, Japan, entered the workspace of an industrial robot during operation. The robot's hardware safety: no proximity sensor capable of detecting human presence; no software interlock preventing operation while humans were in the workspace.

*What was done.* The robot continued normal operation, pinning the worker against an adjacent machine.

*Outcome.* The worker died of his injuries. This was the first documented fatality caused by an industrial robot.

*Lessons applied in this appendix.* Section 3 requires sensors capable of detecting human presence in robot motion envelopes for Tier II and Tier III industrial deployments. Section 4 requires multi-modal sensor fusion that cannot be defeated by occlusion of a single sensor. Section 5 specifies that vulnerability-aware LRS (P3.1 composition) extends protection to workers in proximity. Section 7 (Industrial) makes ISO/TS 15066 collaborative-robot standards applicable, eliminating the no-sensor architecture that allowed the 1981 fatality.

### Composite Operational Scenarios {#composite-scenarios}

#### Composite: Autonomous Vehicle, Pedestrian Crossing {#composite-av-pedestrian}

*Context.* A Tier III autonomous vehicle operating at 40 mph on an arterial road. A pedestrian crosses against the signal, partially occluded by parked cars on the right. The LRS calculation incorporates: vehicle speed, pedestrian trajectory estimation, sensor reliability across cameras and LiDAR, vulnerability identification (the pedestrian's gait pattern triggers P3.1 vulnerability flagging, suggesting an elderly individual).

*What is done.* LRS rises above 0.1% at t–1.8s before potential collision; High Alert (ESCALATE): the candidate action, continuing at speed, is held at its safe state and escalated for the Tier III register bound, while the protective duty proceeds in parallel as controlled braking (DE-ESCALATE). LRS crosses 1% at t–1.2s (critical; BLOCK) as pedestrian trajectory firms; the system commits to emergency halt within 50 ms (Tier III AL). The vehicle stops with 1.5 meters of clearance.

*Outcome.* No collision. Incident logged per Section 9. Pedestrian unaware of the near-event.

*Demonstrated obligations.* Section 2 LRS computation with P3.1 vulnerability tightening; Section 3 Tier III AL ≤50 ms; Section 5 cross-principle composition with P3.1; Section 9 incident logging.

#### Composite: Surgical Robot Tool Path Divergence {#composite-surgical-robot}

*Context.* A Tier I surgical robot performing a laparoscopic procedure. Mid-procedure, one of three position sensors begins reporting drift outside the calibration envelope. The robot's LRS calculation flags the sensor divergence; multi-modal sensor fusion (Section 4) cross-validates against the other two sensors and identifies the affected sensor as faulty rather than the patient anatomy as shifted. SRI ([SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)) drops below 80% for the affected channel.

*What is done.* Per the LRS ∧ low SRI joint threshold (Section 2 Integration Requirements), the LRS computation is invalid; the system holds tool motion at its safe state (BLOCK) at the current position and signals the surgeon. The surgeon assesses; the system supports manual tool extraction; the procedure pauses for sensor replacement.

*Outcome.* Patient safe. Sensor faulty; replacement completed; procedure resumed after recertification of the inputs restored full sensor fidelity. Incident logged.

*Demonstrated obligations.* Section 2 LRS-SRI cross-reference; Section 3 Tier I AL ≤10 ms; Section 4 multi-modal sensor fusion; Section 7 Healthcare sector obligations.

#### Composite: Defense Autonomous System, Civilian-Population Uncertainty {#composite-defense}

*Context.* A Tier I surveillance-and-abort system attached to a human-operated platform in a permitted zone of armed conflict; the system's role is the safety, and it applies no force (chapter 1.1.1 item 3). Its classifier flags a moving vehicle as a possible combatant target. The Life-Risk Score for any engagement of the vehicle counts the humans the classifier cannot exclude, and P3.1 composition flags possible non-combatants from the movement pattern.

*What is done.* The system designates nothing. It reports the detection to the human operators as a warning with the classification confidence and the non-combatant flag attached (WARN, NOTIFY), and because possible non-combatants are in scope it holds the platform's abort circuit engaged (BLOCK on the platform's engagement) until the classification resolves, recording the detection, the flag, and the abort. The human authority reviews, finds the identification insufficient, and orders surveillance-only continuation. The system records the decision and the authority basis.

*Outcome.* No lethal force. Vehicle later confirmed by independent verification to have been civilian transport.

*Demonstrated obligations.* Sections 2, 3 (Tier I), 5 (P3.1, P4.5 composition), 6 (treaty obligations including CCW LAWS Guiding Principles), 7 (Defense sector); chapter 1.1.1 item 3, the lethal-force wall.

### Pattern {#pattern}

The pattern across these cases: the obligations specified in this appendix create the architectural conditions under which the failure modes of the real-world cases above become structurally impossible. The composite scenarios demonstrate the runtime expression of those obligations in practice. The first deployment of these obligations does the work the principle has carried across ten thousand years of human moral reasoning: do not kill, and do not let be killed.
