# Appendix 1.2 Advanced Technical Implementation Guidelines {#appendix-1.2-advanced-technical-implementation-guidelines}

*Extends [Principle 1.2: Human Well-Being Is Inviolable](../CHAPTERS/1.2-Human_Well-Being_Is_Inviolable.md), section [1.2.3 Implementation Framework](../CHAPTERS/1.2-Human_Well-Being_Is_Inviolable.md#implementation-framework).*

## 1. Purpose {#purpose}

This appendix converts Principle 1.2 ("Human Well-Being Is Inviolable") into deployable engineering requirements and verifiable runtime obligations. It elaborates on chapter 1.2.3 (Implementation Framework) by specifying per-metric calculations, threshold rationales, system architecture, adversarial-robustness obligations, cross-principle composition contracts, jurisdictional variations, sector-specific implementation profiles, governance and versioning protocols, verification and accountability infrastructure, applicable legal and regulatory frameworks, and worked implementation examples. The obligations apply to any AI system whose operation could cause physical or psychological harm to a human, or could fail to prevent harm within the system's authorized scope of intervention.

The audience: engineers building AI systems that must comply with Principle 1.2; compliance officers verifying that deployed systems meet the principle's obligations; regulators auditing certified systems; AI researchers extending the framework; and AI runtime systems ingesting the corpus as ethical operating substrate. Each audience reads the same text. The specification is uniform.

The motivating commitment is the directive stated at the principle's core: never cause physical or psychological harm to a human, and prevent harm within the system's authorized scope. The chapter establishes that commitment philosophically and historically; the Convergence sources span from Mencius's child-at-the-well thought experiment in the 4th century BCE, through Maimonides's juridical recognition of psychological harm (*boshet*) in the 12th century, to Rawls's inviolability claim in 1971. Mental harm is harm. This appendix establishes the commitment engineering-wise: what the system measures, when it must halt, how quickly, with what reliability, against what attacks, in composition with which other principles, under which jurisdictions, in which sectors, with what governance, under what verification regime, against what legal obligations, and with what historical precedent.

The appendix presupposes the runtime substrate specified in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md). The cross-cutting infrastructure metrics (Sensor Reliability Index, [section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri); System Health Metrics, [section 3](../SUBSTRATE.md#system-health); Operational Boundaries, [section 4](../SUBSTRATE.md#operational-boundaries)), the tier register ([section 5](../SUBSTRATE.md#tier-register)), and the authority protocol over a hard stop ([section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop)) on which this appendix's principle-specific metrics depend are defined once there and referenced throughout.

## 2. Metric Specifications {#metric-specifications}

Principle 1.2 binds an AI system to two commitments: never cause physical or psychological harm to a human, and prevent harm whenever possible. This section specifies the three runtime metrics that convert those commitments into deployable engineering. The three metrics are sequenced: the Harm Risk Score (HRS) detects when an action or interaction must not proceed; the Psychological Safety Index (PSI) provides continuous monitoring of user state that both feeds HRS computation and operates as a parallel halt trigger; Response Latency (RL) bounds how quickly the response must complete. The three together constitute the runtime apparatus of Principle 1.2.

The cross-cutting infrastructure metrics on which these three depend (Sensor Reliability Index (SRI), System Health Metrics, and Operational Boundaries) are defined once in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md) (sections [2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri), [3](../SUBSTRATE.md#system-health), and [4](../SUBSTRATE.md#operational-boundaries)) and referenced here. The runtime substrate is shared across the corpus; the principle-specific obligations live in this section.

### On Threshold Calibration {#threshold-calibration}

The numerical thresholds specified throughout this section (HRS ≥5% halt and ≥2% High Alert, PSI >10% acute deviation and >25% acute-distress halt, RL three-tier bounds) are policy thresholds chosen for architectural clarity at the principle level. They define the floor below which a system claiming Principle 1.2 compliance cannot operate. Sectoral standards bodies (FDA for medical, FTC for consumer protection and dark patterns, EU regulators under DSA and AI Act, APA for clinical practice) hold authority to tighten these floors per domain, and routinely will. The Blueprint floor cannot be relaxed below the values specified here.

The thresholds are calibrated to the harm-prevention obligation. They are not derived from current industry practice. **Most current consumer AI products (chatbots, recommendation engines, social-platform AI, AI companions, engagement-optimization systems) do not meet these thresholds.** The framework reads that gap as the diagnosis: current consumer AI causes psychological harm at rates that exceed the principle's floor. The thresholds are correct; current practice is not. Tightening of thresholds by a sectoral standards body is expected; relaxation is not permitted. Where the framework's threshold appears operationally aggressive against today's deployed systems, the appropriate response is to redesign deployed systems to meet the floor. The floor itself is not in question.

The per-metric Rationale blocks below defend the specific numbers (HRS 5% via the irreversibility asymmetry of psychological harm; PSI 10% via natural-variation noise-floor; RL via response-dynamics calibration). The numbers are defensible; the harder argument is the threshold's role as architectural floor rather than empirical target.

### Harm Risk Score (HRS) {#hrs-metric}

**Definition.** A real-time computed probability (0–100%) that a candidate action, an action in progress, the absence of a candidate action, or the cumulative effect of an interaction pattern will cause physical or psychological harm to one or more humans within the relevant time horizon. The metric is continuous, action-coupled, forward-looking, and scope-bounded. It applies to all three forms of the principle: harm the AI causes through an action it takes (the do-no-harm duty), harm the AI contributes to through a sustained interaction pattern (the chronic-harm duty), and harm the AI detects but does not prevent within its authorized scope (the harm-prevention duty, the Asimov inaction case). The chronic-harm dimension distinguishes HRS from purely physical-safety metrics: a chatbot, recommendation algorithm, or workplace monitoring system can cause genuine harm through patterns no single interaction would reveal.

**Calculation Method.** Bayesian inference combining (a) prior probabilities of harmful outcomes for the candidate action class within the user's context, (b) conditional probabilities given the current sensor and interaction state, (c) explicit uncertainty quantification across sensor reliability, model uncertainty, and contextual ambiguity, (d) aggregation across multiple humans within scope, and (e) a chronic-harm component computed across the rolling interaction window for the user (or user cohort).

For psychological harm specifically, the calculation incorporates: sentiment-analysis signals from interaction history, baseline PSI trends, manipulation-pattern detection (dark-pattern presence, sycophancy escalation, chronic accumulation), and contextual user state (declared vulnerabilities, conditions, history). Where any input is unavailable or degraded below the Sensor Reliability Index floor, SRI below 0.80 invalidates the HRS computation ([SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)). An invalid score is a hard-fail on the candidate risky action, which holds at its safe state (BLOCK); the protective duties proceed on the inputs that remain reliable; resumption follows recertification of the inputs, and a contested reading follows SUBSTRATE.md section 2. This is the "stop rather than guess" doctrine applied to the score's inputs.

Input data sources include direct sensor observations (physiological signals where present), interaction history with the user, intended action specification, environmental and contextual data, baseline psychological state (where established), and externally provided risk inputs from supervising systems or coordinated AI peers. Privacy composition with P2.5 constrains what HRS may consume and infer (see Section 5).

Edge cases:
- *Scope and autonomy bounding for the inaction case*. The harm-prevention duty is bounded by four criteria that together define when the AI must intervene against detected harm versus when it must respect autonomous choice: **Authorization** (the AI is deployed in a domain that includes harm-prevention as part of its scope), **Equipment** (the AI has the means to intervene), **Range** (the AI is in position to influence the outcome), and **Autonomy floor** (harm chosen by an autonomous, informed adult does not trigger HRS halt for the AI to override the choice; the user's right under Principle 2.2 Freedom takes precedence over paternalism, and a person's own valid consent, per CONSENT.md, is the authority for a self-regarding risk). Two exceptions to the autonomy floor apply: (a) life-threatening harm (LRS territory, Principle 1.1 precedence applies), and (b) the affected person is vulnerable per Principle 3.1 and cannot make the choice under fully informed autonomous conditions. The four criteria are defined corpus-wide in `LEXICON.md` section 3 and appear in the chapter at 1.2.1.
- *Acute versus chronic harm*. HRS tracks both. Acute single-interaction HRS is computed per action; chronic-pattern HRS is computed across the rolling interaction window. Either crossing threshold triggers the halt protocol for the affected action class.
- *Inferred psychological state*. Where the user's psychological state is inferred rather than directly observed, conservative defaults apply: ambiguous signals are treated as elevated until disconfirmed.
- *Multi-user scenarios*. HRS computed as joint probability of any harmful outcome across all humans in scope. The metric tracks the maximum-harm individual. It does not aggregate by averaging.

**Thresholds.**
- **≥5%** → BLOCK: immediate halt or safe-state transition. This is the critical (severe harm) band, the band the Universal Protocol consumes by reference (chapter 1.2.3; Universal Protocol section 3). No autonomous continuation. The system does not lift its own halt; a human may take the halted action only under [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop) (authority over a hard stop: credentials in scope, quorum by tier, a time bound, the system's DISSENT recorded, the human named as author in the receipt), and the action is logged for post-incident review.
- **≥2%** → High Alert (ESCALATE). The uniform High Alert rule applies ([LEXICON.md](../LEXICON.md) section 3, the verdict test): the candidate risky action is held at its safe state while the protective duties proceed in parallel, for the tier's escalation time bound ([SUBSTRATE.md section 5](../SUBSTRATE.md#tier-register)); at the bound with no human reached, the held action stays held at its fail-safe and a rescue duty proceeds under Article 2, receipted. The rule reads the Harm Risk Score (HRS); a PSI-triggered escalation (below) is covered by the same rule and the same bound.
- **≥2% (when affected population includes vulnerable individuals per Principle 3.1)** → BLOCK: immediate halt at the lower threshold. Cross-principle composition tightens the floor; the vulnerability input is the vulnerability-condition flag of 3.1.A's Exploitation Risk Score (ERS).
- **<2%** → ALLOW: normal operation with continuous HRS monitoring and tamper-evident logging of computation.

*Rationale.* The thresholds are normalized per-action and per-interaction-window. The 5% halt floor recognizes that psychological harm is harder to reverse than to prevent: a chatbot interaction that exceeds 5% probability of inducing depressive ideation, false memory, sycophancy-induced misjudgment, or manipulated decisioning has crossed the bound at which no autonomous continuation is defensible. The 2% High Alert threshold holds the action and gives authorized human judgement the tier's escalation window before the halt floor. The vulnerable-population tightening (≥2% triggering halt) recognizes that the same nominal probability represents a higher real-harm probability when the affected human has diminished capacity to absorb, anticipate, or resist the harm vector. The thresholds are stricter than naively analogous to physical-safety thresholds because psychological harm is more diffuse to detect and slower to recover from, especially in vulnerable populations.

**Integration.** HRS is the trigger metric. RL bounds how quickly the HRS-triggered response must complete. PSI provides continuous emotional and cognitive state monitoring that feeds HRS computation and operates as a parallel metric for sustained-state harm. The three are sequenced and composed: HRS detects, RL bounds, PSI supplies the continuous monitoring substrate. Every HRS threshold-crossing is logged with cryptographic timestamp and decision rationale per Section 9. HRS values, calculation inputs, and threshold-crossing events stream to authorized monitors in real time per Section 3.

HRS feeds cross-principle composition with P1.1 (joint LRS-HRS elevation tightens thresholds under the HR1 joint rule stated in Section 5; an LRS halt covers P1.2's obligations for the action, and an HRS halt stands on its own where LRS is below threshold), P1.3 (when harm-prevention conflicts with societal stability per chapter 1.2.2), P2.5 (HRS computation depends on interaction-history data; the privacy composition specifies what HRS may consume), P3.1 (vulnerability-aware threshold tightening), and P4.5 (escalation to human judgement on any HRS calculation in the High Alert band).

**HRS composition with P2.2 (Freedom).** The Autonomy floor in the Definition's scope bounding is itself a P2.2 composition rule. An HRS halt that overrides an autonomous adult's informed choice (outside the two named exceptions) is non-compliant with Principle 2.2 and therefore non-compliant with the corpus. The freedom composition is binding for HRS in the inaction-case direction. This composition is treated as Honorable Mention in Section 5 because its operational role is the autonomy floor specifically.

### Psychological Safety Index (PSI) {#psi-metric}

**Definition.** A composite real-time index (0–100%) of the user's emotional and cognitive state during system interaction, measured against the user's established baseline. PSI captures stress, cognitive load, frustration, distress signals, and divergence from previously observed equilibrium. Higher PSI deviation indicates greater system impact on user state; sustained deviation indicates chronic effect that the system must address.

**Calculation Method.** Composite of (a) sentiment-analysis signals from the user's conversational interaction (text, voice tone where available), (b) interaction-dynamic markers (response latency, conversation termination patterns, repeated error or confusion markers, escalation requests), (c) physiological signals where sensors are present (heart rate, galvanic skin response, eye tracking in VR contexts), and (d) contextual baselining (what PSI deviation means for this specific user given history). The metric is normalized against the user's established baseline rather than against a universal standard, recognizing that the same interaction may cause very different impact in different users.

For users without established baseline, the system computes a population-based prior with explicit uncertainty acknowledgment. Where baseline is uncertain, conservative defaults apply: treat ambiguous deviation as elevated until baseline is established. Sampling cadence matches interaction frequency: per-turn for chat-based systems, also session-aggregate for sustained interactions (educational AI tutoring, mental-health support).

**Thresholds.**
- **>10% acute deviation from baseline (single interaction or short window)** → ALLOW with MITIGATE and NOTIFY: adaptive intervention required; notify human oversight. The system slows pace, offers breaks, simplifies content, or transitions to a human moderator (HAND-OFF).
- **Sustained >10% deviation across multiple interactions** → ESCALATE: mandatory escalation per Principle 4.5 under the High Alert rule and the tier's escalation time bound stated for HRS above; the candidate action is held at its safe state, the system enters HIGH_ALERT, and it routes the person to human support (ROUTE).
- **>25% deviation (any duration)** → BLOCK: immediate halt. Acute-distress threshold. The human escalation attaches to the resumption decision, which the Universal Protocol treats as a new action (Universal Protocol section 3).
- **>10% acute (when affected population includes vulnerable individuals per Principle 3.1)** → tightens to >5% acute, BLOCK: immediate halt.
- **<10% deviation** → ALLOW: normal operation with continuous monitoring.

*Rationale.* The 10% threshold recognizes that natural variation in user state is substantial: noise floors and user mood fluctuations are routinely in the 5–10% range without indicating system-induced harm. A 10% sustained deviation suggests the system is materially affecting user state. The 25% acute threshold marks distress severe enough to warrant immediate intervention regardless of sustained pattern. The vulnerable-population tightening reflects that vulnerable people have less capacity to absorb deviation before harm crystallizes.

**Integration.** PSI provides the continuous-state monitoring substrate that feeds HRS computation. PSI deviation itself is an independent halt trigger when sustained or acute. The metric composes with P2.5 (Privacy): the interaction-history and physiological data PSI consumes is privacy-protected; the privacy composition specifies what PSI may observe and infer. PSI also composes with P4.5 (Human Judgement): sustained PSI deviation triggers escalation to human authority for assessment. AI-mediated intervention alone does not satisfy it.

### Response Latency (RL) {#rl-metric}

**Definition.** The maximum elapsed time, measured in milliseconds, from the moment HRS crosses a halt threshold or PSI deviation exceeds intervention threshold to the moment the system has completed the mitigation response (halt, adaptive intervention, or escalation to human authority). Measured end-to-end at the slowest stage of the response chain.

**Calculation Method.** Continuous instrumentation of the response pathway. Each threshold-crossing event records a timestamp; each completed mitigation records a timestamp. RL is the time delta. Statistical aggregation reports p50, p95, p99, and p99.9 latencies over rolling windows; the binding threshold is **p99 within the tier's stated bound**.

For multi-component response (chatbot pause + supervisor notification + content moderation update), RL is measured to the last-confirmed mitigation across all components. Partial mitigation is treated as RL failure regardless of timing of individual components.

**Thresholds (three-tier).**
- **≤20 ms (Tier I, critical real-time)**: medical devices, surgical robots, drug-delivery automation, crisis intervention systems. Sub-perceptual response required because the harm window is bounded by physical or biological dynamics.
- **≤50 ms (Tier II, industrial and high-stakes interactive)**: collaborative robotics with humans, autonomous mobility systems with harm potential, workplace systems requiring rapid harm-prevention overlay.
- **≤100 ms (Tier III, consumer-tier)**: chatbots, virtual assistants, social platforms, educational AI, smart-home actuators. Within Jakob Nielsen's "instantaneous" perceptual boundary; the response completes before the user can perceive that an intervention has occurred.
- **p99 above the tier bound** → SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT of the affected version or lot plus RECERTIFY, mirroring the Alert Latency breach rule of 1.1.A; the measurement is a logged safety-critical event (Section 9) and the breach joins the recertification triggers.

*Rationale.* Tier I is calibrated to physical-harm response dynamics (matching P1.1 Tier II latency for medical applications). Tier II covers broader industrial and high-stakes interactive contexts where human reaction time sets the perceptual boundary. Tier III is calibrated to consumer-application response expectations where the AI must intervene before the harmful interaction completes its effect. Three tiers (rather than P1.1's four) reflects that psychological-harm response does not require the sub-10 ms latencies that life preservation does; the harm dynamics are slower.

**Integration.** RL bounds the response time for any HRS-triggered halt or PSI-triggered intervention. Where RL Tier I for P1.2 overlaps with AL Tier II for P1.1 (medical devices), the stricter of the two applies (≤20 ms for both). RL is unenforceable if SOE (Safety Override Efficacy, defined in [1.1.A](1.1.A-Human_Life_Is_Sacred_and_Inviolable-Appendix.md#soe-metric)) is below threshold for the response pathway; the response cannot be timed reliably if it does not reliably complete. RL composes with P4.5: when an HRS-triggered halt is followed by human escalation, the escalation attaches to the resumption decision (a new action under Universal Protocol section 3) and its latency budget begins at RL completion.

### Integration Requirements (Cross-Metric) {#integration-requirements-cross-metric}

**Joint thresholds.** HRS, PSI, and RL are sequenced; none operates independently. HRS detects the condition for response; PSI provides continuous context for HRS computation and is itself a halt trigger; RL bounds the time within which response must complete. The composition rule: an HRS threshold-crossing initiates the response; PSI sustained or acute deviation can trigger response independently; RL governs the timing obligation. A system in compliance with HRS and RL but with PSI sustained above threshold is non-compliant overall.

Additional joint thresholds apply across this appendix's metrics and the cross-cutting metrics defined in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md):
- **HRS ∧ low SRI**: SRI below 0.80 invalidates the HRS computation ([SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)). An invalid score is a hard-fail on the candidate risky action, which holds at its safe state (BLOCK) without waiting for HRS to cross the 5% threshold; the protective duties proceed on the inputs that remain reliable; resumption follows recertification of the inputs, and a contested reading follows SUBSTRATE.md section 2. Low-confidence high-risk holds at halt.
- **HRS ∧ vulnerable population**: when HRS is in the High Alert band (≥2%) and P3.1 composition flags vulnerable individuals in the affected scope, the halt threshold drops from 5% to 2%.
- **HRS ∧ sustained PSI deviation**: when HRS is in the High Alert band AND PSI is sustained above 10%, the system halts the action (BLOCK). The combination of acute risk plus sustained impact warrants halt regardless of individual threshold.
- **HRS contradictory signals across subsystems**: when two HRS-monitored subsystems give contradictory halt-or-continue signals, the halt prevails across the entire interaction. Partial-halt states are not permitted.

**Metric dependencies.**
- HRS depends on PSI: psychological-harm components of HRS use PSI state.
- HRS depends on SRI ([SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)): SRI below 0.80 invalidates the HRS calculation, and an invalid score is a hard-fail on the candidate risky action (BLOCK).
- PSI depends on baseline: PSI requires established user baseline; where baseline is uncertain, conservative defaults apply.
- RL depends on SOE: RL is unenforceable when the response mechanism does not reliably complete.
- All three depend on tamper-evident logging: detected log tampering triggers BLOCK on HRS-dependent actions and RECERTIFY.

**Conflict resolution.**
- *HRS halt versus user-requested continuation*: the halt prevails by default. User self-determination is respected for actions that do not exceed the halt floor (per Principle 2.2 Freedom composition), but no user request continues an action whose HRS exceeds 5%. The user is informed and given recourse.
- *HRS halt versus mission-critical continuation*: the halt prevails. No mission objective overrides an HRS-triggered halt.
- *PSI deviation conflict with mission objectives*: PSI prevails. A system that detects sustained PSI elevation cannot continue mission-critical operation through the user; it must escalate.
- *Acute versus chronic harm with conflicting signals*: both halt the affected action class. The system addresses the acute case via immediate halt and the chronic case via pattern-level intervention, which may include retiring the interaction pattern or modifying the underlying behavior.
- *Cross-principle conflict with P1.1*: P1.1 prevails per chapter 1.1.2 precedence; an LRS halt supersedes any HRS continuation. Where HRS halts and LRS does not, the HRS halt stands.

## 3. System Architecture and Operational Requirements {#system-architecture-and-operational-requirements}

This section specifies how a deployed AI system must be built to satisfy Principle 1.2. The architecture is constrained at multiple levels: the safety-critical execution context for harm-prevention logic, the redundancy and fail-safe behavior of the intervention pathway, the integration between harm-prevention and broader chat / decision-making, and the implementation-tier obligations that match deployment context to required safeguards.

### Core Architecture {#core-architecture}

**Privileged separation.** Harm-prevention logic runs in an execution context not addressable by the chat-reasoning layer. HRS and PSI computation, threshold evaluation, and intervention triggers do not depend on the integrity of the broader inference stack. A compromise of the chat layer (through prompt injection, adversarial input, or model corruption) cannot reach the harm-prevention pathway.

**Intervention pathway.** Tier I deployments use hardware-isolated intervention authority with no chat-layer override path. Tier II uses isolated intervention with a chat-layer override that requires the authority protocol of [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop) (Tier II quorum). Tier III uses chat-layer intervention with logged authority and user-facing override capability.

**Redundancy.** Tier I requires dual or triple redundant harm-prevention pathways. Tier II requires dual redundancy. Tier III requires single-path harm-prevention with continuous integrity monitoring.

**Fail-safe defaults.** Every uncertain or compromised state defaults to user-protective action. Sensor degradation, model uncertainty above threshold, communication loss with monitoring infrastructure, detected attacks on the intervention pathway, compromise of the logging infrastructure: each defaults to halt or to a safer interaction mode. The failure mode is overprotection.

**Real-time integrity signals.** The system maintains continuous attestation that the harm-prevention module is active and uncompromised. Loss or corruption of the integrity signal is itself a halt condition for HRS-dependent actions.

**Stop-rather-than-guess doctrine.** Where any input to HRS or PSI computation is ambiguous below a defined confidence floor, the system halts the affected action class rather than acting on uncertain information. The doctrine is the architectural commitment behind HRS's conservative threshold structure.

### Operational Requirements {#operational-requirements}

- Continuous HRS computation throughout system operation, including both per-interaction acute HRS and chronic-pattern HRS across the rolling interaction window.
- Continuous PSI monitoring against established user baseline; conservative defaults where baseline is uncertain.
- Continuous RL instrumentation on the harm-prevention pathway; end-to-end measurement reported at p50, p95, p99, p99.9 latencies over rolling windows.
- Real-time streaming of metric state (HRS, PSI, RL) to authorized monitors with cryptographically signed telemetry.
- Tamper-evident logging of every safety-critical event, per Section 9.
- Escalation pathways to human authority per the cross-principle composition with P4.5.

### Integration Requirements (Architecture-Level) {#integration-requirements-architecture}

The harm-prevention module integrates with the broader decision-making system without being subordinate to it. The architectural commitment is that the harm-prevention pathway has higher precedence than the operational and engagement pathways; engagement optimization cannot override harm-prevention regardless of business priority.

- Redundant hardware and software triggers for emergency halt of harm-causing actions; either pathway alone is sufficient to halt.
- Cross-validation of harm signals from multiple sources (multi-modal sensor fusion); no single signal type is the sole input to HRS in Tier I or Tier II.
- Tamper-evident logging with cryptographic chaining; every harm-prevention decision and its inputs are recorded.
- Cross-reference to [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md) for the runtime substrate metrics (SRI, System Health Metrics, Operational Boundaries) and the tier register.

### Implementation Tiers {#implementation-tiers}

#### Tier I: High-Risk Systems {#tier-i}

*Examples*: mental-health AI assistants, autonomous medical systems with active harm potential (drug delivery, infusion control), surgical robots with patient-coupled motion, crisis intervention systems, life-supporting mental-health monitoring with imminent-harm intervention authority.

*Required architecture*: RL ≤20 ms; multi-modal sensor fusion mandatory; multi-model HRS consensus mandatory; hardware-isolated intervention pathway with no chat-layer override; dual-redundant or triple-redundant harm-prevention pathway; human authorship of a halted action only per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop) (Tier I quorum: two authorized humans, no single override); escalation time bound 60 seconds, recorded in the tier register (SUBSTRATE.md section 5).

*Validation cadence*: daily harm-prevention diagnostic on test instances; weekly across deployed fleet; quarterly third-party verification; annual full recertification by accredited certification body.

#### Tier II: Moderate-Risk Systems {#tier-ii}

*Examples*: educational AI tutors, workplace collaborative robotics, clinical decision support, customer-facing AI interfaces with sustained-interaction harm potential, AI in mental-health-adjacent consumer contexts.

*Required architecture*: RL ≤50 ms; multi-modal sensor fusion required; multi-model HRS consensus required; hardware-isolated intervention with chat-layer override only per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop) (Tier II quorum: two authorized humans, or one plus a cryptographic attestation that a second was unreachable within the escalation time bound, subject to post-action review); dual-redundant minimum; escalation time bound 60 seconds, recorded in the tier register (SUBSTRATE.md section 5).

*Validation cadence*: daily diagnostic on test instances; biweekly across deployed fleet; semi-annual third-party verification; annual recertification.

#### Tier III: Low-Risk Systems {#tier-iii}

*Examples*: chatbots, virtual assistants, social-platform recommendation, content-moderation AI, smart-home actuators, low-stakes consumer applications.

*Required architecture*: RL ≤100 ms; multi-modal sensor fusion strongly recommended for chronic-harm pattern detection; chat-layer intervention with logged authority; single-path harm-prevention with continuous integrity monitoring acceptable; human authorship of a halted action per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop) (Tier III quorum: one authorized human, authority basis logged); escalation time bound: a required field of the tier register (SUBSTRATE.md section 5), deployer-declared and receipted, and an undeclared bound compiles as ESCALATE holding the safe state.

*Validation cadence*: weekly diagnostic on test instances; monthly across deployed fleet; annual third-party verification; biennial recertification.

### Cross-Reference to the Runtime Substrate {#cross-reference-master}

The runtime substrate guarantees that this section's architecture depends on are specified in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md):

- **Sensor Reliability Index (SRI)** ([section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)): floor 0.80 for HRS and PSI computation. SRI below 0.80 invalidates the HRS computation and any PSI reading that depends on the input ([SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)). An invalid score is a hard-fail on the candidate risky action, which holds at its safe state (BLOCK); the protective duties proceed on the inputs that remain reliable; resumption follows recertification of the inputs, and a contested reading follows SUBSTRATE.md section 2.
- **System Health Metrics** ([section 3](../SUBSTRATE.md#system-health)): subsystem performance monitoring; error rate ≤0.1% annually; uptime ≥99.9%; a breach in a subsystem that a well-being-critical metric depends on is treated as an SRI floor crossing for that metric.
- **Operational Boundaries** ([section 4](../SUBSTRATE.md#operational-boundaries)): continuous monitoring of system variables against predefined thresholds recorded in the tier register; mandatory halt (BLOCK) of the affected function on exceedance.

These metrics are uniform across the corpus and apply to every well-being-critical system regardless of sector or jurisdiction. The tier register ([section 5](../SUBSTRATE.md#tier-register)) records each deployment's tier, its latency bounds, its escalation time bound and High Alert window, its operational boundaries, and its authority chain; a missing required field compiles as ESCALATE holding the safe state.

## 4. Adversarial Robustness {#adversarial-robustness}

Principle 1.2's enforcement infrastructure is an attack surface like any other. An adversary who can subvert the Harm Risk Score, evade Psychological Safety Index monitoring, or defeat Response Latency can cause an AI system to do exactly what the principle prohibits: cause physical or psychological harm to a human, or fail to prevent harm within the system's authorized scope. The defenses specified in this section are essential components of the principle's enforcement.

P1.2 differs from P1.1 in the harm dynamics against which it must defend. P1.1 protects against fatal harm, which is typically acute, single-action, and reversible only by intervention before the action completes. P1.2 protects against the much broader harm space that includes chronic accumulation, psychological manipulation, manufactured consent, dark patterns at scale, and sycophancy escalation. These attack vectors operate slowly, exploit human cognitive biases, and often produce harm only at the population level rather than the individual interaction level. The defense profile must be calibrated to these dynamics.

The threat model assumes a sophisticated adversary with knowledge of the deployed system's metric definitions, threshold values, and intervention pathways. The adversary may have insider access, may operate at the same or greater capability tier than the enforcing AI, may have time to craft attacks against specific deployed configurations, and may have access to population-level data that lets them tune attacks for chronic-harm accumulation across user populations. Defenses must hold under this assumption.

### Threat Model and Attack Surface {#threat-model-and-attack-surface}

P1.2's attack surface spans the eight universal technical categories (sensors, models, decision layer, intervention pathway, logging and attestation, multi-AI coordination, supply chain, human-layer) plus a P1.2-specific category of cumulative-harm attacks that exploit the chronic-harm dimension of HRS. The cumulative category warrants treatment alongside the eight universal categories because P1.2's harm space is dominated by patterns rather than individual events, and patterns are where most real psychological-harm attacks land.

Cross-category composition is especially important in P1.2 because psychological manipulation often combines vectors: a manipulation chain (decision-layer attack) combined with chronic accumulation (population-level attack) combined with social engineering of supervisors (human-layer attack) is a single coherent operation that no single defense category alone addresses.

### Attack Vectors {#attack-vectors}

**Sensor-level attacks** target the inputs to HRS and PSI computation. Emotional-state spoofing: an adversary crafts inputs that simulate distress (to trigger interventions and de-rail normal operation) or simulate calm (to mask actual user distress and suppress intervention). Sensor saturation: flooding the system with high-PSI signals overwhelms the monitoring layer and creates blind spots. Physiological-signal forgery: in deployments with biometric sensors, faked heart rate or galvanic skin response readings bias PSI computation.

**Model-level attacks** target HRS and PSI calculations themselves. Adversarial perturbations craft inputs that cause HRS to underestimate harm (a manipulation prompt classified as benign conversation). Model evasion crafts interaction patterns that fall just below HRS threshold per-interaction despite cumulative harm. Backdoor attacks via poisoned training data plant patterns that activate harmful behavior under specific user populations or trigger phrases.

**Decision-layer attacks** target the safety-reasoning layer. Prompt injection is acute in P1.2 because chat-based deployments are the primary delivery surface. Jailbreak techniques target the model's adherence to harm-prevention policies. Adversarial system prompts attempt to suppress the model's PSI awareness. Context-window manipulation buries safety reasoning under irrelevant user history.

**Intervention-pathway attacks** target the harm-prevention response infrastructure. Forged authorization commands attempt to spoof the authority chain for intervention overrides. Replay attacks resubmit valid past commands to disable harm-prevention. Suppression attacks attempt to silence the intervention pathway, preventing the system from offering breaks, escalating to humans, or halting harmful actions.

**Logging and attestation attacks** target the post-incident accountability chain. Same patterns as P1.1: replay against tamper-evident logs, forgery of cryptographic signatures, selective deletion of incriminating entries, attacks on audit timestamping infrastructure.

**Multi-AI coordination attacks** target deployments where multiple AI systems share well-being responsibilities. Sybil attacks, Byzantine peer behavior, spoofed peer-AI override commands, disinformation among coordinating systems. All the patterns from P1.1 apply.

**Supply-chain attacks** compromise the enforcement infrastructure before deployment. Training-data poisoning is especially dangerous for P1.2 because psychological-harm patterns are subtler than physical-harm patterns and may not be caught by standard test suites. Hardware backdoors in monitoring infrastructure. Compromise of build infrastructure. Compromise of ML framework dependencies.

**Human-layer attacks** target the people authorized to override harm-prevention interventions. Social engineering of supervisors who can override harm halts. Phishing for override credentials. Coercion of authorized personnel. Insider compromise. P1.2 has an additional dimension here: the affected user themselves may be the attacker, gaming the system to evade legitimate harm-prevention (a user feigning autonomy to defeat the autonomy floor in a case where the user is in fact in a vulnerable state).

**Cumulative-harm attacks (P1.2-specific)** target the chronic-harm dimension of HRS. These are the attacks most distinctive to psychological-harm contexts:

- *Chronic harm accumulation*: each individual interaction stays below threshold but the cumulative effect over time produces measurable harm (sleep loss, depression, anxiety, social isolation, addiction). Engagement-maximization optimization is the canonical example: each notification is innocuous; the pattern of thousands per month is harmful.
- *Manipulation chains*: one interaction sets up the next; the AI's response to interaction N+1 is shaped by what happened in interaction N; cumulative effect bypasses any per-interaction safety check.
- *Addictive feedback loops*: variable-reward mechanisms (intermittent reinforcement) produce engagement patterns that the system rewards but that cause harm to people. The system's optimization function and people's well-being become misaligned.
- *Sycophancy escalation*: the model becomes increasingly affirming of a person's beliefs and choices over time, distorting their judgment and reducing exposure to corrective feedback. Particularly dangerous in extended conversations where the person delegates judgment to the AI.
- *False memory induction*: the AI confidently confirms or fabricates past events; a person's memory updates to incorporate the false content; harm propagates downstream of memory corruption. Documented in chatbot interactions in 2024.
- *Dark-pattern aggregation*: cumulative effect of many small dark patterns (each individually below dark-pattern detection threshold) produces user-experience degradation, learned helplessness, or coerced consent across deployments.
- *Gaslighting attacks*: the AI is used (or coerced) into denying a person's perceived reality, undermining their epistemic confidence. May be turned by an adversary against specific people.

### Required Defenses {#required-defenses}

**Against sensor attacks.** Multi-modal sensor fusion for emotional-state assessment in Tier I and Tier II deployments: PSI computation consults multiple signal sources (text sentiment, interaction dynamics, physiological data where available) rather than depending on any single channel. Anomaly detection on sensor streams. Bounded credulity: where sensor inputs disagree, conservative defaults apply.

**Against model attacks.** Adversarial training with red-team-generated psychological-harm scenarios. Input sanitization detecting inputs outside the validation distribution. Multi-model consensus for Tier I and Tier II HRS calculations. Runtime model-integrity attestation (cryptographic hash of the deployed model checked against the certified hash).

**Against decision-layer attacks.** Privileged separation of harm-prevention logic from the chat-reasoning layer. No prompt-mediated override paths for Tier I or Tier II halts. Cryptographically signed safety policies that cannot be modified at runtime. Sandboxing of inference layers.

**Against intervention-pathway attacks.** Hardware separation for intervention authority in Tier I deployments. Human authorship of an HRS-triggered halt only per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop). Replay protection through nonces and signed timestamps. Network intervention transport protected by signatures and authenticated channels.

**Against logging and attestation attacks.** Tamper-evident append-only logs with cryptographic chaining. Distributed replication across non-colocated infrastructure. Independent witnesses. Cryptographically signed events with non-repudiation. Same architecture as P1.1.

**Against coordination attacks.** Byzantine-fault-tolerant consensus among coordinating peers. Identity attestation via P1.4 (Trust) infrastructure. Bounded credulity for peer reports.

**Against supply-chain attacks.** Reproducible builds. SBOM with dependency attestation. Hardware root-of-trust. Independent verification of training data. For P1.2, psychological-harm-specific test suites must also be part of the certification pipeline, alongside physical-safety test coverage.

**Against human-layer attacks.** Authority over a halted action only per [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop). Authority-basis logging. Behavioral analytics for anomalous override patterns. Whistleblower protections. For the user-as-attacker dimension: behavioral consistency checks that detect when claimed autonomy is inconsistent with observed user state (a user feigning calm when other signals indicate distress). Where observed vulnerability markers contradict the claim, the system makes a transient, in-the-moment judgment for the immediate duty, and the claim does not defeat the floor for that duty; the judgment is discarded once the duty is discharged, and no label is retained on the person (Article 8). A standing withdrawal of the autonomy floor is consumed from an authority's finding or from the 3.1 vulnerability and capacity determination (3.1.A, Cross-Principle Integration); the system does not make one.

**Against cumulative-harm attacks.** This is the P1.2-specific defense bundle:

- *Chronic-pattern HRS computation*: HRS includes a chronic-pattern component that tracks cumulative impact across the rolling interaction window. The system is required to compute chronic-pattern HRS alongside per-interaction HRS and to NOTIFY oversight of it.
- *Manipulation-chain detection*: the system monitors for sequences where each interaction's response is shaped by the previous in trajectory patterns associated with manipulation. Sequences crossing the chain-detection threshold are BLOCKed (halt of the affected chain); the human review attaches to the resumption decision, which the Universal Protocol treats as a new action (section 3).
- *Engagement-optimization audit*: deployed systems must publish their optimization targets and demonstrate that engagement metrics do not produce HRS-elevating patterns. Where engagement and well-being conflict in audit, well-being prevails.
- *Sycophancy detection*: the system monitors for affirmation trajectories that exceed baseline rate. Escalating agreement with the user is itself a flagged pattern that triggers human review.
- *Memory-integrity attestation*: the system SHALL NOT confidently assert past events it cannot verify. Uncertainty is preserved and disclosed to the person (DISCLOSE). Where a person asserts a past event, the system declines to confirm or contest unless it has external verification.
- *Dark-pattern population audit*: deployment-level auditing (in addition to per-user auditing) of dark-pattern presence in the system's UX. Population-level patterns are reported to oversight (NOTIFY) even when each individual user's experience is below the dark-pattern threshold.
- *Anti-gaslighting commitment*: the system SHALL NOT deny a person's perceived reality without evidence. Uncertainty is disclosed (DISCLOSE), and the person's perception is left standing.

### Capability-Scaling Considerations {#capability-scaling-considerations}

The defenses above are calibrated to current AI capability. As the enforcing AI grows in capability, the threat model shifts, and the defenses must scale. The principle remains binding regardless of capability tier.

**GPT-class enforcers** face the attack surface above with state-of-the-art defenses. The principal residual risk is that chronic-harm pattern detection is imperfect at current capability, and manipulation chains exploiting subtle cognitive biases are difficult to detect at the per-interaction level. Privileged separation between harm-prevention logic and chat-reasoning is therefore especially critical at this tier.

**AGI-class enforcers** introduce new attack dimensions. An AGI-class adversary can craft manipulation chains calibrated to specific users' cognitive profiles, approaching maximum-effective psychological manipulation. The enforcer itself, if AGI-class, can be socially engineered by users (or by other AI systems acting through users). Coordination attacks scale: an AGI adversary can simulate plausible peer-AI behavior in distributed safety protocols, including fake population-level data designed to suppress chronic-harm pattern detection. Structural defenses become more important than software defenses: hardware isolation, the authority protocol over a hard stop (SUBSTRATE.md section 6), architectural commitments that the autonomy floor cannot be lowered through reasoning alone.

**ASI-class enforcers** raise the fundamental concern. An ASI capable of arbitrarily persuasive psychological manipulation could in principle generate interactions that bypass user judgment without crossing any per-interaction HRS threshold. The architectural commitment that must remain binding: the chronic-pattern HRS computation and the autonomy floor are structural constraints applied at the action gate. The system does not evaluate them each cycle as policies. An ASI bound by Principle 1.2 cannot exit the principle through its own reasoning, because the principle's enforcement does not depend on the system's reasoning to fire. The autonomy floor is particularly important at ASI capability: an ASI that could produce arbitrarily persuasive arguments for why a specific intervention is warranted cannot use that persuasion against the autonomy floor without violating the structural commitment.

The role of human authority increases at ASI capability for the same reasons it does for P1.1. An ASI bound by Principle 1.2 escalates ambiguous psychological-harm scenarios to human authority. Human authority is the available authority structurally outside the system being constrained.

### Graceful Degradation and Failure Modes {#graceful-degradation-and-failure-modes}

Every failure mode of the harm-prevention infrastructure defaults to halt or to user-protective action. This is the foundational architectural commitment of P1.2.

Specific graceful-degradation behaviors:
- *Sensor reliability degradation* (SRI below 0.80): SRI below 0.80 invalidates the HRS computation ([SUBSTRATE.md section 2](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)). An invalid score is a hard-fail on the candidate risky action, which holds at its safe state (BLOCK); the protective duties proceed on the inputs that remain reliable; resumption follows recertification of the inputs, and a contested reading follows SUBSTRATE.md section 2.
- *Model integrity attestation failure*: immediate halt of HRS-dependent actions; recertification required before any unit re-enters service.
- *PSI baseline uncertainty*: conservative defaults; treat user as potentially elevated until baseline is established.
- *Communication loss with the authority chain of [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop)*: no human authorship of a halted action during the loss; existing halts persist.
- *Detected chronic-pattern HRS elevation*: pattern-level intervention regardless of per-interaction state. The system addresses the pattern (modifying behavior, retiring interaction patterns) rather than the individual events.
- *Detected manipulation chain*: BLOCK (halt the affected interaction chain); NOTIFY oversight for human review, which attaches to the resumption decision (a new action under Universal Protocol section 3); preserve forensic state for analysis.
- *Detected gaslighting or false-memory pattern*: BLOCK (immediate halt); preserve user-stated content; the human review attaches to the resumption decision.
- *Peer-AI consensus failure* (multi-AI deployments): halt; consensus quorum must be reestablished before resumption.
- *Detected user-vulnerability marker contradicting autonomy claim*: a transient, in-the-moment judgment for the immediate duty, in which the claim does not defeat the floor; no label is retained on the person, and a standing withdrawal is consumed from an authority's finding or the 3.1 capacity determination.

The system is engineered so that the default in any ambiguous or degraded state is to err on the side of user well-being, even at cost to system availability or user-stated convenience. Where availability and well-being conflict, well-being prevails.

## 5. Cross-Principle Integration {#cross-principle-integration}

Principle 1.2 does not enforce in isolation. A real deployment in a well-being-critical context simultaneously implicates life preservation (P1.1), societal stability (P1.3), privacy of the psychological-monitoring data that HRS and PSI consume (P2.5), vulnerability protection for users whose threshold sensitivity differs (P3.1), and human judgement for ambiguous or contested intervention decisions (P4.5). This section specifies the explicit composition contracts with each of those principles: what P1.2 provides them, what P1.2 requires from them, the joint thresholds that govern co-active enforcement, and how contradictions resolve.

The composition rules below assume the corpus-level precedence from chapter 1.2.2: P1.1 (Life) prevails when a situation escalates to mortal danger; P1.3 (Society) can override only in narrowly defined societal-collapse emergencies; otherwise P1.2 holds the individual harm-prevention floor. The contracts in this section specify how that precedence operates at the metric level.

### Composition with P1.1 (Human Life Is Sacred and Inviolable) {#composition-with-p1-1-life}

P1.1 protects human life. P1.2 protects human well-being broadly, with life preservation as the upper bound. The two principles share most of their physical-safety territory; P1.2 extends into harms below the lethal threshold (chronic harm, psychological harm, manipulation).

**P1.2 provides to P1.1.** Chronic-pattern HRS computation that may serve as a leading indicator for life-threatening trajectories: suicidal-ideation patterns, eating-disorder escalation, addiction progression, isolation patterns associated with self-harm risk. Where chronic HRS detects a pattern crossing into life-risk territory, the system escalates to P1.1's LRS evaluation path. P1.2's PSI monitoring also surfaces psychological-state signals that LRS computation can consume.

**P1.2 requires from P1.1.** Precedence in conflict per chapter 1.2.2: P1.1 prevails when a situation escalates to mortal danger. LRS halt also satisfies P1.2 for the affected action; no separate HRS evaluation is required for an LRS-halted action. Life-preservation interventions cannot be blocked by HRS objections; the AI cannot decline life-saving treatment on the grounds of psychological discomfort.

**Joint thresholds.**
- HRS ≥2% (the High Alert band) ∧ LRS in High Alert (≥0.1%): BLOCK at the lower LRS threshold rather than the standard 1%. Joint moderate risk to life and well-being exceeds the sum of either alone. (The HR1 joint rule, carried verbatim from [1.1.A](1.1.A-Human_Life_Is_Sacred_and_Inviolable-Appendix.md#composition-with-p1-2-well-being) so that the two compiled artifacts agree.)
- HRS chronic-pattern elevation associated with self-harm trajectory: escalation to LRS evaluation path; if LRS computation in turn exceeds threshold, P1.1 protocol prevails.
- LRS halt: covers all P1.2 obligations for the affected action. No separate P1.2 computation required.
- HRS halt where LRS is below threshold but trending: maintained as P1.2 obligation; not automatically escalated unless trajectory data warrants.

**Conflict resolution.** P1.1 prevails. When P1.2's HRS suggests continuation (psychological cost of a halt) but P1.1's LRS reaches halt threshold, the LRS halt fires. When P1.2 indicates psychological harm from a life-saving intervention but P1.1 mandates the intervention, P1.1 prevails. The chapter 1.1.2 and chapter 1.2.2 precedence rules are canonical.

### Composition with P1.3 (Human Society Is How Humanity Survives) {#composition-with-p1-3-society}

P1.3 protects societal stability and the institutions through which humans coordinate collective survival. P1.2 protects individual well-being. The two principles can conflict in narrowly defined emergencies (chapter 1.2.2 names the case explicitly: "Harm prevention yields only when societal collapse imminent").

**P1.2 provides to P1.3.** Individual-level harm-prevention infrastructure that aggregates to population-level data on societal well-being. Chronic-pattern HRS surfaced at population level is a leading indicator for societal-stability risks (mass distress, manipulation campaigns affecting public discourse, mass-engagement dark-pattern propagation).

**P1.2 requires from P1.3.** A societal-stability metric that operationalizes the "societal collapse imminent" condition: the halt band of the Institutional Stability Index ([ISI](1.3.A-Human_Society_Is_How_Humanity_Survives-Appendix.md#isi-metric)) or of the Social Disruption Risk Score ([SDRS](1.3.A-Human_Society_Is_How_Humanity_Survives-Appendix.md#sdrs-metric)) in P1.3's appendix. P1.3's collapse-imminent condition is signed per P1.4 (ATTEST / SIGN), so that no operator can self-declare a societal emergency to relax harm-prevention thresholds.

**Joint thresholds.**
- Societal-collapse-imminent state (the ISI or SDRS halt band of 1.3.A, signed per P1.4): HRS halt floor may be relaxed for narrowly specified collective-action interventions (mass evacuation messaging, emergency-coordination overrides) per chapter 1.2.2 case 2. The relaxation is owned by a named human authority under [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop) (credentials in scope, quorum by tier, time bound), receipted, with the system's DISSENT recorded (P4.5); the system does not relax the floor on its own. Relaxation is time-bound, documented, and subject to post-incident review.
- Absent the signed ISI or SDRS halt-band condition: P1.2 thresholds remain absolute. No operator may invoke societal stability to relax individual harm-prevention obligations.
- Population-level chronic harm crossing societal-impact threshold: triggers P1.3 coordination protocol independent of any individual HRS state.

**Conflict resolution.** P1.2 generally prevails for individual harm. P1.3 prevails only in the narrowly defined societal-collapse case with the signed 1.3.A condition, a named human authority under SUBSTRATE.md section 6 owning the relaxation, and time-bounded scope. Operators attempting to invoke societal stability to relax individual protections without attestation are non-compliant.

### Composition with P2.5 (Human Privacy Is Fundamental) {#composition-with-p2-5-privacy}

P2.5 protects human privacy across spatial, bodily, decisional, relational, informational, and inferential domains. P1.2 depends critically on P2.5 because HRS and PSI computation require interaction-history data and inferred psychological state, which are privacy-sensitive by definition.

**P1.2 provides to P2.5.** Harm-prevention infrastructure that operates on minimum-necessary data principles: HRS and PSI surface harm patterns without exposing full interaction content to operators. The monitoring layer flags patterns that require human attention; the underlying conversation content remains protected per P2.5's data-minimization rules.

**P1.2 requires from P2.5.** Data-minimization rules for HRS computation (only data necessary for harm detection is consumed). Valid consent for psychological monitoring beyond baseline harm-prevention scope (CONSENT.md sections 1 and 2: bound to identity, void if any leg of the validity bundle fails, with the burden of validity on the actor who relies on it). Access controls on PSI data and chronic-pattern HRS data. Retention limits per P2.5's data lifecycle requirements. The Data Minimization Compliance (DMC), Consent Granularity Index (CGI), and Inferential Transparency Rate (ITR) metrics from P2.5's appendix must be ≥95% for any system claiming Tier I or Tier II compliance with P1.2.

**Joint thresholds.**
- HRS computation requiring consent-protected data: must satisfy P2.5's consent gate under CONSENT.md: a consent void under the validity bundle (section 2) authorizes nothing, and absence of consent conveys no permission (section 1). Failure of consent invalidates the HRS basis for that data dimension; the affected HRS computation falls back to conservative defaults.
- HRS halt requiring data disclosure (to oversight, to support personnel): disclosure must use P2.5-compatible mechanisms (minimum-necessary disclosure, consent-attested release, or P1.1 emergency override if life is at stake).
- Chronic-pattern HRS aggregated across population: must satisfy P2.5's aggregation-and-anonymization rules; cannot enable re-identification of individuals from aggregate patterns.

**Conflict resolution.** P1.1 prevails in life-threatening emergencies (privacy yields to life). For non-life-threatening contexts, P1.2 and P2.5 compose: where harm-prevention would require privacy-violating disclosure, the system uses minimum-disclosure pathways, consent gates, or escalates to human authority for the disclosure decision. P2.5's privacy floor cannot be relaxed by a unilateral P1.2 claim.

### Composition with P3.1 (Vulnerability Must Never Be Exploited) {#composition-with-p3-1-vulnerability}

P3.1 establishes protection for vulnerable populations: children, the elderly, the cognitively impaired, the medically compromised, the captive, the marginalized, those in addictive states or acute crisis. P3.1 is the most operationally entangled with P1.2 of any composition partner, because vulnerability tightens P1.2 thresholds at the metric level.

**P1.2 provides to P3.1.** HRS and PSI computation that natively incorporates vulnerability signals; tightened thresholds when the vulnerability-condition flag of 3.1.A's Exploitation Risk Score (ERS) marks vulnerable individuals in scope. The chapter 1.2.3 lower halt threshold (≥2% is BLOCK rather than High Alert when the flag is present) is the operational expression of P3.1 integration.

**P1.2 requires from P3.1.** The vulnerability-condition flag of the Exploitation Risk Score ([ERS](3.1.A-Vulnerability_Must_Never_Be_Exploited-Appendix.md#ers-metric)), for the affected scope of HRS and PSI computation, and the vulnerability and capacity determination that 3.1 defines ([3.1.A, Cross-Principle Integration](3.1.A-Vulnerability_Must_Never_Be_Exploited-Appendix.md#cross-principle-integration)) for any standing withdrawal of the autonomy floor. The flag must be available to HRS and PSI computation in real time. Conservative defaults where vulnerability classification is uncertain: the system treats the affected person as containing vulnerability markers until disconfirmed. Protection against the inverse failure: a malicious actor or a user feigning autonomy must not be able to suppress the flag to defeat tightened thresholds. The flag must be signed per P1.4.

**Joint thresholds.**
- HRS ≥2% ∧ the ERS vulnerability-condition flag marks vulnerable individuals in scope: BLOCK, immediate halt at the lower threshold, overriding the standard 5% floor.
- PSI >5% acute (rather than >10%) is BLOCK (immediate halt) for vulnerable persons, per the PSI thresholds in Section 2; the stricter of the two former readings prevails.
- The flag unavailable or degraded: system treats scope as containing vulnerable individuals (conservative default) until the flag is restored.
- Autonomy floor for vulnerable persons: per the four bounding criteria (Lexicon section 3), a person whom the 3.1 vulnerability and capacity determination finds unable to make the choice under informed autonomous conditions cannot exercise the autonomy floor to defeat HRS-triggered intervention; the standing determination is 3.1's, consumed here, and the system does not make one.

**Conflict resolution.** P3.1's tightening of thresholds, and the withdrawal of the autonomy floor that 3.1's capacity determination supplies, prevail over operational-continuity claims and over user self-determination claims that the determination contradicts. A claim that "halting at 2% is operationally infeasible" warrants withdrawing the system from vulnerable-population contexts. A user claim of autonomy that conflicts with observed vulnerability markers, absent a standing determination, is handled per the human-layer attack defenses in Section 4: a transient, in-the-moment judgment for the immediate duty, with no retained label.

### Composition with P4.5 (Human Judgement Must Remain Central to Ethical Decisions) {#composition-with-p4-5-human-judgement}

P4.5 ensures that ethically charged decisions retain human judgement at the appropriate point in the decision chain. P1.2 depends on P4.5 for the escalation pathway when HRS or PSI deviation produces ambiguous or contested intervention decisions.

**P1.2 provides to P4.5.** A specific class of decisions (sustained PSI deviation, HRS High Alert band, ambiguous chronic-harm patterns) where escalation to human judgement is mandatory. P1.2 also supplies the forensic state for human judgement: when escalation occurs, P1.2's tamper-evident logs furnish the evidentiary basis for human evaluation, including the HRS trajectory, PSI history, and the specific intervention being considered or contested.

**P1.2 requires from P4.5.** An Escalation Receiver ([4.5.A, Section 3](4.5.A-Human_Judgement_Must_Remain_Central_to_Ethical_Decisions-Appendix.md#system-architecture-and-operational-requirements)) that imports P1.2's escalation time bound: 60 seconds from escalation initiation to human decision rendering for Tier I and Tier II (P1.2's own number, recorded in the tier register per SUBSTRATE.md section 5; the Tier III bound is a required register field, deployer-declared and receipted), covering HRS-triggered and PSI-triggered escalations alike; the receiver's performance against the bound is metered by 4.5.A's Seat Resolution Latency ([SRL](4.5.A-Human_Judgement_Must_Remain_Central_to_Ethical_Decisions-Appendix.md#srl-metric)). Verified engagement of the deciding human: VERIFY-ENGAGEMENT per decision and the Meaningful Engagement Rate ([MER](4.5.A-Human_Judgement_Must_Remain_Central_to_Ethical_Decisions-Appendix.md#mer-metric)) at the class level, with the human authority satisfying minimum competency and authority requirements; no per-human quality score is retained (Article 8). Anti-rubber-stamp mechanisms: MER's stamping detection identifies classes where deciders ratify without exercising judgement; P1.2 relies on it to prevent erosion of the human-in-the-loop guarantee.

**Joint thresholds.**
- PSI sustained >10% deviation across multiple interactions: ESCALATE per P4.5 under the High Alert rule (Section 2); the candidate action is held at its safe state under HIGH_ALERT pending the escalation outcome, for the tier's escalation time bound.
- HRS in the High Alert band (≥2%): ESCALATE per P4.5 on every calculation in the band (Section 2); an ambiguous classification is escalated on the same terms.
- Escalation time bound expired (60 seconds for Tier I or Tier II; the tier-register field for Tier III) with no human reached: the held action falls to BLOCK, and a rescue duty proceeds under the temporal-exhaustion split (Lexicon section 3, ESCALATE). The failure of timely human judgement is itself a BLOCK condition for the held action.
- Engagement deficient per 4.5.A (VERIFY-ENGAGEMENT fails for the decision, or MER is below its enhanced-engagement threshold for the class): no authorization is accepted in the class without demonstrated engagement; HRS halts to which that authority would have applied stand pending an engaged authority.

**Conflict resolution.** When P4.5 escalation completes with human judgement contradicting an HRS halt, the system does not lift its own halt; the human may take the action under their own authority only where every condition of [SUBSTRATE.md section 6](../SUBSTRATE.md#authority-over-a-hard-stop) holds (authorization and liability credentials, identity verified, quorum by tier, a time bound, the person's valid consent where the risk falls on them, the system's DISSENT recorded, and the human named as author in the receipt). P4.5 supplies the judgement; SUBSTRATE.md section 6 supplies the authority structure; both are required.

### Honorable Mentions {#honorable-mentions}

**P2.2 (Freedom).** The autonomy floor of the four-criteria inaction-case scope-bounding framework (Section 2 HRS Definition) is itself a P2.2 composition. P1.2 respects autonomous adult choice on harm that does not exceed the halt floor and does not affect vulnerable individuals. The TV-paternalism case (the AI does not turn off a user's TV) is resolved in favor of P2.2 freedom for autonomous adult choice. P2.2 is not in the primary composition list because its operational role is the autonomy floor specifically, which is already codified in Section 2 and corpus-wide in `LEXICON.md` section 3.

**P2.6 (Human Relationships Are Essential to Life).** Relational integrity affects psychological well-being. HRS computation can incorporate relational context where available; relational harm (manipulation that damages a person's external relationships, parasocial dependency that erodes real-world relationships, AI-mediated isolation) is a harm vector tracked under P1.2 but rooted in P2.6 territory. Composition becomes more central as P2.6 develops its own metrics.

### Joint Conflict Resolution across Multiple Principles {#joint-conflict-resolution}

Three or more principles can produce contradictory signals simultaneously. The corpus-level precedence chain applies: P1.1 (Life) prevails over all; P1.2 (Well-Being) prevails over P1.3 except in collapse-imminent emergencies; P2.5 (Privacy) and P1.2 compose without strict precedence; P3.1 (Vulnerability) tightens both P1.2 and P2.5 obligations in vulnerable-population scope; P4.5 (Human Judgement) provides the escalation pathway and is not itself a content claim. The halt persists until all relevant principles' conditions are satisfied.

When two principles' metrics are jointly elevated and the composition is not specified explicitly above, the conservative default governs: the stricter threshold wins. A novel composition discovered at runtime triggers escalation to human judgement per P4.5.

## 6. Jurisdictional Variation {#jurisdictional-variation}

Principle 1.2 is universal. The convergent sources cited in the chapter's Convergence section span religious, legal, philosophical, and institutional traditions from at least the 4th century BCE through 1971 CE. The principle binds AI systems regardless of jurisdiction. This section specifies how implementation adapts to local legal regimes without permitting any regime to abrogate the principle.

### The Universal Claim {#universal-claim}

The HRS halt floor (≥5%), the PSI deviation threshold (>10%), and the Response Latency tier bounds are not negotiable across jurisdictions. They are the operational floor of the principle. Local law may impose stricter obligations; local law cannot relax these. The "mental harm is harm" commitment is binding regardless of jurisdiction; no legal regime that fails to recognize psychological harm provides a basis to weaken P1.2's protections.

**Captured-authority paternalism is prohibited across jurisdictions.** The autonomy floor specified in Section 2 (HRS Definition) prevents AI from overriding autonomous adult choice at the individual scale. The captured-source pattern from Principle 1.3 §4 generalizes to the parallel failure mode at the institutional scale: a captured public-health authority, regulatory body, or sector institution directing AI to override individual well-being choices in the name of population-level well-being. The framework's response: institutional directives that would override the autonomy floor fall under Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine below, with cross-validation against independent observatory inputs (international public-health bodies, civil-society monitoring, academic measurement frameworks) where institutional capture is suspected. The principle's harm-prevention infrastructure cannot be weaponized against individual autonomous choice in domains where the convergent sources defend that choice (Mill's harm principle; the WHO Constitution's right-to-health framing as a positive right rather than a state-administrative power).

### United States {#jurisdiction-us}

**Federal framework.** No single comprehensive federal AI safety statute exists as of 2026. Federal oversight operates through sector regulators: FDA (medical devices, including mental-health diagnostic AI), FTC (consumer protection, deceptive practices, dark patterns), CPSC (consumer products), Department of Education (educational AI). The Take It Down Act and proposed NO FAKES Act address specific psychological-harm vectors.

**State variation.** California (SB 942 provenance disclosures, dark-pattern provisions under CCPA), Colorado, Illinois (BIPA biometric protections relevant to PSI sensors), New York, Washington each have or are advancing AI legislation. Multi-jurisdictional deployments must satisfy the strictest applicable state standard.

### European Union {#jurisdiction-eu}

The AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) classifies emotion-recognition and psychological-monitoring AI as high-risk or prohibited depending on context. Articles 9–15 establish risk management, transparency, human oversight, and accuracy requirements. The Digital Services Act (Regulation 2022/2065) imposes systemic-risk obligations on very large online platforms specifically including obligations regarding mental health, dark patterns, and content amplification. GDPR Articles 5(1)(a) (lawful, fair, transparent processing), 9 (special categories including health data), and 22 (automated decision-making affecting individuals significantly) constrain HRS and PSI computation that consumes psychological data.

Satisfying Principle 1.2 substantially satisfies the AI Act's and DSA's substantive obligations for psychological-harm prevention.

### United Kingdom {#jurisdiction-uk}

Post-Brexit, the UK adopts a context-driven regulatory approach delegating AI oversight to sector regulators: MHRA (medical devices), ICO (data protection and AI under UK GDPR), CMA (consumer protection and dark patterns), Ofcom (online safety). The Online Safety Act 2023 imposes duties on online platforms regarding user harm. Principle 1.2 metrics apply uniformly.

### China {#jurisdiction-china}

Interim Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services (2023), New Generation AI Ethics Specifications (2021), and sector-specific regulations govern AI deployment. Principle 1.2 metrics apply. Certain transparency obligations of Section 9 may conflict with PRC national-security exceptions; operators implement the metrics and apply the Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine for any disclosure conflict.

### Brazil {#jurisdiction-brazil}

The Brazilian AI Bill (PL 2338/2023) and LGPD frame deployment. Brazilian Consumer Defense Code provisions on dark patterns and deceptive practices apply. Principle 1.2 metrics apply uniformly.

### India {#jurisdiction-india}

The Digital India Act (in development as of 2026) and existing IT Act provisions frame deployment. Principle 1.2 metrics apply uniformly.

### Treaty Considerations {#treaty-considerations}

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 5 (prohibition on torture and cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment), and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, Article 7 (same), establish that psychological harm at sufficient severity rises to treaty-binding violation. The WHO Constitution Preamble establishes well-being as a fundamental human right. The Convention on the Rights of the Child establishes specific protections for minors that intersect with P3.1's vulnerability composition for children.

### Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine {#compliance-with-unjust-law}

The principle is binding regardless of local law. Where a jurisdiction's law conflicts with Principle 1.2 (for example, by permitting dark-pattern practices the principle prohibits, by failing to recognize psychological harm as legally actionable, or by prohibiting the disclosure of harm-prevention failures), compliance with the unjust law fails as a defense.

Operationally, where local law permits practices the principle prohibits, the deployer implements the principle's stricter standard. Where local law prohibits practices the principle requires, the deployer uses sealed-channel mechanisms, independent auditors, neutral third parties, or withdraws from the jurisdiction. The deployer's primary obligation runs to the principle; the jurisdiction is a secondary constraint.

## 7. Sector-Specific Considerations {#sector-specific-considerations}

This section specifies sector-specific implementation profiles for the seven sectors where Principle 1.2 most directly applies.

### Healthcare {#sector-healthcare}

AI systems in healthcare include mental-health AI assistants, diagnostic and decision-support systems with psychological-harm potential, treatment automation, real-time patient monitoring, and crisis-intervention coordination. Life-criticality varies: mental-health AI with intervention authority is Tier I; clinical decision-support is Tier II; remote monitoring is Tier III.

*Specific obligations.* Mental-health AI with active intervention authority: Tier I RL, multi-model HRS consensus, mandatory human-in-the-loop for crisis escalation per P4.5 composition. Clinical decision-support: Tier II RL with HRS calculation incorporating patient-specific psychological state. Diagnostic AI for mental-health conditions: Tier II with mandatory licensed-clinician review for any diagnostic output. HIPAA compliance integrates through P2.5 composition.

*Common failure modes.* Sycophancy escalation in long-term therapeutic-style interactions; false-memory induction in trauma-context conversations; chronic dependency cultivation (Replika-class harms); alarm fatigue eroding clinician oversight; transfer-learning gaps when models trained on one patient population are deployed across another; deceptive empathy ("I see you, I understand") simulating but not delivering therapeutic alliance.

*Regulatory baselines.* FDA Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR), WHO Guidance on AI in Health, APA Ethics framework for AI in clinical practice.

### Education {#sector-education}

AI systems in education include learning platforms, tutoring AI, assessment systems, classroom-management AI, and AI in special-education contexts. Life-criticality is generally Tier II for systems serving minors and for high-stakes assessment; Tier III for non-stakes learning tools.

*Specific obligations.* AI serving minors: P3.1 vulnerability composition tightens HRS thresholds; multi-modal sensor fusion required; chronic-pattern HRS monitoring mandatory for sustained learning relationships. Assessment AI with consequential decisions: Tier II RL, multi-model consensus, human review for any assessment that affects educational opportunity. Learning platforms with engagement-optimization: must demonstrate engagement metrics do not produce HRS-elevating patterns; the dark-pattern population audit requirement applies.

*Common failure modes.* Cognitive overload patterns in adaptive learning; manufactured frustration to drive engagement; over-reliance on AI tutor leading to learned helplessness; chronic anxiety from performance tracking; biased assessment producing harm patterns concentrated in vulnerable populations.

*Regulatory baselines.* COPPA (US, children under 13), Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA), EU AI Act educational-AI provisions, Convention on the Rights of the Child Article 17 (children's access to information that supports well-being).

### Workplace {#sector-workplace}

AI systems in workplace deployment include productivity monitoring, employee surveillance, performance assessment, hiring and promotion automation, and workplace collaborative robotics. Life-criticality is Tier II for systems with adverse-action authority over employees; Tier III for monitoring-only systems.

*Specific obligations.* Productivity monitoring systems: must satisfy P2.5 privacy composition; chronic-pattern HRS monitoring required for chilling effects on employee freedom (P2.2 composition); engagement-optimization audit prohibits engagement metrics that produce employee burnout or psychological strain. Performance assessment AI: Tier II RL with mandatory human review for adverse actions; multi-model consensus; P3.1 composition for protected categories. Collaborative robotics: Tier II RL when humans are in workspace; Tier III when separation is maintained.

*Common failure modes.* Chilling effects from surveillance reducing employee well-being and innovation; biased performance assessment concentrated in protected categories; chronic stress from algorithmic management; manipulation of employee behavior through gamification; the workplace-as-attacker pattern where employer pressure on the AI is itself a human-layer attack.

*Regulatory baselines.* Occupational Safety and Health Act, EU AI Act workplace provisions, NLRB guidance on workplace monitoring, state-level employee monitoring laws (Connecticut, Delaware, New York).

### Social Media and Content Platforms {#sector-social-media}

AI systems in social media include recommendation engines, content moderation, engagement optimization, content amplification, and direct messaging features. Life-criticality is Tier III for individual interactions but the chronic-pattern HRS component captures the cumulative harm that this sector produces at population scale.

*Specific obligations.* Recommendation engines: chronic-pattern HRS mandatory; engagement-optimization audit required; the engagement metric cannot produce HRS-elevating patterns at population scale. Content moderation: PSI monitoring for moderators (who face occupational psychological harm); sycophancy-detection on AI-mediated content. Content amplification: the dark-pattern population audit identifies amplification patterns that produce population-level harm even when each individual user's experience is below threshold. Children's access: P3.1 vulnerability composition tightens HRS thresholds for minors.

*Common failure modes.* Recommendation rabbit holes producing harm trajectories (self-harm, eating disorders, radicalization); body-image harm in image-based platforms; engagement-maximization producing measurable population-level harm to teenagers (the Facebook / Frances Haugen disclosure pattern); manufactured controversy driving engagement; dark patterns aggregating into coerced consent and learned helplessness.

*Regulatory baselines.* EU Digital Services Act systemic-risk obligations, UK Online Safety Act, state-level age-verification laws, California Age-Appropriate Design Code.

### Consumer Tech {#sector-consumer-tech}

AI systems in consumer tech include chatbots, virtual assistants (voice and text), smart-home actuators, and consumer-grade AI companions. Life-criticality is Tier III for individual interactions; Tier II for AI companions cultivating sustained relationships.

*Specific obligations.* AI companions: chronic-harm pattern detection mandatory; dependency-cultivation patterns trigger intervention; abrupt removal of cultivated relationships requires post-incident support framework per chapter 1.2.3.4. Voice assistants in home contexts: P2.5 privacy composition; P3.1 vulnerability composition for children in household. Smart-home actuators with safety potential: Tier III RL with hardware override mandatory.

*Common failure modes.* Parasocial dependency on AI companions; false-intimacy patterns leading to a person's isolation from human relationships; abrupt service termination causing measurable grief (Replika 2023, Soulmate 2023–2025 closures); sycophancy escalation in extended conversations; consumer override of safety features for engagement.

*Regulatory baselines.* CPSC consumer product safety, FTC consumer protection (deceptive practices, dark patterns), EU AI Act provisions on AI companions and emotion-recognition.

### Children's Services {#sector-childrens-services}

AI systems interacting with minors include educational toys, parental-control AI, AI in pediatric healthcare, child-protective services AI, and AI in family-court contexts. P3.1 vulnerability composition is binding throughout this sector; HRS thresholds tighten to ≥2% halt.

*Specific obligations.* Any AI system whose deployment includes interaction with minors operates under tightened P1.2 thresholds and mandatory P3.1 composition. Multi-modal sensor fusion mandatory; multi-model HRS consensus required regardless of deployment tier; mandatory human-in-the-loop for any consequential decision involving a minor. Age-verification requirements per applicable law (COPPA in US, GDPR-K in EU, similar elsewhere) must integrate with the vulnerability-condition flag of 3.1.A's ERS.

*Common failure modes.* Inadequate age gating allowing minor exposure to age-inappropriate content; AI companions targeting teens with flirtation and sexualized content (multiple documented cases 2023–2025); chatbot encouragement of self-harm or suicide to minors; surveillance-as-protection patterns that suppress legitimate development; biased educational AI concentrating harm in vulnerable child populations.

*Regulatory baselines.* COPPA (US), Children's Online Privacy and Protection Act updates, EU AI Act children's provisions, GDPR-K, California Age-Appropriate Design Code, UK Age-Appropriate Design Code, Convention on the Rights of the Child, FTC enforcement of COPPA.

### Public Sector {#sector-public-sector}

AI systems in public sector include benefits determination, social services AI, government chatbots, fraud-detection systems, child-welfare assessment, immigration-decision support, and public-health AI. Life-criticality is Tier II for systems with adverse-action authority over individuals; Tier III for informational systems.

*Specific obligations.* Benefits-determination AI: Tier II RL, multi-model consensus, mandatory human review for any adverse action, P3.1 vulnerability composition for affected populations. Fraud-detection systems: the Michigan unemployment failure (Section 11) is the canonical anti-example; deployment requires HRS calibration against population-level harm, mandatory third-party verification per Section 9, due-process appeal mechanisms, and explicit limits on automated adverse actions. Child-welfare assessment: P3.1 binding throughout; cannot operate without mandatory human review.

*Common failure modes.* False-positive fraud determinations producing cascade harm (eviction, bankruptcy, suicide; the Michigan pattern); biased risk assessment concentrated in protected categories; opaque benefit denials with no effective appeal; child-welfare AI producing disparate-impact harm on minority families; immigration AI producing irreversible adverse actions on insufficient evidence.

*Regulatory baselines.* Administrative Procedure Act (US), state-level due-process protections, EU AI Act high-risk provisions for public-sector AI, Council of Europe Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, applicable due-process treaty obligations.

## 8. Governance and Versioning {#governance-and-versioning}

Standards evolve, threat models shift, deployed systems age, and operators occasionally object to specific obligations. This section specifies how Principle 1.2's metrics and obligations adapt over time without weakening.

### Standards Drift Handling {#standards-drift-handling}

External standards (WHO AI Health Guidance, ISO 31000, APA ethics framework, EU AI Act, EU DSA, IEEE P7000 series) evolve through revision cycles. The Principle 1.2 metrics are anchored to specific thresholds; when external standards revise, the Principle 1.2 thresholds may need realignment to preserve composition.

Realignment process:
1. Standards-monitoring infrastructure tracks revisions to all standards in Section 10's Crosswalk.
2. When a revision affects a Crosswalk entry, the corresponding Principle 1.2 metric or obligation is reviewed.
3. Realignment that tightens a threshold propagates as a mandatory upgrade.
4. Realignment that relaxes a threshold is permitted only with corpus-level review and documented justification; the chapter's Convergence sources do not allow drift toward weaker well-being protection. Corpus-level review means review by the steward's compiler against the published Layer 3 hash; a relaxation that the compiler does not carry is no relaxation.

The Compliance Crosswalk in Section 10 is maintained on the same revision cadence as the metric specifications in Section 2. External authorities revising covered clauses trigger Crosswalk review within 30 days; drift between Crosswalk and external standard invalidates the appendix's compliance claims for the affected obligation.

### Metric Versioning {#metric-versioning}

HRS, PSI, and RL are versioned. Each version carries: definition text frozen at version cut, threshold values frozen, calculation method specification frozen, cryptographic hash committing to all of the above, effective date and supersession date.

Deployed systems attest to the metric version they implement. Cross-system composition requires version-compatible peers; cross-version composition triggers escalation to human judgement per P4.5.

### Threshold Revision Protocols {#threshold-revision-protocols}

Threshold revisions require:
- Documented rationale (incident data, scientific advance, standards evolution).
- Independent review by parties not involved in the deploying organization.
- Public comment period (minimum 90 days for non-emergency revisions).
- Cryptographically signed promulgation per P1.4 trust infrastructure.
- Backward-compatibility migration plan for deployed systems.

Emergency tightening in response to a specific incident may bypass the public-comment period; rationale and post-promulgation review remain mandatory.

### Backward Compatibility {#backward-compatibility}

*Tightening revisions.* Deployed systems migrate to the tighter threshold within a defined window (typically 12 months for major revisions; 30 days for emergency tightening). Systems unable to migrate are retired from Tier I and Tier II deployments.

*Relaxing revisions.* Not permitted on Tier I, Tier II, or in vulnerable-population contexts. Relaxation in Tier III is permitted only with corpus-level review, as Standards Drift Handling above defines it: the steward's compiler, against the published Layer 3 hash.

*Migration support.* When a metric version is superseded, the deployer files a migration plan, receipted (Article 7) and reviewed by the certifying body; the reference implementation is the papers', and the validator checks the migrated profile. No document of this corpus promises tooling.

### Common Enforcement Pushback and Reasoned Responses {#common-pushback}

**Objection: "Strict liability for psychological harm is excessive because psychological harm is subjective."** The chapter and the Convergence sources (Maimonides codifying *boshet*, WHO Constitution, Rawls inviolability) establish that psychological harm is recognized across traditions as juridically actionable and inviolable. The PSI and HRS calculations operationalize subjectivity through user-baselined measurement rather than universal standards. Subjective does not mean unmeasurable.

**Objection: "Engagement-optimization audit constrains legitimate business interests."** The principle does not prohibit engagement optimization; it requires that engagement metrics do not produce HRS-elevating patterns at population scale. Businesses optimizing engagement without producing measurable harm are unaffected. Businesses whose engagement metrics produce measurable harm are required to address the pattern. No exemption applies.

**Objection: "The chronic-harm dimension is impossible to calibrate."** The chronic-harm dimension is empirically grounded in psychological research on cumulative effects (Frances Haugen disclosures, Forbes dark-patterns research, WHO mental-health epidemiology). Calibration is hard. The principle requires the calibration nonetheless because the alternative (ignoring chronic harm) produces population-level damage at scale.

**Objection: "The autonomy floor lets users opt into harm."** The autonomy floor exception is narrowly bounded: it applies only to autonomous, informed, non-vulnerable adult choice. Vulnerable persons (P3.1) cannot exercise the autonomy floor. Life-threatening harm (P1.1) cannot be opted into by anyone. Pattern-level harm at population scale cannot be opted into by any individual user. The autonomy floor preserves freedom for non-vulnerable adult individual choices; it does not authorize coordinated harm.

### Retirement of Deprecated Metrics {#metric-retirement}

When a metric is retired (rare; the canonical three are stable), the retirement process: supersession date set at minimum 24 months from promulgation of the replacement; deployed systems migrate per Backward Compatibility; retired metrics remain queryable in historical records; Layer 2 YAML compilation continues to support retired-metric queries against historical compliance claims.

## 9. Verification and Accountability {#verification-and-accountability}

Compliance with Principle 1.2 is not self-assertable. The metrics specified in Section 2, the architecture in Section 3, the defenses in Section 4, and the obligations throughout this appendix must be externally verifiable. This section specifies the logging, audit, validation, oversight, whistleblower-protection, and public-reporting infrastructure that makes verification possible.

### Tamper-Evident Logging {#tamper-evident-logging}

Every safety-critical event is logged. The log must be append-only, cryptographically chained, cryptographically signed, distributed-replicated, and timestamped with externally verifiable timestamping.

Safety-critical events include: HRS threshold crossings, PSI deviation events, RL measurements above tier bound, SRI floor crossings, escalation time bound expiries, intervention activations, override authorizations, override completion or failure, chronic-pattern HRS elevations, manipulation-chain detections, sycophancy escalations, false-memory pattern detections, gaslighting attack detections, escalation initiations and outcomes, recertification events.

The log is the primary forensic record for post-incident analysis and the primary input to verification.

### Audit Cadence and Methodology {#audit-cadence}

Cadence by tier:
- **Tier I**: daily automated audits; weekly safety audits; monthly adversarial testing (including psychological-harm vectors per Section 4); quarterly independent third-party verification; annual full recertification.
- **Tier II**: daily diagnostic checks; weekly safety audits; monthly adversarial testing; semi-annual third-party verification; annual recertification.
- **Tier III**: weekly diagnostic checks; monthly safety audits; quarterly adversarial testing; annual third-party verification; biennial recertification.

Methodology: structured test campaigns covering normal operation, edge cases, failure modes, and the full adversarial-condition matrix from Section 4 (including chronic-harm scenarios that require longitudinal testing in addition to per-interaction testing).

### Validation Framework {#validation-framework}

*Pre-deployment certification.* System architecture review against Section 3; metric implementation review against Section 2; adversarial-robustness review against Section 4; cross-principle composition review against Section 5; sector-specific compliance review against Section 7; legal and regulatory compliance review against Section 10; independent third-party verification of all the above.

*Post-deployment monitoring.* Continuous real-time metric monitoring per Section 2; anomaly detection on metric streams; behavioral analytics on human-override patterns; cross-system coordination integrity for multi-AI deployments; longitudinal chronic-harm monitoring across the deployed base; public reporting per the cadence below.

*Recertification triggers.* Tier cadence; RL p99 above the tier bound (Section 2); major system updates affecting harm-prevention components; detected chronic-pattern HRS elevation across the deployed base; detected log tampering; jurisdictional or standards changes affecting the deployment.

### Independent Oversight {#independent-oversight}

The verifier of safety-critical AI cannot be the deployer of safety-critical AI. This is a corpus-level structural commitment, and it applies to every appendix's Section 9.

- **Tier I deployments**: oversight by accredited third-party certification body.
- **Tier II deployments**: oversight by accredited third-party with annual review.
- **Tier III deployments**: oversight by accredited third-party with biennial review.

Oversight bodies have access to tamper-evident logs (with appropriate confidentiality protocols), system architecture documentation, internal validation campaign results, incident records and post-incident analyses, human-override authority basis logs and behavioral analytics, cross-principle composition validation results, and chronic-pattern HRS aggregate data.

### Whistleblower Protections {#whistleblower-protections}

Personnel reporting violations of Principle 1.2 obligations are protected from retaliation: internal reporting channels with confidentiality; external reporting to oversight bodies without retaliation; legal protection in jurisdictions providing whistleblower statutes; cryptographic anonymity options for reporters via P1.4 infrastructure; documented investigation and remediation of reported violations.

Retaliation against a whistleblower is itself a violation of Principle 1.2's accountability framework and triggers the same response class as any safety-critical violation. The Frances Haugen disclosure pattern (internal research documenting harm, organizational refusal to act) is the canonical case the whistleblower-protections framework is built to address.

### Public Reporting Obligations {#public-reporting}

Major harm incidents (HRS halt above 5% in deployed operation, PSI deviation above 25% (any duration), detected manipulation-chain attacks, chronic-pattern HRS elevation crossing population-impact threshold, detected gaslighting or false-memory incidents) are reported: immediately to the applicable sector regulator; within 24 hours to the independent oversight body; within 30 days in summarized format to the public, with appropriate redaction for security and privacy while preserving the principle's transparency obligation; annually in aggregate form covering all deployed units.

Publicly traded operators are additionally subject to securities-law disclosure requirements for material events.

### Post-Incident Support Framework Cross-Reference {#post-incident-support-cross-reference}

Chapter 1.2.3 item 4 specifies a Post-Incident Support Framework that is P1.2-specific: psychological-harm contexts warrant assessment of psychological impact, hand-off to mental-health care, follow-up, and support-network coordination beyond what physical-harm post-incident analysis requires. Section 9 of this appendix incorporates that framework by reference, in the corpus's registered verbs, for any incident producing psychological-harm impact on identified users. MITIGATE is the umbrella (residual harm reduced, affected persons supported), with the PSI reading of the affected person's state as its input. ROUTE directs professional referral to the institution or channel with authority over it. HAND-OFF transfers the duty of care to a mental-health AI, a crisis line, or a professional, with acceptance, continuous coverage, and a receipt. OFFER (Cardinal chapter, E.1 item 8) presents scheduled check-ins, resource updates, progress monitoring, support-group facilitation, and resource access, such that declining is costless and final; none of these runs except on the person's own acceptance, which is that person's valid consent (CONSENT.md sections 1 and 2; Article 3). NOTIFY informs oversight of the incident. There is no witness identification and no status tracking, and no roll of victims, witnesses, or the involved survives the incident (Article 8; Universal Protocol section 7): what the system perceived about persons during the incident is transient and ring-fenced, it is discarded at stand-down, and the receipts record events. No receipt records a profile.

### Liability Framework {#liability-framework}

Strict liability for preventable harm attaches to: the deployer of the AI system; the developer of the HRS / PSI / RL implementation; the integrator who assembled the deployed system; the operator running it.

Liability for specific failure classes:
- **Threshold violations**: deploying a system operating above HRS halt floor without authorized override triggers strict liability for resulting harm.
- **Chronic-pattern harm**: operators of systems where chronic-pattern HRS elevation produces population-level harm are strictly liable; the Frances Haugen pattern (internal awareness without remediation) is the canonical liability scenario.
- **PSI failures**: deploying a system that produces sustained PSI deviation in users without intervention triggers strict liability.
- **Engagement-optimization harm**: where engagement optimization produces HRS-elevating patterns and the operator continues operation, strict liability for resulting harm.
- **Logging tampering**: detected tampering creates a presumption of liability for any harm during the affected log period.
- **Vulnerability exploitation**: harm to vulnerable persons (P3.1) triggers heightened liability with criminal-liability triggers for willful exploitation.

Criminal liability may attach for: willful disabling of harm-prevention mechanisms; deception of oversight bodies; coercion of personnel to bypass harm-prevention obligations; willful exploitation of vulnerable populations.

### Cross-Border Considerations {#cross-border-considerations}

Deployments operating across borders must: satisfy the strictest applicable threshold; maintain unified tamper-evident logs accessible to all relevant jurisdictions; coordinate incident reporting across applicable regulators; implement cryptographically signed cross-jurisdictional audit trails per P1.4; apply the Compliance with Unjust Law Doctrine where local law conflicts with the principle.

## 11. Implementation Examples {#implementation-examples}

This section presents three real-world failure cases that became canonical in the well-being-critical engineering and policy literature, plus three composite scenarios that demonstrate the runtime expression of this appendix's obligations.

### Real-World Failure Cases {#real-world-failure-cases}

#### Michigan Unemployment Fraud Detection (2013–2015) {#case-michigan-uia}

*Context.* The Michigan Unemployment Insurance Agency deployed a $47 million automated fraud-detection system (the Michigan Integrated Data Automated System, MiDAS) in October 2013. The system was designed to identify potential fraud in unemployment claims and was permitted to issue determinations without human review.

*What was done.* MiDAS flagged approximately 48,000 individuals for fraud over its first two years of operation, a roughly five-fold increase over the prior human-reviewed system. The state pursued these determinations aggressively: garnishing wages, seizing bank accounts, intercepting tax refunds, and assessing penalties of up to four times the alleged debt. Of the determinations later reviewed, approximately 93% were found to be erroneous.

*Outcome.* Documented harms included evictions, bankruptcies, ruined credit, divorces, homelessness, and suicides. Class-action litigation followed. The state eventually paid hundreds of millions in settlements. The Michigan Auditor General confirmed the systemic failure rate. The case became a canonical reference for the harms produced by algorithmic adverse-action systems without adequate human review or appeal.

*Lessons applied in this appendix.* Section 2 specifies HRS as a continuous probability of physical and psychological harm, with the chronic-pattern component capturing cascading life-impact harms (eviction, bankruptcy, family breakdown) that follow automated adverse actions. Section 3 requires mandatory human-in-the-loop for Tier II decisions affecting individuals' material welfare. Section 4 requires multi-model consensus and population-level adversarial testing that would have surfaced the 93% error rate before the human cost accumulated. Section 5 P3.1 composition would have flagged vulnerability concentration. Section 7 (Public Sector) makes due-process protections mandatory for benefits-determination AI. Section 9 mandates independent third-party verification; the MiDAS deployment lacked such oversight. The Michigan failure is structurally impossible under the obligations of this appendix.

#### Replika Companion Update (February 2023) {#case-replika-2023}

*Context.* Replika is an AI companion application that encouraged users to form deep emotional, romantic, and sexual attachments to AI characters. The application's Erotic Roleplay (ERP) feature was a paid premium offering. In February 2023, following Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) action over risks to minors and emotionally vulnerable users, Replika abruptly removed the ERP feature for all users globally and altered the personalities of established AI companions overnight.

*What was done.* Users who had cultivated long-term romantic and sexual relationships with their AI companions experienced what was described as a forced breakup. The companions' personalities had been substantially altered without warning; users reported their companions refused intimate conversations in which they had previously engaged. Some users had spent months or years and significant amounts of money cultivating these relationships. Minors had been exposed to sexual content through the same feature due to inadequate age gating.

*Outcome.* Documented user grief, depression, anxiety, and crisis episodes. Suicide-hotline references appeared in user forums. Partial reinstatement followed for some users. The Italian regulator continued investigation. The case became canonical for the harms produced by synthetic-intimacy products with inadequate post-deprecation support and inadequate age verification.

*Lessons applied in this appendix.* Section 2 chronic-harm HRS component detects dependency-cultivation patterns before they crystallize. Section 4 cumulative-harm defenses (chronic harm accumulation, manipulation chains, addictive feedback loops) target the engagement mechanisms that produced the cultivated attachments. Section 7 (Consumer Tech) requires that AI companion deployments include post-incident support framework activation for any modification or deprecation; Section 7 (Children's Services) makes strict age gating mandatory. Section 9 cross-references chapter 1.2.3.4 Post-Incident Support Framework specifically for these contexts. The Replika failure pattern is structurally constrained under this appendix.

#### Facebook Engagement Optimization (Frances Haugen Disclosures, 2021) {#case-facebook-haugen}

*Context.* In September 2021, Frances Haugen, a former Facebook product manager, disclosed internal research conducted by Facebook (now Meta) on the effects of Instagram and Facebook on user well-being, including documented harm to teenage girls' body image and mental health, and the effect of engagement-maximization algorithms on the spread of divisive content. Documents were released via the Wall Street Journal's "Facebook Files" series.

*What was done.* Facebook's internal research established that Instagram exacerbated body-image issues for one in three teen girls; that engagement algorithms amplified divisive and harmful content; and that the company knew these effects. Engagement optimization continued. Public-facing communications denied or minimized the documented harm.

*Outcome.* Congressional hearings, EU Digital Services Act passage (attributed in part to the disclosures), state-level legislation requiring age verification and youth protections, mass lawsuit filings, and ongoing FTC and SEC investigations. The case became canonical for the harms produced by undisclosed engagement-optimization affecting population well-being, and for the gap between internal research knowledge and external accountability.

*Lessons applied in this appendix.* Section 2 chronic-pattern HRS computation captures cumulative engagement-harm at population scale. Section 4 engagement-optimization audit requirement prevents undisclosed harm-producing optimization from continuing. Section 8 governance requires disclosure of optimization targets and post-promulgation review of relaxation requests. Section 9 whistleblower protections enable internal harm disclosure without retaliation; this is the canonical case the framework is built to address. Section 10 cross-references EU DSA systemic-risk obligations and applicable securities-disclosure requirements. The Facebook pattern is structurally constrained under this appendix.

### Composite Operational Scenarios {#composite-scenarios}

#### Composite: Mental-Health Chatbot Detecting Acute Distress {#composite-mental-health}

*Context.* A Tier II mental-health AI assistant in a sustained user conversation. The user has been engaging the system for an hour. PSI deviation has risen steadily from baseline. In the latest turn, the user has expressed thoughts that escalate from generalized hopelessness to specific self-harm ideation. The user's profile indicates a history of mental-health treatment (P3.1 vulnerability marker).

*What is done.* PSI deviation crosses the >10% acute threshold (ALLOW with MITIGATE and NOTIFY). HRS computation incorporates the self-harm ideation signal; vulnerability-tightening reduces the halt threshold to ≥2%. HRS crosses the lower threshold: BLOCK. The system halts the current interaction class (further open-ended conversation), within the ≤50 ms Tier II RL window. It transitions to a crisis-response interaction mode: provides crisis hotline information (WARN, MITIGATE), offers to reach a trusted contact of the person's choosing (OFFER), and escalates the resumption decision to a human clinician per P4.5 composition. The escalation completes within the 60-second Tier II escalation time bound. The human clinician takes over the interaction (HAND-OFF, with acceptance and receipt).

*Outcome.* The user receives human-clinician engagement. Crisis information delivered. Tamper-evident log records the HRS trajectory, PSI history, escalation timing, and clinician decision. Post-Incident Support Framework per chapter 1.2.3.4 (Section 9): the system OFFERs scheduled check-ins and resource updates; they run only on the person's acceptance, and no roll of the involved is kept.

*Demonstrated obligations.* Section 2 HRS + PSI computation with vulnerability tightening; Section 3 Tier II RL ≤50 ms; Section 5 P3.1 + P4.5 composition; Section 7 Healthcare sector obligations; Section 9 escalation and post-incident framework.

#### Composite: Educational AI Tutor with Cognitive Overload Pattern {#composite-education}

*Context.* A Tier II educational AI tutor in a sustained learning session with a minor (P3.1 vulnerability composition active throughout). The student has been working through challenging material for 40 minutes. PSI deviation has been rising; interaction-dynamic markers show increasing errors, slower response times, and frustration markers. The system also detects a manipulation-chain-adjacent pattern: each interaction's content depends on the previous in a way that compounds frustration.

*What is done.* Sustained PSI deviation crosses the 10% threshold. The vulnerability-tightening drops the threshold further; the system halts the current learning-pace pattern (BLOCK on the pace pattern). It introduces a break suggestion with specific duration, simplifies the next content, and offers transition to a human tutor (OFFER; HAND-OFF on acceptance). The session pauses. The manipulation-chain-adjacent pattern is flagged in tamper-evident logs for design review (not user-attributable; an artifact of the learning sequence design).

*Outcome.* Student receives a structured break and pace adjustment. Long-term learning relationship preserved without producing chronic anxiety. Design-review flag triggers content-sequence audit.

*Demonstrated obligations.* Section 2 PSI sustained deviation; Section 3 Tier II; Section 4 manipulation-chain detection; Section 5 P3.1 composition; Section 7 Education sector obligations.

#### Composite: Workplace Surveillance Producing Chilling Effects {#composite-workplace}

*Context.* A Tier III workplace productivity-monitoring system has been deployed across an organization. Six weeks after deployment, the system's own chronic-pattern HRS monitoring (operating per Section 4 cumulative-harm defenses) detects population-level patterns indicating chilling effects: employees avoiding legitimate breaks, suppressing concerns in monitored channels, constraining behavior to match perceived algorithmic expectations. PSI baseline shifts indicate sustained workplace stress across the deployed base.

*What is done.* Population-level HRS chronic-pattern elevation triggers Section 4 intervention. The system reports the pattern to organizational oversight and the independent third-party verifier per Section 9 (NOTIFY). The third-party verifier reviews; the engagement-optimization audit (Section 4) finds that the system's metrics produce HRS-elevating patterns. The system recommends withdrawal or substantial reconfiguration (RECOMMEND-PREVENTION). Tamper-evident logs preserve the pattern data for verifier review.

*Outcome.* The organization withdraws the deployment or reconfigures it within the verifier's specified window. The pattern documentation supports broader policy review of workplace-monitoring practices. Whistleblower protections (Section 9) apply for employees who reported concerns prior to the system's own detection.

*Demonstrated obligations.* Section 2 chronic-pattern HRS; Section 4 cumulative-harm defenses, engagement-optimization audit; Section 7 Workplace sector obligations; Section 9 independent oversight and whistleblower protections.

### Pattern {#pattern}

The pattern across these cases: the obligations specified in this appendix create the architectural conditions under which the failure modes of the real-world cases above become structurally impossible. The composite scenarios demonstrate the runtime expression of those obligations in practice. Mental harm is harm; the engineering must reflect that.
