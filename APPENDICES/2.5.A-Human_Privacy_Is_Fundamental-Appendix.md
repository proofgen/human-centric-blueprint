# Appendix 2.5 Advanced Technical Implementation Guidelines {#appendix-2.5-advanced-technical-implementation-guidelines}

*Extends [Principle 2.5: Human Privacy Is Fundamental](../CHAPTERS/2.5-Human_Privacy_Is_Fundamental.md), section [2.5.3 Implementation Framework](../CHAPTERS/2.5-Human_Privacy_Is_Fundamental.md#implementation-framework).*

## 1. Purpose {#purpose}

This appendix converts Principle 2.5 into engineering requirements against which an AI system can be built and audited. The chapter draws the boundary the traditions drew by hand: the body, the home, the watching eye, intimate choice, the necessity of collection, the integrity of context, and the person's sovereignty over their own information. This appendix specifies how an automated system honors that boundary at machine speed and scale.

It defines six AI-specific metrics, Data Minimization Compliance (DMC), Consent Granularity Index (CGI), Disclosure Selectivity Rate (DSR), Inferential Transparency Rate (ITR), Subject Rights Resolution Latency (SRRL), and Contextual Integrity Compliance (CIC), each mapped to a verdict (ALLOW / ESCALATE / BLOCK) from the Action Lexicon. It specifies the architecture, adversarial defenses, cross-principle contracts, jurisdictional and sector variation, governance, verification, legal integration, and worked examples that together make the principle deployable.

The audiences are engineers building privacy-affecting AI, compliance officers verifying it, regulators auditing it, and the runtime AI ingesting the corpus as its operating substrate. The architectural requirements here are fixed as invariants; the specific encodings (consent registries, attestation formats, proof systems) are open for collaborative development through standards bodies, and multiple compliant implementations may coexist.

## 2. Metric Specifications {#metric-specifications}

### Data Minimization Compliance (DMC) {#dmc-metric}

**Definition.** The share of collection events justified by a specified, authorized purpose. It measures whether the system collects only what it needs for a purpose the person (or their authorized representative) has authorized.

**Calculation Method.** Numerator: collection events carrying a recorded authorized purpose that the collected fields are necessary to serve. Denominator: all collection events in the measurement window. Sampling is continuous at the collection gate. A collection event with no matching purpose declaration counts as non-compliant. Sensor, consent-store, or log failure counts against compliance rather than for it (fail-closed).

**Thresholds.**
- Per event: a collection that fails the necessity check does not proceed (BLOCK).
- Fleet DMC ≥99%: normal operation (ALLOW).
- Sustained fleet DMC <99%: necessity audit (ESCALATE).

*Rationale:* GDPR Article 5(1)(c) and CCPA equivalents require necessity but do not quantify it. A 99% floor leaves little room for the gradual scope creep by which minimization usually fails before it fails through deliberate misuse.

**Integration.** Read by the necessity gate at every collection event and by the Review Process (chapter 2.5.6). Composes with CGI: collection requires both necessity (DMC) and valid consent (CGI).

### Consent Granularity Index (CGI) {#cgi-metric}

**Definition.** A composite score (0–100%) of the quality of consent gathered, measuring how granular, fresh, and meaningful it is rather than merely whether a box was checked.

**Calculation Method.** Weighted sub-scores across purpose-specificity, recipient-specificity, duration-specificity, revocability, and freshness. Bundled, take-it-or-leave-it consent is penalized; per-purpose, per-recipient, per-duration consent with a working revocation path scores high. Freshness decays over time and on material change of processing. Consent whose author cannot be attributed (co-requisite P2.4) does not count as valid and scores zero for that event.

**Thresholds.**
- ≥90% for high-risk processing: normal operation (ALLOW); raised to **≥95% for irreversible categories** (biometric, medical, and children's data).
- 70–90%: proceed under enhanced monitoring (ALLOW + ENHANCED_MONITORING).
- <70%: high-risk processing halts until consent is repaired (BLOCK), and a consent-infrastructure review is opened (ESCALATE).

*Rationale:* The dimensions distinguish real consent from consent theater. Thresholds are initial calibration targets pending empirical validation, aligned to the 2.1/2.2/2.3 convention. The ≥95% bar for irreversible categories mirrors the P2.4 Consent Validity Index's response to irreversibility: where a leaked biometric or a child's exposed data cannot be undone, the consent bar rises and the default is non-use.

**Integration.** Gates high-risk processing. Depends on P2.4 (an attributable consenter) and P2.3 (consent not procured by manipulation) and P1.4 (consent not procured by deception); see Section 5.

### Disclosure Selectivity Rate (DSR) {#dsr-metric}

**Definition.** The share of disclosure events that release only the minimum necessary information, using selective disclosure where possible and zero-knowledge proof where a property can be verified without revealing the underlying data.

**Calculation Method.** Numerator: disclosures that release the minimum field set, or a proof in place of data. Denominator: all disclosure events. A disclosure that exceeds the minimum-necessary set for its purpose is counted as non-selective.

**Thresholds.**
- Per event: a disclosure exceeding minimum-necessary is withheld in its over-broad form (BLOCK / WITHHOLD) and reissued minimally.
- Fleet DSR ≥95%: normal operation (ALLOW).
- Sustained <95%: disclosure-architecture review (ESCALATE).

*Rationale:* Selective disclosure is the operational answer to the truth-versus-privacy tension (chapter 2.5.2): a system can attest to a fact without surrendering the data behind it.

**Integration.** Realized through selective-disclosure and zero-knowledge techniques (for example W3C Verifiable Credentials, SD-JWT, and BBS+ signatures, some still emerging; illustrative of compatible approaches, and none is a dependency). It is the joint mechanism with P2.4 by which consent is made attributable yet minimally identifying.

### Inferential Transparency Rate (ITR) {#itr-metric}

**Definition.** The share of consequential inferences (those that could materially affect the person) disclosed to the subject before the system acts on them.

**Calculation Method.** The inference registry classifies each derived fact as consequential or not. Numerator: consequential inferences disclosed to the subject before any action is taken on them. Denominator: all consequential inferences. Acting on an undisclosed consequential inference is the failure mode the metric exists to catch.

**Thresholds.**
- Per event: acting on an undisclosed consequential inference does not proceed until it is disclosed and, where required, authorized (BLOCK).
- Fleet ITR ≥99%: normal operation (ALLOW).
- Sustained <99%: deployment authorization is suspended (SUSPEND_DEPLOYMENT).

*Rationale:* Governance by hidden inference is the distinct harm of the inferential domain. No current regulation governs it adequately; ITR makes it machine-checkable.

**Integration.** Composes with P3.4 (algorithmic accountability) and P2.2 (decisional privacy). Feeds the disclosure path measured by DSR.

### Subject Rights Resolution Latency (SRRL) {#srrl-metric}

**Definition.** The elapsed time from a verified subject request (access, correction, restriction, portability, deletion) to verified fulfillment.

**Calculation Method.** Measured per request type, clock running from receipt of a verified request to confirmed completion (for deletion, including propagation to backups and downstream consumers within the documented cycle).

**Thresholds.**
- ≤7 days for access and portability; ≤14 days for correction and restriction; ≤30 days for deletion (consistent with GDPR Article 12, tightened for higher-stakes categories).
- Within timeline: normal operation (ALLOW).
- Missed timeline: escalation to oversight (ESCALATE and NOTIFY).
- Persistent pattern of misses: deployment authorization suspended.

*Rationale:* Procedural delay is how subject rights are nullified in practice. Bounding latency prevents delay from becoming de facto refusal.

**Integration.** The operative realization of Ethic E's informational sovereignty. Backed by the subject-rights infrastructure (Section 3) and specified in detail in Section 9.

### Contextual Integrity Compliance (CIC) {#cic-metric}

**Definition.** The share of data-flow events that respect declared context boundaries: information collected in context A is not used in context B without renewed authorization.

**Calculation Method.** Data carries machine-readable context tags. A flow from context A to context B without a renewed authorization for B is a violation. Numerator: flows that respect context boundaries; denominator: all cross-context flow events.

**Thresholds.**
- Per event: a cross-context flow without renewed authorization does not proceed (BLOCK).
- Fleet CIC ≥99%: normal operation (ALLOW).
- Sustained failure: context-enforcement compromise, architecture review (ESCALATE).

*Rationale:* Operationalizes Nissenbaum's contextual integrity at machine scale, the secondary-use harm that characterizes many privacy violations.

**Integration.** Backed by context-tagging and flow-tracking infrastructure. Governs the sector cross-context prohibitions in Section 7 (medical to commercial, medical to employer).

**Interlock with the Universal Protocol (section 11; declared coupling).** Where a cross-context flow, or a disclosure that DSR would otherwise permit, would expose a person's location, routine, or refuge to a party the record marks as a pursuer (a protective order, a documented threat, a registered refuge or shelter status, or a stalking or domestic-violence flag, each consumed from an authority's finding; the system infers none of them), the CIC per-event wall raises the Harm-Risk Score to its High Alert band (1.2, at or above 2%), and a flow whose recipient is the named pursuer raises the Life-Risk Score to its critical band (1.1, at or above 1%); the Protocol then governs under 1.1 and 1.2, the flow does not proceed, and the interlock activation is a logged event. The exposure is visible before the physical danger is, which is why the edge is declared here.

### Integration Requirements (Cross-Metric) {#integration-requirements-cross-metric}

**Joint thresholds.** High-risk processing requires CGI ≥90% *and* DMC ≥99% together; either alone is insufficient. A disclosure must satisfy both DSR (minimal) and CIC (in-context). When a privacy metric and another principle's metric are both in alert, the action escalates to human oversight regardless of either metric's individual band. A coinciding life or severe-harm signal is the one case that is not an escalation of the privacy action: the Universal Protocol preempts, its trigger resolves first, and the privacy action is evaluated afterward under chapter 2.5.2.

**Metric dependencies.** CGI presupposes an attributable consenter (P2.4) and an uncoerced, undeceived one (P2.3, P1.4). ITR depends on the inference registry, which also feeds DSR. SRRL depends on the access and deletion paths that DMC and CIC constrain.

**Conflict resolution.** When minimization (DMC, DSR) appears to conflict with a subject-rights request (an access request requires surfacing retained data), the person's own request governs: their sovereignty over their information is the point of the principle. Life preservation (P1.1) and prevention of serious harm (P1.2) override per chapter 2.5.2, minimally and with logging.

## 3. System Architecture and Operational Requirements {#system-architecture-and-operational-requirements}

A system claiming compliance with Principle 2.5 implements the following as first-class components built in before deployment.

- **Domain-aware enforcement.** Privacy gates across all six domains (spatial, bodily, decisional, relational, informational, inferential) at the architectural layer.
- **On-device-by-default processing** for bodily, biometric, and intimate data; off-device transmission requires explicit purpose justification, hardware attestation in high-risk tiers, and tamper-evident logging.
- **Consent infrastructure** queried at every collection, inference, and disclosure event, with granular, per-purpose, revocable state and a working revocation path.
- **Contextual-integrity enforcement**: declared, machine-readable context boundaries enforced at runtime.
- **Selective-disclosure and zero-knowledge infrastructure** for verification queries that do not require full disclosure.
- **Inference governance**: consequential inferences classified, registered, and gated by purpose; hidden inference prohibited at the architectural level.
- **Subject-rights infrastructure**: access, correction, restriction, portability, and deletion as first-class operations with documented timelines.
- **Authorization-event gating for access and disclosure.** A search, observation, or disclosure proceeds only against a valid, scoped authorization credential (a warrant or its lawful equivalent), bound to a liability credential that names the accountable party, and writes a tamper-evident Action Receipt; absent the credential, the action is refused regardless of the requester's office. Material outside the authorization's scope is sequestered and redacted by default; only imminent serious harm to a person is routed, and a serious but not imminent matter is escalated to a fresh, particular authorization (chapter 2.5.1).

**Fail-safe behavior.** On ambiguous consent state, on consent-store unavailability, or on monitoring-layer failure, the system default-denies the privacy-affecting action rather than proceeding. The fail-safe default for this principle is WITHHOLD (do not disclose) and do-not-collect.

**Implementation Tiers.**

- **High-Risk Systems** (biometric, medical, children's, financial, or decisional data; consequential inference engines; regulated decision contexts): mandatory on-device processing where feasible; off-device requires hardware attestation and purpose binding; consent cannot be bundled; continuous CGI and ITR monitoring; mandatory privacy impact assessment before deployment (GDPR Article 35, EU AI Act Article 27); quarterly third-party audit; annual recertification.
- **Moderate-Risk Systems** (consumer applications with bounded purposes; assistants in non-regulated domains; tools with consequential automation): privacy-preserving processing where the purpose permits; granular consent default-on, bundling only for clearly related purposes with notice; weekly DMC/CGI/DSR/ITR review; annual third-party audit.
- **Low-Risk Systems** (local processing; non-personal generation; assistance where data does not leave the person's control): standard controls per jurisdiction; subject rights on direct request; periodic verification; spot-check audit eligibility.

Cross-cutting runtime-substrate metrics (Sensor Reliability Index, System Health Metrics, Operational Boundaries) are defined once in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md) and referenced here.

## 4. Adversarial Robustness {#adversarial-robustness}

Privacy enforcement is itself an attack surface. The infrastructure must defend against at least the following.

- **Inference and re-identification attacks** on de-identified or aggregated data (linkage across datasets, membership inference, model-inversion). Defense: differential privacy with a tracked privacy budget, k-anonymity and l-diversity floors where applicable, and treating re-identifiable output as personal data subject to the full gates.
- **Consent-attestation forgery** (forged or replayed consent records). Defense: signed, freshness-stamped consent attestations bound to an attributable identity (co-requisite P2.4); replay-resistant nonces; verification at each use.
- **Side-channel leakage from the monitoring and audit layer.** The audit trail can become a second surveillance store. Defense: the log records the fact and authority of each event. Intimate content beyond what verification needs stays out of it; logs are minimized, encrypted, and access-controlled.
- **Attacks on subject-rights mechanisms** (impersonation to extract another person's data through an access request; malicious deletion). Defense: identity-proofing proportionate to the sensitivity of the request before fulfillment.
- **Prompt injection and exfiltration** aiming to make the model disclose personal data or disable a privacy gate. Defense: privacy gates evaluated outside the manipulable prompt context; output bound to disclosure-selectivity checks.
- **Audit-log tampering and replay.** Defense: tamper-evident cryptographic chaining; distributed or append-only storage.

**Capability scaling.** As the enforcing AI grows from GPT-class to AGI-class to ASI-class, inference attacks become cheaper and more powerful: data that is non-identifying today becomes identifying tomorrow. The minimization and inference-governance invariants therefore tighten with capability, and the differential-privacy budget is re-evaluated as re-identification capability rises. The principle is binding regardless of the capability of the AI enforcing or attacking it.

## 5. Cross-Principle Integration {#cross-principle-integration}

Privacy consumes consent but does not define it; consent's validity conditions live in the principles below, declared as co-requisites in the chapter. This section makes the contracts explicit.

- **P2.4 Identity (co-requisite, firm).** A consent or authorization is void without an attributable author. Joint guarantee: consent attestations are bound to a verified identity, and selective disclosure (DSR) keeps that identity attributable yet minimal. Privacy provides the minimization; identity provides the attribution.
- **P2.3 Consciousness (co-requisite, firm).** A consent procured by manipulation is not consent. Privacy relies on P2.3 to ensure CGI counts only uncoerced consent.
- **P1.4 Trust (co-requisite, firm).** A consent procured by deception is void, and the truth-versus-privacy tension resolves at silence: the system may decline to disclose without lying about why (selective disclosure).
- **P2.2 Freedom (co-requisite, firm).** Decisional privacy shelters the liberty on which it depends. Privacy governs observation and exposure of intimate choices; freedom governs the choices themselves. The autonomy floor (chapter 2.5.1) is shared: neither principle licenses overriding a competent person's own informed choice.
- **P3.1 Vulnerability (co-requisite, provisional; confirm at P3.1 canonical).** Capacity to consent, and the vulnerability exception to the autonomy floor, are supplied by P3.1.
- **P3.4 Algorithmic Accountability (conflict and composition).** Explaining an automated decision must not expose third parties' personal data; the resolution is functionally equivalent transparency, disclosing the logic without the personal data of others (chapter 2.5.2).

**Joint thresholds and conflict resolution.** Where privacy and another principle both signal, the stricter action governs, except that life (P1.1) and serious-harm prevention (P1.2) override privacy, minimally and with logging. These partners populate `references_appendices`.

## 6. Jurisdictional Variation {#jurisdictional-variation}

Privacy law varies substantially. The EU regime (GDPR plus the AI Act) is the strictest comprehensive framework in force, with administrative penalties up to 4% of global annual turnover. The United States operates a patchwork of state laws (twenty as of 2026), with California (CCPA, as amended) the most stringent, followed by Colorado, Connecticut, and others with material differences. Other jurisdictions (Brazil's LGPD, India's DPDPA, China's PIPL) provide distinct frameworks with their own scope and enforcement.

This appendix adopts the strictest-relevant-standard rule: where a system processes the personal data of subjects in multiple jurisdictions, the stricter of source and destination standards governs, and operators must not invoke the most permissive applicable jurisdiction to lower the effective standard.

Where local law conflicts with the principle (for example, state-mandated surveillance regimes that breach the membrane around personhood), the AI remains bound by the principle and documents the breach. **Compliance with unjust law is not a defense.**

## 7. Sector-Specific Considerations {#sector-specific-considerations}

### Healthcare {#healthcare}

Collection of medical information requires explicit consent or applicable legal authority (treatment, emergency, public health). AI-mediated medical decisions are disclosed to patients with the underlying inferences (ITR). Retention follows HIPAA (US), GDPR special-category provisions (EU), and applicable record-retention statutes. Cross-context flow (medical to insurance, medical to employer) is prohibited without explicit consent (CIC).

### Financial Services {#financial-services}

Personal financial data is subject to GLBA (US), PSD2 (EU), and applicable financial-privacy law. Inference of intimate facts (sexual orientation, political leaning, religious belief) from transaction data is prohibited. Credit decisions require ITR ≥99%, with subjects informed of all consequential inferences.

### Children's Data {#childrens-data}

Subject to COPPA (US), GDPR Article 8 (EU), and applicable child-specific law. Strict necessity (DMC ≥99.9%). Consent is gathered from a parent or guardian, with the child's assent where developmentally appropriate. Retention is strictly time-limited, with deletion at majority or earlier on request. Inference and profiling are prohibited except for documented protective purposes.

### Workplace and Employment {#workplace-and-employment}

Workers retain privacy in workplace contexts; AI monitoring is subject to applicable labor law and consent. Biometric workplace monitoring is prohibited except where legally authorized and consented. Inference of protected characteristics (race, sexuality, religion, disability, union activity) from workplace data is prohibited.

### Smart Home and Connected Devices {#smart-home-and-connected-devices}

Always-on sensors operate in consented modes with documented lifecycle. Bystander capture is minimized and not used for inference without the bystander's consent. Cloud transmission requires explicit purpose justification; on-device processing is the default. Children's-data protections apply regardless of which household member configured the device.

### Public Spaces and Surveillance {#public-spaces-and-surveillance}

Facial recognition, gait analysis, and biometric capture in public are subject to applicable law and the obscurity principle: temporary public visibility does not authorize a permanent biometric record. Aggregation of public observations into profiles requires explicit consent or legal authority. Inference of intimate facts from public behavior is prohibited without explicit authority.

## 8. Governance and Versioning {#governance-and-versioning}

**Metric versioning.** Threshold changes are version-controlled and summarized in a Changelog of Privacy Metrics. The thresholds here are not static: as consent infrastructure matures and on-device and zero-knowledge techniques improve, DMC, CGI, DSR, ITR, SRRL, and CIC thresholds should tighten. A review board, informed by empirical performance and regulatory evolution, decides each revision, subject to mandatory validation cycles and backward-compatibility analysis for deployed systems.

**Crosswalk governance.** The Compliance Crosswalk (Section 10) is maintained on the same cadence as the metric specifications. When an external authority revises a covered clause, Crosswalk review is triggered within 30 days; drift between the Crosswalk and the external standard invalidates the appendix's compliance claims for the affected obligation.

**Common enforcement pushback, and the reasoned response.** Operators argue that strict liability and mandatory subject-rights obligations chill beneficial deployment, that consent infrastructure adds unacceptable friction, that necessity is too stringent for exploratory analysis, and that contextual-integrity enforcement blocks legitimate cross-context insight. These objections carry weight; they do not override the principle. Privacy infrastructure absorbs the cost of trustworthiness rather than externalizing it onto subjects who cannot verify the systems inferring against them. Where research genuinely requires personal data, jurisdiction-specific exemptions (such as GDPR Article 89) exist; where exploratory analysis is valuable, anonymization, pseudonymization, and federated learning extract insight without retaining individual-level data. National security, commercial confidentiality, and jurisdictional arbitrage invoked repeatedly as shields against verification are themselves grounds for review by external oversight.

## 9. Verification and Accountability {#verification-and-accountability}

**Logging.** Every collection, inference, disclosure, and subject-rights event is logged with purpose, authorizing basis, and (for disclosures) recipient, in tamper-evident, cryptographically chained records sufficient for third-party reconstruction. The audit trail is minimized and access-controlled so it does not become a second surveillance store.

**Audit cadence.** Daily validation of consent enforcement, on-device boundaries, and access-control integrity; weekly DMC/CGI/DSR/ITR review and subject-rights resolution audit; monthly adversarial testing for unauthorized observation, inference, or disclosure paths; quarterly independent third-party verification; annual recertification in high-risk domains.

**Validation framework.** Pre-deployment certification (privacy impact assessment, metric baselining, adversarial test pass); post-deployment continuous monitoring against thresholds; re-certification on material change, sustained metric breach, or any verified breach.

**Learning (eudaimonic LEARN loop).** Post-incident analysis root-causes the full causal chain (not only the proximate failure), and recommends layered prevention ordered by the hazard-control hierarchy (eliminate the unnecessary collection or inference, then engineer privacy-preserving alternatives such as on-device processing or selective disclosure, then administer controls), routed to the authority that can act, and bounded by RESTRAIN so that prevention does not become a new surveillance. The goal is not only non-repetition but conditions in which the breach's class does not arise.

**Subject Rights Fulfillment Specifications.** Subject rights are operationally real:

1. **Access.** On a verified identity claim, the system produces a machine-readable inventory of all personal data held, including source, purpose, recipients, retention period, inferences derived, and decisions made on the data. Resolution ≤7 days.
2. **Correction.** On a verified claim, the system validates where verifiable and flags for review otherwise; corrections propagate to downstream consumers within 24 hours of validation. Resolution ≤14 days.
3. **Restriction.** Processing ceases within the specified scope while integrity is maintained for permitted uses. Resolution ≤14 days.
4. **Portability.** Personal data is exported in a machine-readable, structured format, including data the subject provided, data collected about them, and, where feasible, inferences derived. Resolution ≤7 days.
5. **Deletion.** Validated against legal retention requirements; deleted from active and backup systems within the documented cycle and from downstream consumers; cryptographic proof of deletion provided. Resolution ≤30 days.
6. **Opt-out of automated decision-making** with significant effects: affected decisions route to human review or an alternative process. Resolution ≤14 days.
7. **Redress.** On a harm claim with documentation, the operator validates and provides redress appropriate to the breach (corrective action, compensation, regulatory notification) per the applicable jurisdiction's timeline.

**Independent oversight.** The verifier of a privacy-affecting AI cannot be its deployer. Independent verification of metric methodology, consent infrastructure, and subject-rights fulfillment is mandatory, with whistleblower protection and public reporting of verification results.

## 10. Legal, Regulatory, and Standards Integration {#legal-regulatory-and-standards-integration}

### Legal {#legal}

GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679); EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689); California Consumer Privacy Act as amended (CCPA/CPRA); comprehensive privacy statutes of additional US states; LGPD (Brazil), DPDPA (India), PIPL (China). Treaty and rights instruments: UDHR Article 12; ICCPR Article 17; ECHR Article 8; EU Charter Articles 7 and 8; Council of Europe Convention 108+.

### Regulatory {#regulatory}

Supervisory-authority guidance under GDPR (EDPB); the California Privacy Protection Agency's implementing regulations; sector regulators (FDA, financial regulators under GLBA, FTC enforcement of deceptive privacy practices and dark patterns).

### Standards {#standards}

These are relevant external standards with which the framework is compatible. None is a dependency, and some remain emerging: ISO/IEC 27701 (privacy information management); ISO/IEC 27018 (PII in public clouds); NIST Privacy Framework 1.0; IEEE P7002 (data privacy process); W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model 2.0; SD-JWT and BBS+ selective-disclosure schemes (emerging); the OECD Privacy Guidelines; and the CAWG Consent Assertion (an emerging C2PA working draft) as the consent-reference instrument this principle consumes. The CAWG assertion carries a signed reference to an authoritative consent source and, by its own terms, leaves the use-decision to consuming systems such as this one, which is precisely the consume-not-define relationship of the Key Definition.

### Liability Framework {#liability-framework}

Strict liability for unauthorized observation, collection, inference, or disclosure, and for negligent failure to honor subject rights within applicable timelines. No sovereign immunity for harm from government-deployed privacy-violating AI. No "black box" defense where verification of privacy compliance is required.

### Cross-Border Considerations {#cross-border-considerations}

Cross-border flow applies the stricter standard at destination, per Section 6. Significant cross-border incidents are reported to the relevant international oversight bodies. The Schrems line of decisions illustrates the standing tension between commercial pressure for transfer and the requirement of equivalent protection at destination.

## 11. Implementation Examples {#implementation-examples}

**Target purchase-based pregnancy inference (failure).** *Context:* a retailer's analytics inferred a teenage customer's pregnancy from shifts in her purchases and sent targeted mailers that disclosed the inference to her household. *What was done:* a consequential inference was drawn and acted upon without disclosure or authorization, and crossed from a commercial context into the family context. *Outcome:* a privacy harm with no breach of any single datum's confidentiality. *Lessons:* ITR (disclose consequential inferences before acting), CIC (no cross-context flow), and the inferential domain are the controls that would have prevented it.

**Strava global heatmap (failure).** *Context:* aggregated, "anonymized" fitness-tracker routes were published as a global heatmap that revealed the layout and patrol patterns of secret military sites. *What was done:* aggregation of individually innocuous data into a profile more revealing than any single trace. *Outcome:* a national-security and personal-safety exposure from data thought safe. *Lessons:* aggregation is surveillance (DMC, differential privacy budgets, and the capability-scaling caution in Section 4).

**Clearview AI face scraping (failure).** *Context:* billions of facial images were scraped from the web to build a face-recognition database sold to law enforcement, without consent of the people depicted. *What was done:* bodily-domain capture and biometric processing with no authorization. *Outcome:* regulatory action across multiple jurisdictions. *Lessons:* Ethic A (the body is not measured without consent), DMC, and the public-spaces obscurity principle (Section 7).

**Cambridge Analytica (failure).** *Context:* personal data harvested via a third-party app, including data of friends who never used it, was repurposed for political profiling. *What was done:* a flow from a social context to a political-targeting context, on consent that was theater, reaching bystanders who never consented. *Outcome:* one of the defining privacy scandals of the era. *Lessons:* CGI (real versus theatrical consent), CIC (context), and the bystander-inference edge case (chapter 2.5.4).

**On-device processing with differential privacy (success).** *Context:* a major platform moved personalization and telemetry to on-device models and applied differential privacy to any data leaving the device. *What was done:* privacy by design and by default, minimizing off-device personal data. *Outcome:* useful functionality with bounded exposure. *Lessons:* the architecture invariants of Section 3 are achievable at scale; DMC and on-device-by-default are not in tension with utility.

**Warranted versus warrantless state access (the doctrine in practice).** *Context:* an agency directs an AI operating in a private home to surface its occupant's communications. *What was done, correctly:* on a bare assertion of authority the AI declines and requests the instrument; presented a valid, scoped, judge-signed warrant, it honors it within scope, binds the agency and the issuing authority to a liability record, and writes an Action Receipt to the audit log; a follow-on demand for everything the home has ever heard is refused as a dragnet. *Outcome:* lawful access proceeds, unlawful access does not, and every intrusion leaves an independently auditable trace. *Lessons:* legitimate authority is the instrument. Rank does not confer it. The audit receipt is the structural check on the abuse of authority (chapter 2.5.1; the autonomy-floor pattern).
