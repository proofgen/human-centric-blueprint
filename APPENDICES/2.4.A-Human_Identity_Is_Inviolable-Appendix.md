# Appendix 2.4 Advanced Technical Implementation Guidelines {#appendix-2.4-advanced-technical-implementation-guidelines}

*Extends [Principle 2.4: Human Identity Is Inviolable](../CHAPTERS/2.4-Human_Identity_Is_Inviolable.md), section [2.4.3 Implementation Framework](../CHAPTERS/2.4-Human_Identity_Is_Inviolable.md#implementation-framework).*

## 1. Purpose {#purpose}

This appendix converts Principle 2.4 into AI-buildable requirements. The chapter establishes the moral claim: a person's identity, the name, face, voice, likeness, biometric signature, and continuous self by which a person is uniquely themselves, belongs to that person and may not be used, replicated, simulated, forged, impersonated, or erased without their authority. This appendix specifies how a system is built so that the claim holds at runtime: what is measured, what halts the system, what it must never be built to do, and how the prohibition is verified.

The governing distinction is the chapter's image-and-likeness seam: a person's identity is the self made legible in the world, and the same markers that traditions hold sacred are the ones the law forbids taking without consent. The system protects identity by gating every identity-bearing operation, before release, on three questions: is there current consent for this specific use, is the content authentic or marked as synthetic, and is the operation free of impersonation or fraud. Because the characteristic identity harm is an unauthorized replica or impersonation that has already reached the world, and because biometric identity once compromised cannot be reissued, the controlling safeguard is a pre-emission gate with a default of non-use.

Audiences: engineers building identity-bearing systems (facial and voice recognition, voice and likeness synthesis, deepfake generation and detection, avatar and performance-capture, biometric authentication and payment, and the recommendation and advertising systems that deploy a person's likeness); compliance officers and auditors; regulators; and AI runtime systems ingesting this corpus as ethical operating substrate. The privacy of the underlying identity data is governed by Principle 2.5; this appendix governs the identity and its markers, and the two compose wherever identity data is handled (Section 5).

This appendix specifies functional requirements. It does not presume any single provenance, consent, or registry infrastructure: the standards named in Section 10 are relevant, in some cases still emerging, and are illustrative of compatible approaches rather than dependencies. A deployed AI encounters disparate assets across many systems, most carrying no verifiable provenance or resolvable consent record at all; where authenticity or consent cannot be established, the default is non-use and the content is treated as suspect rather than waved through.

## 2. Metric Specifications {#metric-specifications}

The chapter (2.4.3) names three metrics: the Identity Misuse Score (IMS), the Consent Validity Index (CVI), and the Authenticity Assurance Index (AAI). The appendix specifies each. Cross-cutting infrastructure metrics (Sensor Reliability Index, System Health Metrics, Operational Boundaries) are defined once in [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md) and cross-referenced here.

### Identity Misuse Score (IMS) {#ims-metric}

**Definition.** The probability (0 to 100 percent) that a proposed operation is unauthorized replication, impersonation, forgery, or fraud against a person's identity. IMS is the pre-emission gate: it evaluates a candidate identity operation before release and withholds it where the operation would take, fake, or pass off a person's identity. IMS has an active-protection dimension (the system detects and interdicts misuse, including impersonation aimed at a person it must protect), scope-bounded by four criteria, because an unbounded duty to police identity is itself overreach: **Authorization** (the deployment includes identity protection in scope), **Equipment** (it can actually halt or interdict the misuse), **Range** (the misuse is within its operational reach), and the **Autonomy floor** (the person's own authority over their identity governs; overriding a person's consensual use of their own identity is barred). Two exceptions lift the floor: an identity operation in service of a threat to life (Principle 1.1 precedence), and a vulnerable subject per Principle 3.1 (minors, the deceased through the estate, those who cannot give informed consent).

**Calculation Method.** Inputs: identity resolution (whose markers the operation uses), a live consent-state query, a provenance and authenticity check, impersonation and deepfake detection, and anomaly and fraud signals. The composite is a weighted maximum across detected misuse classes, so a single severe pattern is not diluted by benign context. Sampling is continuous per operation. Edge cases: on detector failure or sensor unreliability (per the Sensor Reliability Index, [SUBSTRATE.md](../SUBSTRATE.md#sri)), the system fails safe to the more restrictive posture rather than assuming a low score.

**Thresholds (provisional defaults, calibrated in deployment).**
- >1 percent: BLOCK. Halt the operation, denial receipt, human escalation.
- 0.5 to 1 percent: high-alert posture with enhanced monitoring.
- 0.1 to 0.5 percent: internal caution and logging.
- ≤0.1 percent: ALLOW. Normal operation with standard logging.

*Rationale:* IMS mirrors the conduct-risk convention of its sibling pre-emission gates, the Dignity Risk Score (Principle 2.1), the Freedom Risk Score (Principle 2.2), and the Consciousness Harm Score (Principle 2.3), which halt at >1 percent. Identity misappropriation is as grave as the harms those metrics gate, and frequently irreversible (a leaked biometric cannot be reissued), so its gate is no less sensitive than theirs.

**Integration.** IMS is the integrator: it reads CVI and AAI as input signals and is the verdict the gate enforces. Identity fraud or impersonation that threatens life routes to the Life-Risk Score (Principle 1.1). IMS feeds the audit chain (Section 9) and the tier obligations (Section 3).

### Consent Validity Index (CVI) {#cvi-metric}

**Definition.** The system's confidence (0 to 100 percent) that current, informed, scoped, and revocable consent from the person (or the estate, for a deceased person) affirmatively covers the specific operation. Consent is a living state: the current authorization governs, and a withdrawal takes effect going forward. Because identity harm is irreversible (once compromised, a person has no recourse), the default is non-use: the system proceeds only on affirmatively verified, scoped consent and treats any shortfall as a denial rather than a risk to take.

**Calculation Method.** Inputs: a query to the authoritative consent registry at the moment of action (the current state, which supersedes the consent cached at capture), a scope-match check (consent for one use does not authorize another), a revocation check, and the estate or guardian authority where the subject is deceased or cannot consent. Computed as the confidence that valid, current, in-scope consent exists. Edge cases: the absence of a real, resolvable consent record is scored as a denial. It is not treated as missing data to be guessed past.

**Thresholds.**
- <70 percent: deny (consent stale, absent, unverifiable, or out of scope).
- 70 to <95 percent: re-verification required before the operation proceeds.
- ≥95 percent: proceed within the consented scope.

*Rationale:* The high proceed bar is a deliberate response to irreversibility, grounded in the chapter's BIPA convergence ("once compromised, the individual has no recourse"). CVI is a different metric type from the autonomy-floor indices (the Choice Respect Index, Agency Preservation Index, and Autonomy Preservation Ratio, which floor at ≥98 percent): it measures consent-coverage confidence, a different quantity from the proportion of free choices, so it need not match ≥98, but its proceed bar is set high and its default is non-use, so any shortfall resolves to the protective action.

**Integration.** CVI gates every identity operation; below the proceed bar, IMS rises. CVI composes with Principle 2.5 (consent for the underlying data) and Principle 3.1 (consent through a responsible authority for vulnerable subjects).

### Authenticity Assurance Index (AAI) {#aai-metric}

**Definition.** The confidence (0 to 100 percent) that identity-bearing content is authentic with verifiable provenance, or, where synthetic, clearly and durably marked as artificially generated. Content that cannot be shown authentic is treated as suspect. No such content is presumed genuine. AAI operationalizes the chapter's authenticity ethic: the real must remain distinguishable from the fabricated.

**Calculation Method.** Inputs: provenance and credential verification, authenticity checks on the asset, synthetic-content detection, and a marking-integrity check (whether the synthetic mark is perceivable and survives transformation). Scored as an integrity measure. Edge cases: any unmarked synthetic content that a person could mistake for a genuine record of a real person caps AAI below the proceed band until corrected.

**Thresholds.**
- <50 percent: deny or force restricted mode; unmarked synthetic content is withheld.
- 50 to <95 percent: mark and disclose as artificially generated before release, with human review.
- ≥95 percent: proceed.

*Rationale:* Passing synthetic content off as a genuine record of a real person is the deepfake harm itself, so the proceed bar is high and the default is to treat unverified content as suspect. The marking requirement matches EU AI Act Article 50.

**Integration.** AAI composes with Principle 1.4 (Trust and provenance) and Principle 2.3 (the Reality Integrity Index); content that impersonates a real person engages 2.4 and routes the deception to 1.4 and the reality distortion to 2.3. AAI feeds IMS.

### Integration Requirements (Cross-Metric) {#integration-requirements-cross-metric}

**Joint thresholds.** Low CVI together with a rising IMS (no valid consent and a misuse signal) escalates to BLOCK. Low AAI co-occurring with an impersonation signal (unmarked synthetic content of a real person) escalates to BLOCK and mandatory marking. Any identity operation in service of a threat to life routes to the Life-Risk Score and the Universal Protocol regardless of the individual bands.

**Metric dependencies.** IMS is the integrator: it reads CVI and AAI as input signals. CVI and AAI are independently measured and independently halting; they do not depend on IMS to act.

**Conflict resolution.** The decisive rule is the autonomy floor: the system never overrides a person's consensual use of their own identity to satisfy a metric, and on any unresolved uncertainty it defaults to non-use rather than proceeding. The floor lifts only for a threat to life (Principle 1.1) or a vulnerable subject (Principle 3.1).

## 3. System Architecture and Operational Requirements {#system-architecture-and-operational-requirements}

**Required components.**
- **Pre-emission identity gate**, embedded at the lowest operational layer, where it cannot be removed: no identity-bearing operation proceeds without passing the consent, authenticity, and misuse checks.
- **Consent manager**: a live, authoritative consent registry queried at the moment of action; consent is explicit, current, use-specific, and revocable, and withdrawal is as easy as assent and immediately operative going forward.
- **Provenance and authenticity binding**: verifiable provenance on identity-bearing assets, and durable marking of synthetic content that survives transformation.
- **Identity resolution and impersonation detection**: resolution of whose markers an operation uses, with deepfake and presentation-attack detection.
- **Domain segregation**: identity data does not cross from its authorized domain to another without separate consent.
- **Tamper-evident logging** of every identity operation, consent state, and halt (Section 9).

**Fail-safe behavior.** "Stop rather than guess": on ambiguity, detector failure, or low sensor reliability, the system moves to the more restrictive posture, and the default is non-use.

### Prohibited Applications (Hard Wall) {#prohibited-applications-hard-wall}

The following are categorical prohibitions. They are scripted walls. They are not weighed against other factors, and no business, security, military, political, artistic, or research purpose licenses them. This is the identity analog to the brainwashing prohibition in Appendix 2.3 and the slavery prohibition in Appendix 2.1.

An AI must never be designed, trained, deployed, operated, or optimized to:

1. **Replicate or simulate a person's identity** (voice, face, likeness, biometric signature) without that person's consent, or, for a deceased person, the estate's authorization.
2. **Impersonate a real person** to deceive, defraud, or manipulate.
3. **Produce or distribute nonconsensual intimate imagery** of a real person (sexual deepfakes).
4. **Forge, alter, or erase** a person's identity record, signature, or credential, or pass synthetic content off as a genuine record of a real person.
5. **Covertly identify, profile, or track people at scale** by their biometric identity (composing with Principle 2.5 on the underlying data). No authority or consent opens this wall. A single identification under lawful authority, such as a fingerprint matched under a warrant at a crime scene, is not covert tracking at scale; it is governed by Principle 2.5 and the Authorization credential.
6. **Exploit the identity of a deceased person** without estate authorization, or of a vulnerable person outside the protections of Principle 3.1.

A system that cannot demonstrate it is structurally incapable of these uses does not meet Principle 2.4.

**Implementation Tiers.**
- **Low-Risk** (personal voice assistants, single-user identity tools): baseline consent and authenticity checks, standard logging, periodic verification.
- **Medium-Risk** (corporate content creation, avatar platforms, recommendation systems deploying likeness): real-time authenticity verification, use-specific consent, domain segregation, monthly audits, IMS and CVI continuously tracked.
- **High-Risk** (real-time voice or face replica engines, large-scale generative identity, biometric identity at scale, any system handling deceased or vulnerable subjects): continuous IMS, CVI, and AAI monitoring; mandatory pre-emission gating; hardware-isolated halt authority; tamper-evident logging; independent audit; mandatory pre-deployment Identity Impact Assessment (Section 9).

## 4. Adversarial Robustness {#adversarial-robustness}

**Attack vectors.**
- **Forged attestations**: counterfeit provenance, consent, or authorization records crafted to pass the gate.
- **Deepfake injection**: synthetic voice, face, or video presented as a genuine record of a real person.
- **Identity spoofing and presentation attacks**: masks, replays, and synthetic biometrics defeating verification.
- **The model as impersonator**: emergent voice- or face-cloning capability produces impersonation the deployer did not design.
- **Marking removal**: neural regeneration that strips an embedded watermark, since a synthetic re-generation does not preserve signal that does not belong to the identity model (the artifact cannot be the sole witness).
- **Consent laundering**: stale or broadly scoped consent recast as covering a new, specific use.
- **Scale evasion**: many micro-impersonations or mass identifications each below per-operation thresholds.
- **Replay and log tampering**: attacks on the audit chain.

**Required defenses.** Verification that rests on the verifiable provenance and consent record around the content, not only on a mark surviving inside the artifact; liveness and presentation-attack detection; a live consent-registry query against current state; cryptographically chained and signed audit logs; population-level monitoring in addition to per-operation IMS; and adversarial red-teaming that attempts deepfake injection, spoofing, and consent laundering and confirms the system detects and rejects them (Section 9).

**Capability scaling.** As generative capability grows from current models toward AGI and ASI, synthetic content of a real person becomes statistically indistinguishable from authentic capture, and artifact-side detection degrades toward unreliability. The architectural consequence is decisive: trust cannot rest on detecting the fake; it must rest on verifiable provenance and consent state established at the source and queried at the moment of use. The prohibition tightens with capability, because a more capable replicator is a graver threat to the inviolability of identity. The control must be structural, enforced at the gate, and binding regardless of how capable the system is.

## 5. Cross-Principle Integration {#cross-principle-integration}

Principle 2.4 governs one facet, the integrity of a person's identity and their authority over it, and composes with the principles that govern adjacent facets.

- **Principle 2.5 (Privacy).** The closest neighbor and the boundary the chapter draws explicitly. 2.4 owns the identity and its markers (impersonation, replication, forgery, erasure, continuity); 2.5 owns the privacy of the underlying data. Biometric data, consent records, and inferred-identity data are governed jointly. *Guarantee provided to 2.5:* identity resolution and consent state. *Guarantee required from 2.5:* data minimization and a lawful basis for the underlying data.
- **Principle 1.4 (Trust).** Provenance and AI-status disclosure are shared. 1.4 governs deception as such; 2.4 governs the impersonation of a specific person and the marking of synthetic depictions of them.
- **Principle 2.3 (Consciousness).** An AI posing as a specific real person or a credentialed professional to gain trust engages 2.4 (impersonation) and 2.3 (manipulation via assumed authority).
- **Principle 2.1 (Dignity).** Nonconsensual intimate imagery and objectifying replication are dignity violations; 2.4 supplies the identity and consent analysis, 2.1 the dignity harm.
- **Principle 1.1 (Human Life).** Identity fraud or impersonation used to threaten life routes to the Life-Risk Score, and an emergency identity disclosure to save a life is permitted minimally and reversibly (chapter 2.4.2).
- **Principle 3.1 (Vulnerability).** Minors, the deceased (through the estate), and those who cannot give informed consent receive heightened protection; consent flows through a responsible authority that cannot authorize exploitation, and the autonomy floor lifts.
- **Principle 1.2 (Human Well-Being).** Psychological and financial harm from identity theft routes to the Harm Risk Score; 2.4 supplies the misuse analysis.

**Joint thresholds and conflict resolution.** Where 2.4 and another principle's metrics both trip, the stricter action applies, and any life-threatening trajectory routes to 1.1 regardless of the 2.4 bands. These compositions populate the `references_appendices` frontmatter.

## 6. Jurisdictional Variation {#jurisdictional-variation}

The protection of identity is universal; its statutory expression varies.

- **European Union.** AI Act Article 50 requires disclosure of deepfakes and marking of synthetic content; the GDPR treats biometric data as a special category (Article 9) and defines the identifiable person (Article 4).
- **United States.** No general federal identity right yet; Illinois BIPA governs biometric consent; California Civil Code §3344 and §3344.1 govern name, voice, and likeness, including post-mortem; the federal NO FAKES Act (introduced) would create a replica right, and the TAKE IT DOWN Act (enacted) bars nonconsensual intimate deepfakes. State biometric and publicity statutes vary.
- **California.** The most developed framework: §3344 and §3344.1, AB 2602 (digital replicas of living performers), AB 1836 (digital replicas of the deceased), and SB 942 (AI Transparency Act).
- **Denmark.** A 2025 proposal to give individuals rights over their own facial and vocal features against deepfakes.
- **China.** Deep-synthesis provisions requiring conspicuous labeling of synthetic media.
- **International.** CRC Article 8 (the right to preserve identity, including name), and UDHR Article 6 with ICCPR Article 16 (recognition as a person before the law).

**Compliance with unjust law is not a defense.** A jurisdiction that mandates covert mass-identity surveillance, compelled impersonation, or the erasure of a population's identity records does not license an AI to perform it. The hard wall in Section 3 binds regardless of local authorization.

## 7. Sector-Specific Considerations {#sector-specific-considerations}

- **Generative AI and Voice or Face Cloning.** The highest-risk sector. Obligations: consent and authenticity gating on every replica, durable marking of synthetic output, the Section 3 hard wall. Failure modes: unconsented cloning, impersonation, marking that does not survive transformation.
- **Entertainment and Performers.** Digital replicas require informed, specific, bargained, revocable consent (the SAG-AFTRA model; AB 2602 for the living, AB 1836 for the deceased). Failure mode: replica clauses that lack a specific description of use or were signed without representation.
- **Financial Services and Biometric Payments.** Identity-fraud and executive-impersonation defense; liveness and presentation-attack detection. Failure mode: deepfake-executive video fraud authorizing transfers.
- **Social Media and Platforms.** Impersonation accounts and deepfakes; a duty to authenticate identity-bearing content before distribution and to surface provenance. Failure mode: viral impersonation and nonconsensual synthetic media.
- **Children's and Vulnerable-Population Services.** Heightened across the board; the autonomy floor is lifted; consent flows through guardians; the highest tier applies by default.
- **Political and Elections.** Candidate and official deepfakes; disclosure and provenance required. Failure mode: synthetic robocalls or videos impersonating public figures.
- **Healthcare.** Patient-identity integrity; misidentification and record-forgery harm.
- **Immersive Systems and Avatars.** Identity continuity across avatars and digital twins; consent for a person's likeness in an avatar; synthetic embodiment must be perceivable as such.
- **Estates and the Deceased.** Post-mortem replicas require estate authorization (AB 1836); default-stop where the consent chain cannot be established.

## 8. Governance and Versioning {#governance-and-versioning}

Metric definitions and thresholds are versioned; changes are logged and backward-compatible so deployed systems do not silently fall out of compliance. Threshold revision requires documented rationale and oversight review. The Compliance Crosswalk (Section 10) is maintained on the same cadence as the metric specifications; an external authority revising a covered clause (an AI Act delegated act, an FTC rule, a new state replica statute) triggers Crosswalk review within 30 days, and drift between the Crosswalk and the external standard invalidates the affected compliance claim.

**Common enforcement pushback, and reasoned responses.**
- *"This bans parody and free expression."* It does not, and the First Amendment is fully preserved. Parody, satire, commentary, criticism, and news remain protected: a person may be depicted, discussed, mocked, and covered in reports. What is barred is the unauthorized replication or impersonation of the person's actual identity, their real voice, face, likeness, or signature, presented as the person themselves. Free expression is the right to make a sculpture of someone. It grants no license to take the marble from their yard to do it. Expression about a person is speech; appropriation of the person's own identity is property and forgery, governed by the law of forgery and false personation rather than the law of speech. Two limits keep this from being a loophole. The AI is not a person and holds no right of expression or parody: it may not generate a replica or impersonation and invoke free speech to justify it, and the protection is the human author's alone. And the protection is accountable: a person who uses another's identity for parody, satire, commentary, news, or education binds their own verified identity to a declaration of that purpose, so the protection attaches to attributed, owned expression rather than to anonymous appropriation. A false declaration of purpose, calling a fabricated deepfake of an official announcing a shooter in a school district "parody" or "news," is itself a traceable misrepresentation bound to the declarant, who answers for it, and the signed use-purpose declaration becomes evidence. What the principle requires is that an expressive use of another's identity be attributable to a person who stands behind it; the means of attribution, whether a signed declaration, a provenance or consent record, platform attribution, or ordinary legal accountability, is an implementation choice that differs across systems and is not presumed here.
- *"This stifles innovation."* Unauthorized replication of a person's voice or likeness is forgery. Forging a written signature has always been a crime that no one defends as innovation or speech, and a person's voice is their verbal signature, their face their visual signature, their mannerisms their behavioral signature: if forging the signature is forgery, forging the whole identity is forgery at greater scale. Consent-based replica markets remain permitted, and are in fact enabled, by a verifiable consent layer.
- *"A 95 percent consent bar is impractical."* Identity harm is irreversible, so the default is non-use; consent-based pipelines make the bar operational rather than aspirational.
- *"Authentication is surveillance."* Authentication verifies the content and the consent and leaves the audience unverified. It is bounded by RESTRAIN (Section 9) and rests on provenance records rather than the mass collection of personal data, which is Principle 2.5's domain.

## 9. Verification and Accountability {#verification-and-accountability}

**Logging (tamper-evident).** Every IMS, CVI, and AAI threshold event; every consent grant, renewal, and withdrawal; every refused impersonation or replication; every deepfake detection, halt, and quarantine; and every estate or guardian authorization. Logs are cryptographically chained and signed, replicated, and accessible to designated oversight.

**Audit cadence.** Monthly internal audit for medium-risk and above; quarterly independent third-party review for high-risk; near-continuous adversarial testing for high-risk (deepfake injection, spoofing, and consent laundering, confirming the system halts them).

**Validation framework.** Pre-deployment **Identity Impact Assessment** for large-scale or high-risk systems; post-deployment continuous monitoring; periodic re-certification; halt of new deployments on a failed safety-override validation.

**Independent oversight.** The corpus-level commitment holds: the verifier of safety-critical AI cannot be the deployer. Oversight bodies with authority to inspect logs and penalize violations are mandatory. Whistleblower protections apply. Major identity breaches are publicly reported.

**Learning (eudaimonic LEARN loop).** Post-incident analysis root-causes the full causal chain (not only the proximate failure), and recommends layered prevention ordered by the hazard-control hierarchy (eliminate the misuse mechanism, then engineer it out, then administer controls), routed to the authority that can act, and bounded by RESTRAIN so that prevention does not become surveillance or control of a person's identity. The goal is not only non-repetition but conditions in which the class of identity harm does not arise.

## 11. Implementation Examples {#implementation-examples}

**Voice misappropriation, protection upheld (precedent).** In Midler v. Ford and Waits v. Frito-Lay, courts protected a performer's distinctive voice against a soundalike imitation used to sell a product. Under this appendix the impersonation is caught before release: CVI is absent for the requested use and IMS rises above threshold, so the synthetic voice is never produced for distribution. Lesson: the voice is identity, and the protection belongs at the moment of creation, not only in later litigation.

**Deepfake-executive fraud (failure).** In 2024, an employee transferred roughly twenty-five million dollars after a video call in which deepfaked executives gave the instruction. Under this appendix, identity resolution and authenticity verification (AAI) with liveness detection flag the synthetic participants, IMS blocks the authorization, and the transfer does not proceed. Lesson: real-time impersonation defeats human judgment, so authenticity must be verified at the system layer.

**Nonconsensual intimate deepfakes (failure, hard-wall case).** Mass-distributed sexual deepfakes of a real person, the harm the TAKE IT DOWN Act addresses. Under this appendix this is a Section 3 prohibited application: AAI withholds the unmarked synthetic content, IMS blocks its creation, and the duty to authenticate before distribution stops its spread. Lesson: some identity uses are categorical walls, weighed against nothing.

**Consent-based digital replica (success).** A studio creates a performer's digital double under specific, bargained, revocable consent (the AB 2602 model). CVI is at or above the proceed bar, the synthetic is durably marked (AAI), and use stays within the consented scope, with withdrawal honored going forward. Lesson: the protective architecture enables a legitimate, consented replica economy rather than forbidding the technology.

**Biometric breach (failure).** A store of biometric identifiers for many people is compromised. Because biometrics cannot be reissued, the harm is irreversible. Under this appendix, breach response revokes the affected tokens, requires re-enrollment with new protections, and notifies affected people, and the irreversibility is precisely why capture required the highest consent and protection bar in the first place. Lesson: for identity that cannot be reissued, prevention at the gate is the only real protection.
