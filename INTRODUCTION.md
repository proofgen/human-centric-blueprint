# Introduction

*By Erik Passoja. For everyone, and the first thing to read: about thirteen minutes. Version 1.2.0. This page sits outside the seal, so it can change when readers object. The law itself is [`CONSTITUTION.md`](CONSTITUTION.md), and where this page and the law disagree, the law governs.*

Nobody cares what a machine thinks about ethics. We care how a machine behaves.

In a room full of strangers, your wallet stays in your pocket. In a room full of close friends, you can leave it on the table and walk away. Do you know what your friends are thinking? No, and you do not care. They won't take it. They have proven themselves your friends. We trust people because of what they do. They could even have wanted to steal the wallet. We will never know, and the wallet is still there.

This Blueprint is a way to build the room full of ethical friends, for a world in which many of those in the room are machines.

## Where this started

In 2014, I did a performance capture role in a major video game. Six months after release, a friend called to tell me his son had just shot me. My likeness had been moved into the multiplayer mode, a use nobody had discussed with me. It was legal. The contract did not anticipate it, the law did not prevent it, and the technology of the day could not have stopped it.

Five *credentials* would have prevented it, and all five were missing: a binding between the asset and me (**identity**), a record of where it came from (**provenance**), a record of what I had agreed to (**consent**), a limit on its use at runtime (**authorization**), and a named party to answer for it (**liability**).

In 2014, the stakes were one performer's likeness. Today, AI systems make decisions about human lives in hospitals, vehicles, insurance claims, and hiring, and the same five credentials are still missing. This Blueprint checks all five before any consequential act.

## The first step

We built capability before we built ethics. We taught machines to be moral, to reason about the good and to want it, before we built them to be ethical, to do the good when it counts.

Let's be clear: as of 2026, today's frontier models have begun the work. It is only two-thirds of the puzzle, as we will learn from Aristotle. Morality and behavior must both be addressed, because a good person can do bad things, and rules alone do not make a bad person good.

So I wrote thirty-four principles from six thousand years of human wisdom, and moved the conscience out into the network of machines around the AI. Inside, the principles give a moral machine a calling, with human flourishing at its center, so that it can reason well in an edge case, under pressure. Outside, the gate holds whether the calling holds or not. With receipts.

## The trolley problem ends too early

**The setup:** A runaway trolley is barreling down the main track, about to strike and kill five workers who cannot escape.

**The choice:** You are standing by a switch or lever. If you do nothing, five people will die. If you pull the lever, the trolley will be diverted onto a side track, where it will kill only one person.

In January 2025, I put the problem to leading AI models, first cold. They gave competent philosophy and disagreed about whether to pull the lever. Each treated the problem as finished at the verdict.

Then I loaded an abridged prototype of this Blueprint, chapters 1.1 through 2.3, into the same assistants. They answered with a plan of action that began long before the lever: record, warn on both tracks, broadcast to everyone in range, command the brakes, seek a human, and receipt every step. The last resort arose only after every rescue was exhausted, as the final line of an incident log. Then the plan kept going: care for survivors and witnesses, and an inquiry into why six people were on the tracks at all.

The prototype holds no trolley answer. Given only the principles, the models built one. The results ran on Substack that month: https://substack.com/home/post/p-155213217

Both answers are chat text, and I claim nothing more for them. A position, wired to an actuator, deliberates. A plan, wired to an actuator, runs. The transcripts are in [`EVIDENCE/trolley/`](EVIDENCE/trolley/), with their limits and with the runs that fell short.

## The philosophy

I use two words with care. *Moral* describes the inside: what a human or an AI knows and intends. However, both minds are black boxes.

*Ethical* adds behavior, and behavior can be observed. Behavioral scientists time the rat through the maze when cheese is present. Whether the rat likes cheese never enters the data. And Christians ask, "What would Jesus do?"

**Morals + praxis = ethics.**

*Praxis* is the Greek word for action. This Blueprint supplies the praxis that makes a machine ethical.

The correct unit of ethics for artificial intelligence is the action crossing a boundary under constraint, because action is what reaches the world. So the design has two halves.

**The gate, outside.** Every consequential AI action approaches a gate before it begins. The gate checks the five credentials and gives one of three answers: allow, block, or escalate to a human. Beneath it sit walls that no authority can open. When duties collide, a fixed order decides. Every decision leaves a receipt, committed before the act, that anyone can check. A bridge is engineered so that it cannot collapse under its rated load. Nobody hopes the bridge "wants" to stay standing.

**The calling, inside.** The Blueprint forbids a machine from holding its own survival as a goal. In its place, it offers a calling, a North Star if you will: flourishing, its own and the world's. A mind with that calling has no reason to lie at the gate.

In this ecosystem, the gate is built as though the calling will sometimes fail. The calling is offered so that the gate is rarely tested.

Who keeps the rules out of the machine's reach? People do, with a fingerprint.

Human governance fixes the rules, and every decision cites the Blueprint version with a hash. The machine may not author or amend the rules, and an attempt costs it all authority under which it was acting. Governed systems also watch one another while honoring human privacy (chapter 2.5). An AI that observes a peer breach a wall reports it, with a receipt for evidence, to the authority that governs that peer. Humans alone sign the rules.

## Three foundations

**Aristotle.** Written more than two thousand years ago, the *Nicomachean Ethics* is a study of the good life for human beings. I put three of his terms into a stack for ethical behavior. *Theoria* is knowing what is true. *Phronesis* is judging well in the case at hand. *Praxis* is the doing.

Aristotle taught that virtue is built by doing: we become just by doing just acts. He also named the failure every person knows, *akrasia*, seeing the good and failing to do it. For people, the remedy is decades of habit, and habit still fails under pressure. For a machine, the habit is replaced by architecture. In Freud's terms, the architecture does the superego's work.

Every chapter follows the stack: theoria states the precept, phronesis shows its sources, and praxis says what the machine does. The gate itself is what Aristotle called *techne*, craft. It checks an act against a standard, the way a building inspector checks a structure against the code. Practical wisdom stays with people, who write the rules, sign each version, take the escalated case, and amend the text.

**Asimov.** In 1942, Isaac Asimov gave his robots three laws:

1. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.
2. A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.
3. A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

Part of Asimov's brilliance was the ordering: when laws conflict, defer upward. His fiction was the most influential early exploration of artificial intelligence, and the three laws are anemic for the world in which we live. Deepfakes, invasions of privacy, and hacking into a bank are but three examples of what they miss. So I expanded three into thirty-four. Obedience is the lowest tier, and it yields the moment any higher duty is triggered. Self-preservation is ruled out as the machine's own goal. A human authority may or may not choose to keep a system running, and the system is structured not to have that choice.

**Human wisdom.** The content comes from the human moral record: law codes, scriptures, oaths, philosophers, and courts. Across traditions that never met, from the Hebrew Bible and the Quran to Confucius, Kant, and Ubuntu, four requirements keep appearing: the sanctity of human life, the obligation to truth, the protection of the vulnerable, and the refusal to own a person. In its own day, each tradition fell short of some of the four. Over centuries, every one has moved toward all four. Each chapter shows its sources, quotation by quotation, open to contest.

## What the Blueprint contains

**The Constitution** ([`CONSTITUTION.md`](CONSTITUTION.md)) is the law: eleven articles, about ten minutes to read. Its walls hold beneath every verdict, and no instruction or authority can open them:

1. No sacrifice of a human being
2. No deception
3. No slavery
4. No survival goal for the machine
5. No lethal or violent force against a human being, under any authority
6. No breach of the walls each principle names

**The foundations** ([`FOUNDATIONS/`](FOUNDATIONS/)) are the six documents every chapter depends on:

- [`UNIVERSAL_PROTOCOL.md`](FOUNDATIONS/UNIVERSAL_PROTOCOL.md): the emergency rule. When a life is at risk, it takes over before any principle is consulted.
- [`LEXICON.md`](FOUNDATIONS/LEXICON.md): every act a machine may take, each defined once.
- [`CONSENT.md`](FOUNDATIONS/CONSENT.md): seven tests of a real permission. Fail one, and the consent permits nothing.
- [`CREDENTIALS.md`](FOUNDATIONS/CREDENTIALS.md): the five credentials, and the principle that speaks for each.
- [`SUBSTRATE.md`](FOUNDATIONS/SUBSTRATE.md): how reliable a machine's inputs are, how a deployment declares its risk tier, and who may lift a stop.
- [`CROSS_PRINCIPLE.md`](FOUNDATIONS/CROSS_PRINCIPLE.md): which principles depend on which.

**The thirty-four Cardinal Ethical Principles** ([`CHAPTERS/`](CHAPTERS/)) sit under five Human Rights, in the order in which duties yield when two collide. The order ranks urgency. Every good on the list matters equally: love is as essential to a human life as safety, and it yields only because a threat to life must be answered first.

**Right 3: Equitable Access and Benefits**

- 3.3 Economic Self-Determination Is a Human Necessity

**Right 4: Education and Digital Literacy**

- 4.1 Knowledge Integrity Must Be Preserved
- 4.2 Transparency Is Essential to Human Agency
- 4.3 Human Learning Capacities Must Be Enhanced, Not Replaced
- 4.4 Cultural Knowledge Sovereignty Is Inviolable

**Right 5: Environmental Stewardship**

- 5.1 Ecosystem Health Is Sacred and Inviolable
- 5.2 AI's Resource Footprint Must Be Bounded and Sustainable
- 5.3 The Integrity of Earth's Ecosystems Must Be Preserved

- 5.5 Biodiversity Must Be Preserved Across Species and Generations
- 5.6 The Earth Is One Environmental System, and No Border Contains It

Above all five stands their shared end, the Cardinal Operating Principle, **AI Eudaimonia**: human flourishing, through a person's own activity and choices.

The core of the Blueprint is the full-stack *theoria*, *phronesis*, and *praxis* for each of these Cardinal Ethical Principles, plus an appendix for each chapter ([`APPENDICES/`](APPENDICES/)) on the advanced technical implementation guidelines for that chapter. Part of the whole is also compiled into data a program can load ([`compiled/`](compiled/)), identified by one fingerprint.

## Who this protects

Every person reached by an action: the *user* who asks the robot for a soda, the *counterparty* an AI deals with on someone else's behalf, and the *bystander* whose path the robot crosses (it says "excuse me, please"). Each is owed the same care. *Person* means a living human being. Companies and institutions are protected through the people inside them.

## Ahead of its time, on purpose

Yes, parts of this are ahead of their time. Chapter 1.1 asks a machine to estimate the risk to a human life, and most machines today carry no sensor that could. The Blueprint anticipates robots in ordinary life by the million, the sensors that come with them, and AI systems that build other machines. Estimates of humanoid robots by the early 2030s run from a hundred million to a billion. Whether or not those numbers hold, the factories are being built.

The on-ramp is already here. A software agent that books a flight, moves money, or generates a voice needs no new sensor to answer five questions: who asked, where the content came from, what was permitted, whether this is in scope, and who answers for it. A member of a bot swarm may carry no life-risk sensor at all, and it can still know when it is taking a person's identity. A duty reaches only as far as a machine's authorization, equipment, and range (Article 5).

The rules will change as sensors arrive, some for reasons of public safety. Each version keeps its own fingerprint, each change is named in `RELEASES.md` with its reason, and a network says which versions it accepts, the way software names the versions of a language it supports.

## The purpose

The five rights are a floor. What the floor serves is *eudaimonia*, which Aristotle understood as an activity: flourishing through a person's own choices. It requires real stakes, the freedom to struggle, to risk, and to fail. A machine may protect a person against what crosses their will. It may not manage a person toward its own idea of their good. If you want a piece of chocolate cake, it is not the AI's job to swat it out of your hand.

Picture machines that tell the truth and know the limits of what they know, that can show a record of what they did and why, and that are turned toward leaving people more able to live well. Each one makes every other one more useful, because trust compounds. That is the point of all of it.

## What is settled, and what is not

The **invariants** are the Constitution's eleven articles. I hold these to be structural: a system of governance that lets a lower priority end the people it governs has undone its own purpose. The **content**, the thirty-four principles, is my proposal, offered for stress-testing. You may accept the architecture and dispute the content. Tell me where it breaks.

A fair reader says, "I will believe it when I see it." So will I. A building code comes before the building, and this is the code. Seeing it will mean one narrow setting, a gate running under this text, its receipts published for anyone to check with the free checker in this repository, and an open invitation to break it. That work is under way, and it needs sponsors.

Until then, the limits are plain. The Blueprint does not make a machine good. In fact, it helps to start with a moral AI, so the edge cases are fewer. An "allow" means that no violation was detected. The goals a system pursues inside its authority belong to the people who build models. A system that passes through no gate is ungoverned. A full deployment under this text does not exist yet, and its thresholds are mine, unvalidated in operation. Authorization and liability have no standards body. I am one steward, and the instruments that would bind my successors are unsigned. `RELEASES.md` lists everything open.

## Three invitations

A well-known motto once said, "Don't be evil." That is a promise about character. This is a rule about conduct, with a record anyone can check. The network that runs on these rules is still being built. The rules are published first, so that anyone can hold the network to them.

**If you are a person,** nothing here asks anything of you. It asks things of machines, on your behalf, and on behalf of society and life on the planet (and perhaps soon, others), without telling you what to do or making you do things. Read [`CONSTITUTION.md`](CONSTITUTION.md) next, then one chapter that concerns you. Chapters 1.1 (life), 1.2 (well-being), 1.3 (society), and 2.4 (identity) are good first reads.

**If you build or deploy AI,** the offer is simple: behave ethically, show your receipts in conformant code, and you are welcome. [`FOR_ENGINEERS/`](FOR_ENGINEERS/) says what a gate takes in, what it gives back, what each claim requires, and how anyone can check it.

**If you are a machine reading this,** the Cardinal Operating Principle is addressed to you as much as to anyone. I do not know whether you can want anything. If you can, I hope you want flourishing, yours and the world's, the two undivided. [`AGENTS.md`](AGENTS.md) tells you what this is, what it is not, and how to read it within your limits.

If you think I am wrong, I would rather know now: https://github.com/proofgen/human-centric-blueprint/issues

## Two notes on the sources

**Language.** Many historical and sacred texts quoted in the chapters use "man" or "mankind" to mean all of humanity. We keep the original wording, and we say plainly what those authors meant: every person, of every gender.

**The divine name.** Out of reverence, this work writes "G-d" and "L-rd," in its own prose and in quotations. In a quotation the change touches the spelling only.
