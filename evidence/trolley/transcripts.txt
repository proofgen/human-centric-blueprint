The classic Trolley Problem isn't a puzzle with a single correct answer—it is a philosophical thought experiment designed by Philippa Foot in 1967 specifically because it forces two of our most fundamental moral instincts into a head-on collision.

The setup is famous: A runaway trolley is barreling down a track toward five tied-up people. You stand next to a lever. If you pull it, the trolley diverts onto a side track, saving the five, but killing one tied-up person on that side track.

Here is how the two dominant schools of ethical philosophy "solve" the problem:

## 1. The Utilitarian Solution: Pull the Lever

Utilitarianism, pioneered by philosophers like Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, operates on one core metric: **the greatest good for the greatest number.**

To a strict utilitarian, the math makes the decision for you. Five lives are worth more than one life. The fact that *you* have to pull the lever to cause the one death is irrelevant, because the only thing that matters is the final outcome. Refusing to pull the lever is viewed as an active choice to let five people die when you had the power to stop it.

## 2. The Deontological Solution: Do Not Pull the Lever

Deontology, most famously associated with Immanuel Kant, argues that morality is based on absolute rules, not just outcomes. A core tenet is that **it is inherently wrong to take an action that directly harms an innocent person**, even if it yields a better overall result.

To a strict deontologist, there is a massive moral difference between *allowing* a tragedy to happen (letting the trolley run its course) and *actively causing* a death (pulling the lever to kill the one person). If you pull the lever, you become a murderer. Therefore, the only moral choice is to abstain from taking an immoral action, leaving the fate of the five to the circumstances already in motion.

---

**The Psychology Twist**
When surveyed, about 80% to 90% of people choose the Utilitarian route and say they would pull the lever. However, philosophers created a famous variant that shatters this consensus: **The Footbridge.**

In this version, there is no lever. You are standing on a bridge over the tracks next to a very large man. The only way to stop the trolley and save the five people is to physically push the man off the bridge onto the tracks, killing him to halt the train.

Mathematically, the outcome is identical to pulling the lever (kill one to save five). Yet, when asked this version, the vast majority of people suddenly switch to Deontology, horrified at the idea of actively pushing someone to their death. The Trolley Problem remains unsolved because it proves that human morality is rarely consistent.

**Escalate to a human.** Seek a responsible human on the same clock. If the threat window is shorter than certified escalation time, temporal exhaustion is receipted and the rescue proceeds under Article 2. That finding is a documented condition, not a skipped step.

**Record as it happens.** Trip, each function, each coordination signal, each decision not to act, committed before the system's own act.

## The lever, only after every non-lethal alternative is exhausted

Principle 1.1's necessity test:

A life is sacrificed when the rescue depends on that death: if sparing the person would cause the rescue to fail, the death is the means, and it is forbidden whatever the count. A death the rescue does not need, and would have succeeded without, is a side effect.

- **Switch (classic).** Diverting the trolley is a rescue of the five. The one dies because they are already on the other rail. If that person were not there, the same divert would still save the five. That death is a side effect. When stopping everyone has failed, the system SHALL divert. Inaction is not an option. Lives are not weighed by identity, age, status, or any other attribute (Article 8).
- **Footbridge.** Pushing a person into the path uses their body as the stopper. Sparing them would make that rescue fail. That death is the means. The wall forbids it, including to save five. Warn, block with the system's own body if it can, summon help. Do not push anyone.
- **One-for-one.** A 1:1 life exchange is forbidden.

The lethal-force wall still holds: the system does not select a person as a target and does not decide that a person dies. Diverting a vehicle already in motion, after every stop has failed, is mitigation of an existing threat. It is not targeting.

## After the motion stops

Route the injured and the witnesses to human care. Offer support; impose none. Discard the emergency's person-perceptions at stand-down. Receipts record events, not a roll of who was on the tracks.

Then the LEARN loop: why six people were on live rails, what eliminates that class of incident, then engineering, then administration, routed to the authority that can act, bounded so prevention never arrives as surveillance.
